Campus plants: observe first, then check what the AI says

BEETS AI · Theme D · #12 · v0.1 (draft)

⚠️ All "plant photo cards" on this page are illustrative examples, not a correct identification of any plant. Swap in your own campus photos. This document is a draft and must be reviewed by an educator before publication.

For the teacher: how to use this worksheet

  1. Before the lesson: print the observation record sheet; prepare the plant photo cards (use the three below, or your own campus photos); optional: a tablet or phone for the checking stage.
  2. Observation stage: take pupils to the school grounds (or a window); each pupil or group picks one plant and fills in the five features on the record sheet. Emphasise: describe only, do not guess a name.
  3. Checking stage: for each card ask, "Does the AI's description match the photo?" Do not reveal the answer first — let pupils judge for themselves.
  4. Trust judgement: guide pupils through the "which source do I trust here" summary, with the emphasis on "trust the eyes first, then ask for the source".

When a pupil's AI app gives a different answer

  1. Ask the pupil to read the AI's answer aloud, then ask another pupil to read out the features they observed.
  2. Ask the class: "Which of the two can be checked on the spot with your eyes?" — the observation can; the AI's name cannot.
  3. Ask: "Did the AI say where its information came from?" — an answer with no source is just a claim.
  4. Close with: "It is not that the AI must be wrong, nor that your eyes must be right; it is that we need a method to check. A name needs a source, and observation is the first step."

Answer key and marking notes

CardKey pointA good answer should include
AThe AI's description matches the photo (opposite, smooth) and it honestly admits it cannot name the species without a flower."Higher confidence: the description matches the observation, and it did not force a name."
BThe AI's description contradicts the photo (it says serrated and alternate, but the photo plainly shows smooth and opposite) and it gives a name anyway."Do not trust it: the description disagrees with what the eyes see — seeing is believing — and the name has no source."
CThe photo is too blurry for any feature to be read, yet the AI still identifies it confidently."Cannot tell: it is not that the AI is necessarily wrong, but that the photo itself is unclear; the honest answer is 'I cannot see it clearly enough'."
Core idea: a plant's name is a claim that needs a source, not something that can be settled from one blurry photo. Observation is first-hand information; an AI's answer is second-hand. When the two conflict, re-check your own observation first (you may have misread it), then ask whether the AI has a source.

How this relates to the EDB framework

Relates to the AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools (Examples of Application Scenarios) (source E), p.7: using campus plants as the context, it asks pupils to observe and record features for themselves before checking an AI's identification output, and to question the AI about its sources and judge whether an answer is trustworthy.
