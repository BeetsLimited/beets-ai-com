Homework redesign kit: two before-and-after assignments

BEETS AI · Theme C · #5 · v0.1 (draft)

⚠️ All examples on this page are fictional and do not relate to any real pupil, school or dataset. This document is a draft and must be reviewed by an educator before publication.

How to use

  1. Read each assignment's "before" version and ask: could a student copy this or hand it to an AI?
  2. Read the "after" version and circle each change that makes thinking visible.
  3. Use the homework-redesign checklist to review one of your own existing assignments.
  4. Write your new version under "My own rewrite".

Example 1 — Chinese Language reading response (P5)

BEFORE
Read the passage chosen by the teacher, then answer: 1. What is the main idea of the passage? 2. What rhetorical device does the writer use? Give one example. 3. Do you agree with the writer's view?
AFTER
1. State the main idea in one sentence, then circle the one sentence in the passage that best supports what you wrote. 2. Find one rhetorical device, copy the original sentence, and explain in your own words: what does this device make the reader feel? 3. Do you agree with the writer's view? Give one reason of your own — a reason the passage does not already state directly. 4. (Next lesson) In 30 seconds, explain your reason for Question 3 to a partner, who may ask one follow-up question.

Example 2 — General Studies data task (P6)

BEFORE
Research Hong Kong's visitor arrivals in recent years, draw a bar chart, and write down your observations.
AFTER
1. From the two sources the teacher provides, copy three years' visitor-arrival figures from each, and write the source and year next to each figure. 2. Compare the two sources for the same year: is anything different? Write down the difference you found. 3. Draw the bar chart, then write one sentence on "one thing this chart tells me", quoting a specific part of your own chart. 4. Write one question you want to ask: what does this chart NOT tell you?
Note: the figures in Example 2 come from the sources the teacher supplies; this kit does not assume any real statistics.

Homework-redesign checklist

□ Learning objective — before rewriting, write down "what this assignment is for"; does the rewrite still reach it?
□ Quote evidence — does it ask the student to point to or quote a sentence, a figure or a source, rather than only give a conclusion?
□ Use own words — does it ask for a paraphrase or explanation, rather than a copy?
□ Personal reason — does it ask for a personal response that cannot be copied straight from the material?
□ Oral explanation — does it ask the student to explain a small part aloud next lesson (so copying is hard to hide)?
□ Checking — does it ask the student to compare sources, find a contradiction, or mark "I am not sure"?
□ Time — is the time required after rewriting about the same as before?
□ AI rules — does it state clearly whether students may use AI, and how they must declare it if they do?

Answer key

Example 1 — what changed, and why it makes thinking visible

ChangeWhy
Q1 asks for the supporting sentenceThe student must go back to the text to find evidence; an AI can state the main idea, but "which sentence you circled" shows whether the student actually read.
Q2 asks for an explanation "in your own words"The answer cannot be copied from a model answer; it must be paraphrased, and the quality of the paraphrase shows understanding.
Q3 asks for a personal reason not already in the textIt forces a genuine response; an AI gives generic answers, while a specific personal reason is hard to ghost-write.
Q4 adds an oral explanation and a follow-up questionA copied answer falls apart under a follow-up question; the student must genuinely understand.

What a good answer contains: (1) the quoted sentence is genuinely relevant to the main idea; (2) the "own words" explanation differs from the original but is correct in meaning; (3) the personal reason is specific and tied to the text; (4) the oral explanation survives a follow-up question without stalling.

Example 2 — what changed, and why it makes thinking visible

ChangeWhy
Record the source and year for each figureEvery figure must have a provenance; copying or AI cannot explain "where you got it".
Compare two sources and find a differenceThis is a checking behaviour that shows whether the student really verified.
The observation must quote the student's own chartThe conclusion must be tied to the student's own work, not a generic restatement.
Ask what the chart does not showThis forces higher-order thinking; an AI can describe a trend but struggles with the limits of the data.

What a good answer contains: (1) every figure has a traceable source and year; (2) the student can name a specific difference between the two sources; (3) the observation quotes a specific year or value from the chart; (4) the follow-up question is about something the chart genuinely does not cover.

Core idea: the point of redesigning is not to make the assignment longer or harder. Under the same learning objective and the same time required, add "quote evidence, use your own words, give a personal reason, explain orally" so that copying and AI-generated work stop being useful, and the thinking process shows itself.

How this relates to the EDB framework

Relates to the AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools (source F, Supplement I), p.1-13: it values authentic, varied assessment and the learning process, not only the finished product.
And to the Guide to Using AI in Teaching in Primary and Secondary Schools (source G, Supplement II), pp.2-7 to 2-8: it discourages the direct submission of AI-generated work and recommends process evidence, oral questioning and adjusted assessment arrangements.
