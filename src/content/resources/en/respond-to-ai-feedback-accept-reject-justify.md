> A tool's comment is neither a verdict nor an instruction that has to be followed. What the pupil decides is whether to accept it, reject it, or handle the point another way — and the grounds for that decision go on the same sheet.

## What this solves

Comments on English writing have always been the kind of text most easily mistaken for an instruction. A generated line — "this paragraph could be more specific" — reads like a teacher's remark, so the pupil revises, and once the revision is done the whole business of feedback is treated as finished. The difficulty is that the sentence revised may have been the strongest in the piece, while the sentence that really needed work, because no comment mentioned it, stays exactly as it was.

The other familiar scene: a pupil rejects a comment and cannot say why, so it reads as unwillingness to revise. These are two faces of one problem — the response was never treated as an act of judgement, only as obedience or disobedience.

This sheet splits the response into three: accept and revise, reject and justify, handle it another way. For each comment the pupil chooses one and writes it in the "grounds or change" column. Rejecting is not a matter of taste: it has to clear three thresholds, and if none of them holds, the comment is accepted or handled another way. Three comments a lesson, two of them fixed by rule: one that would change what the pupil does, and one where they cannot tell whether it is right. The first practises judging whether a change is worth making; the second is where verification is practised — uncertainty is precisely the place to check.

With a tool in the room the sheet blocks one more thing: it never asks for the tool's comment to be handed in. The pupil writes, in their own words, what the comment is about, so what stays on the sheet is their reading and their decision rather than the tool's output.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, pupils can:

1. Name the three responses and choose one for each comment.
2. Say what a comment is about and place it in one of four kinds.
3. Complete a response across four columns: the comment in one line, what it is about, my response, grounds or change.
4. Check the three thresholds before rejecting, and name which one holds.
5. Say what to do first when they cannot tell whether a comment is right, and where to verify it.

The page addresses the strands **human-AI communication and collaboration** and **reflection on the use of AI**: the comment comes from a tool, the response is the pupil's, and the response has to carry its grounds. Three values are practised. **Critical thinking** is working out what the comment is about before deciding whether to change anything. **Responsibility** is putting grounds on both the changes and the refusals instead of leaving the judgement to the tool. **Timely and appropriate use** is knowing which one comment deserves the time and which can be left.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson, in a fixed order: the three responses, the four kinds, the four columns, choosing three comments, the three thresholds, then writing and reading back. There are three responses and four kinds; none is added or removed, and neither are the three thresholds.

### 1. Three responses (fixed)

| Response | When it is the right one | What goes on the sheet |
|---|---|---|
| **Accept and revise** | The comment names a real problem, and the change makes the writing more accurate or clearer | What the change is (one line) |
| **Reject and justify** | The comment conflicts with the facts, with the task, or with accuracy | Which threshold holds (one line) |
| **Handle it another way** | The problem is real, but the change the comment suggests is not the best way to deal with it | What you will do instead (one line) |

- **All three have to be available.** Three acceptances in a row usually means nothing was judged; three rejections in a row usually means the comments were not read.
- **"Handle it another way" is not a postponement.** It has to name another move, concretely — replacing a generalisation with an example, for instance.

### 2. Four kinds: what the comment is about (fixed)

| Kind | What the comment is usually about | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|---|
| Fact and information | Whether a date, a name, a figure or a tense in the piece is right | "The year you give here does not match your source." |
| Structure and organisation | Paragraph order, topic sentences, links between parts | "The topic sentence of paragraph two does not match the example that follows." |
| Language and wording | Whether a word is accurate, a tone fits, a sentence reads | "The tone of this sentence is suddenly much harder than the rest." |
| Evidence and sources | Whether the sentence says where the claim comes from and who says so | "This claim gives no source." |

- **One kind per comment — the main one.** The point of sorting is to know which layer has to change: information, structure, wording, or sourcing.
- **If it will not sort, read the comment again.** A comment you cannot understand is usually not worth following, and that is exactly the case the "cannot tell whether it is right" comment is there to handle.

### 3. The four-column sheet (fixed)

| Column | What goes in it | What must not |
|---|---|---|
| The comment in one line | In your own words, what this comment is about | The tool's wording, a screenshot, a transcript |
| What it is about | One of the four kinds | Two kinds side by side |
| My response | One of the three responses | "Look again later", "undecided" |
| Grounds or change | One line: what changed, or which threshold holds, or what you will do instead | Praise for yourself or scorn for the tool |

- **The first column is in your own words.** This is the most important rule on the sheet: copying the wording turns the page into a transcript, while writing it yourself shows which point you understood.
- **The fourth column gets one line.** Grounds that fit on one line are grounds you actually hold.

### 4. Three comments a lesson, the first two fixed (fixed)

From the comments the tool made on your draft, choose three. The first two are fixed by rule:

1. **One that would change what you do.** Reading it changes how you write next time, not only this sentence.
2. **One where you cannot tell whether it is right.** It may be right or wrong — this one is verified before it is accepted or rejected.

The third is yours: the one you most want to reject, or the one you understand least.

- **Three only.** Three comments answered properly beat ten marked "read"; the lesson has room for three.
- **Choose before writing.** The choice is itself a judgement, and the comments a class picks are usually the hardest points in the task.
- **Uncertain is not the same as rejecting.** For the uncertain one, verify first, then record in the third column what you checked.

### 5. Rejecting has to clear three thresholds (fixed)

"Reject and justify" is not a feeling. It is three thresholds that can be checked one at a time. If any one holds, the comment may be rejected; if none holds, it is accepted or handled another way.

| Threshold | When it holds | How to write it (one line) |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Contrary to fact | The comment says something in the piece is wrong, and you have verified that the piece is right | "I checked the source; the year in my draft is correct." |
| 2. Contrary to the task | The comment asks for a different form, audience or length from the one the task set | "The task asks for a letter to a parent; this tone fits that reader." |
| 3. Following it would make the content less accurate | After the change the sentence would be less accurate or vaguer than before | "If I follow it, the sentence becomes a claim I cannot support." |

- **All three have to be writable.** "I think mine sounds better" is not a threshold; "I checked the source and my draft is right" is.
- **If no threshold holds, accept.** A rejection that clears nothing gets rewritten — that is how the sheet works, not a penalty.

### 6. How much for S1-S3 and S4-S6

| Stage | How many comments | Which responses | Columns |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1-S3 | Two | Choose between accept and revise, or reject and justify | All four columns, with half a line acceptable in the fourth |
| S4-S6 | Three | All three, including handling it another way | Four columns, one line each |

- **S1-S3 do not use "handle it another way".** Two options are enough to practise judging and then stating grounds; the third is held for S4-S6.
- **What is reduced is the amount, not the rule.** The three thresholds still apply in the junior forms; they are usually read with the teacher.

### 7. The 60-minute lesson

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 8 min | The three responses: read the table and sort two examples together |
| 7 min | The four kinds: sort four example comments and say why |
| 8 min | Choose three comments, starting with the one that would change what you do and the one you cannot judge |
| 20 min | Write all four columns, one row per comment, one line in the fourth |
| 10 min | Read with a partner, asking only: which threshold does this rejection clear? |
| 7 min | Close: the teacher reads out two or three strong fourth columns and the class names the threshold each one clears |

### Terms used on this page

Six words carry the work here, each in its ordinary sense. Naming them keeps the sheet readable alongside the other resources in this library.

| Term | What it means on this sheet |
|---|---|
| feedback | What the tool or the teacher says about the draft — recorded as a kind and a response, never copied out |
| questioning | The pupil's own questions about a comment before deciding |
| reflection | The fourth column: what changed, or which threshold holds, and why |
| verify | Checking a claim in the comment against the draft, the task or a source before rejecting |
| over-reliance | Accepting a comment because it reads confidently, without judging it |
| timely and appropriate | Choosing which comment deserves the time this lesson |

## Answer key and marking notes

**Examples (all fictional, showing the format only)**

| The comment in one line (the pupil's words) | What it is about | My response | Grounds or change |
|---|---|---|---|
| The tool says my second paragraph does not match its topic sentence | Structure and organisation | Accept and revise | I changed the topic sentence to "schools can start small", which the example now matches |
| The tool says the year is wrong | Fact and information | Reject and justify | I checked page 40 of the textbook; the year in my draft is correct |
| The tool says the tone is too hard | Language and wording | Handle it another way | I kept the judgement but added the source that supports it, so the tone is carried |
| The tool says the paragraph gives no source | Evidence and sources | Accept and revise | I added the source and the year on the same line |

**A strong fourth column:** "I changed 'everyone agrees' to 'two of the three questionnaires said so', because the original claimed a level of support I could not verify." It says what changed and what made the change necessary.

**Common errors and responses:**

- **Three acceptances.** Response: accepting is also a judgement and needs grounds. If all three deserved changing, check whether the uncertain one was chosen at all.
- **A rejection whose fourth column says "I think mine is better".** Response: that is not a threshold. Name one of the three: contrary to fact, contrary to the task, or following it would make the content less accurate.
- **The first column copies the tool's whole sentence.** Response: write it in your own words. The sheet keeps no tool wording, and no screenshot or record is handed in.
- **Two or three lines in the fourth column.** Response: keep one — the line with the strongest grounds.

**What counts as done:** two comments at S1-S3 and three at S4-S6, four columns filled for each, and a fourth column that names grounds someone else could check. The page does not score, rank or pass judgement on a pupil's ability.
