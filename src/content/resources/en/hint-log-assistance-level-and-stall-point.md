> **In this page, a hint means help given by a teacher, a classmate or a tool — not words typed into a tool.** What goes on the record sheet is which level of help was taken and which kind of stall it was, never the wording of the hint itself.

## What this solves

The hardest thing to see in a mathematics exercise is *where* a pupil stopped. A pupil puts a hand up, and whoever answers — teacher, classmate or the school-approved tool — usually gives everything at once: the whole method, the first step, sometimes the result. The pupil copies it out, the page is right, and where the thinking broke is recorded nowhere. The same break happens again on the next question of that kind.

The other familiar scene: the teacher asks which part is unclear and gets "all of it". That is not refusal; there is no shared name for the place where the pupil stopped. A difficulty that could be described precisely becomes a sentence that describes nothing.

This record sheet names the two things separately. **Help** has five levels, from "not needed" to "cross-check"; **stalls** have four kinds: reading the question, choosing a method, computing, checking. Each time a pupil takes help, they mark a level and a kind, then write one line: what I filled in myself. Three to five questions a lesson gives the teacher a record that points at a position: is the pattern that most of the class stalls at choosing a method, or that one or two stall at checking? The count is of questions, not of names.

With a tool in the room the sheet does one more thing: it separates asking from thinking. The tool may supply a hint, but the pupil still has to write what they filled in themselves — so human-ai collaboration becomes something written down rather than a single press of a button, and over-reliance leaves a trace that can be verified.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, pupils can:

1. Name the five levels of help and mark the level they actually took.
2. Name the four kinds of stall and say which kind they hit this time.
3. Write one line after each hint — what I filled in myself — describing their own move, not the tool's words.
4. Write one line at the end of the lesson: what I will do first on the next question.
5. Read the class count of questions and name the one kind of stall they will watch for next lesson.

The page addresses the strands **human-AI communication and collaboration** and **reflection on the use of AI**: help can come from a person or from a tool, and the pupil has to say which level they took. Three values are practised. **Critical thinking** is naming the kind of stall before deciding whether to ask. **Responsibility** is writing down what you filled in yourself instead of handing the finishing to a tool. **Timely and appropriate use** is knowing which level of help is already enough.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson, in a fixed order: the five levels, the four kinds, three to five questions, one line after each hint, one line at the end, then the class count. There are five levels and four kinds; no level or kind is added, removed or renamed.

### 1. Five levels of help (fixed)

The names of L1 to L4 are word-for-word the same as on the existing teacher-controlled hint cards page; this page adds L0, "not needed". No fifth level is invented and no level is renamed.

| Level | Name | What this level may give | What it may not contain |
|---|---|---|---|
| **L0** | Not needed | The pupil finished unaided and took no help at all | — |
| **L1** | Re-read the question | Restating what the question asks; naming the quantity to be found | Any method, any value |
| **L2** | Strategy direction | Naming a method, theorem or diagram worth considering | Substitution, any algebraic step, any value |
| **L3** | First step | Only the first algebraic or logical step | Later steps, the final value |
| **L4** | Cross-check | Comparing two workable approaches and saying which is safer, and why | Working out the result |

Two rules go with the table:

- **L0 is a record, not a blank.** A question that needed no help is still recorded; otherwise the count only shows the questions that went wrong and hides what the class could already do.
- **Marking a level too high is not the error; hiding it is.** If L3 was taken, mark L3. Writing L3 as L2 builds the next teaching decision on wrong data.

### 2. Four kinds of stall (fixed)

| Kind | What it usually looks like | One question to ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Reading the question | The question has been read but the quantity to be found cannot be named | "What is this question asking me to find?" |
| Choosing a method | Understood, but which method, theorem or diagram to use is unclear | "What kind of question is this, and what usually works?" |
| Computing | The method is right; the algebra or arithmetic goes wrong | "Is the value at this step sensible?" |
| Checking | The answer is worked out, and there is no second way to verify it | "Is there another way to verify this result?" |

- **One kind per question — the first place it stopped.** A stall moves: past the method, a pupil may stall again at the computing. Record the first break.
- **The four kinds are classroom vocabulary, not a judgement.** They describe the question and the steps, not the pupil's ability.

### 3. Three to five questions a lesson (fixed)

Three to five questions, no more and no fewer.

- **Three questions recorded properly beat ten ticks.** The lesson only has room for three to five; take them from the exercise the teacher has set.
- **They can be three of one kind or three different kinds.** Three of one kind shows whether the same stall repeats; three different kinds show which kind is most common.
- **Nothing but the question number, the level, the kind and two lines goes on the sheet.** No tool output, no screenshots.

### 4. After every hint, one line: what I filled in myself

Each time help is taken, write one line under that question describing the step you did yourself.

- **Write your own move.** For example: "I circled the quantity the question asks for" or "I chose the cosine rule because two sides and the included angle are given."
- **Do not copy the tool's or a classmate's wording.** The sheet keeps your action, not someone else's sentence.
- **If you cannot write it, mark one level higher.** Recording that L3 did not unblock you is the useful outcome — that is the position to work on next.

### 5. At the end, one line: what I will do first on the next question

At the end of the lesson write one line at the foot of the sheet, naming a concrete first move rather than a resolution.

- **Concrete enough to do.** "On the next question I will write the quantity to be found beside the question number" is a move; "I will be more careful" is not.
- **One line only.** A line that fits on one line is the one that gets used next lesson.

### 6. The class count: questions only

The teacher collects the sheets and writes only question counts on the board — no names, no ranking, no comparison with another class.

| What is counted | How it is written |
|---|---|
| Questions by level | How many questions stopped at L0, L1, L2, L3, L4 |
| Questions by kind | How many questions were reading, method, computing, checking |
| The most common pairing | For example, "L2 with choosing a method: six questions" |

- **The unit is the question, not the person.** Counting people turns the board into a ranking; counting questions says which part of this exercise is hard.
- **Counts only, no comparisons.** Not "better than last time", and not against another class. The comparison that matters is which kind of stall is most common in this class on this exercise.
- **The count is for the next lesson, not a record of pupils.**

### 7. The 60-minute lesson

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 8 min | The five levels: read the table, mark two worked examples together |
| 7 min | The four kinds: read the table, sort four examples |
| 25 min | Work three to five questions, marking level and kind on each hint and writing what I filled in myself |
| 8 min | Read the two lines with a neighbour, checking only whether they are concrete; no names read aloud |
| 7 min | Write the closing line: what I will do first on the next question |
| 5 min | The teacher writes the class count and names the most common kind |

### Terms used on this page

Six words carry the work here, and each is used in its ordinary sense. Naming them keeps the sheet readable alongside the other resources in this library.

| Term | What it means on this sheet |
|---|---|
| feedback | What the teacher, a classmate or the tool says about the work — recorded as a level and a kind, never copied out |
| questioning | The pupil's own ask, and the teacher's, at the moment of a stall |
| reflection | The two written lines at the end of each question and at the foot of the sheet |
| verify | Checking a result by a second route rather than re-reading the first |
| over-reliance | Taking help at a level above what the stall actually needed, or taking it before reading |
| human-ai collaboration | The tool supplies a level of help; the pupil records what they filled in themselves |

## Answer key and marking notes

**Examples (all fictional, showing the format only)**

| Question | Level marked | Kind | What I filled in myself | What I will do first next time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quadratic: find the maximum value | L1 | Reading the question | I circled "maximum value" and wrote that it is the y-coordinate of the vertex | Write the quantity to be found beside the question number |
| Sequence: find the 20th term | L2 | Choosing a method | I decided it was arithmetic because the difference between neighbouring terms is constant | Write the difference between two terms and look for the pattern |
| Statistics graph: compare two sets of data | L3 | Computing | I computed the difference and checked it by reading the graph a second way | Verify with a second method as soon as I finish computing |
| Probability: two balls drawn | L0 | — | I started from the definition and took no help | Keep starting from the definition |

**A good line for "what I filled in myself":** "I wrote down the quantity the question asks for, and only then saw that I had been hunting the wrong thing." It names a move and what the move changed.

**Common errors and responses:**

- **Everything marked L0.** Response: check against the moments you were stuck. If nothing was needed, L0 is the honest record — the teacher is reading the distribution of questions, not ranking independence.
- **A level is marked but the line says "I understand now".** Response: understanding is not a step. Write what you did — which quantity you circled, which method you chose.
- **Three kinds marked on one question.** Response: mark only the first place it stopped. A later stall can go in a note, but it stays out of the count.
- **A tool's sentence copied onto the sheet.** Response: the sheet carries your own move. Tool wording stays off it, and no screenshot or record of the conversation is handed in.

**What counts as done:** three to five questions each carry a level and a kind, one line of what I filled in myself, and one closing line on what to do first next time. The page does not score, rank or compare pupils or classes.

Every resource in this library is written within the AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools and sits inside the policy scope of the Blueprint for Digital Education Development in Primary and Secondary Schools; the teaching choices are the teacher's, following the Guide to Using AI in Teaching in Primary and Secondary Schools.

This resource maps to:

- **Blueprint for Digital Education Development in Primary and Secondary Schools** (Executive Summary, "Positioning and Principles", item 5, "Forward-looking innovation"): schools are asked to explore new technologies in teaching and assessment while holding to proper values and ethics. Recording how much help was taken is one way of doing that. This page names the item and its sense; it does not quote the text.
- **AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools**: sets out the learning strands. This page corresponds to "human-AI communication and collaboration" and "reflection on the use of AI". The page names them only; it quotes nothing from the framework.
- **EDB Circular Memorandum No. 156/2026** (Annex II, IV. Key Messages, 4) Student Chapter, "Step-by-step Application of Technology"): warns against premature advancement that causes over-reliance, and asks for a stage-by-stage approach. Splitting help into levels and stalls into kinds is one classroom way of meeting that. **The item is written in terms of lower and upper primary; for S1-S6 this page borrows only the principle of avoiding over-reliance, and does not present the item as a requirement for secondary schools.** The page does not quote the item.
- **Mathematics Curriculum and Assessment Guide (Secondary 4-6)** (Mathematics Education Key Learning Area; updated November 2015; section 4.2, "Guiding Principles"), three passages quoted verbatim below. **All three cover Secondary 4 to 6 only**:

  - **"Quality interaction"**: "The effective use of questioning and feedback guides students in the learning process. Students also contribute to each other's learning when they interact in different groupings to give mutual feedback. Teachers may use "scaffolds" to support them in areas that may be beyond their current reach."
    — L1 to L4 are four positions for that scaffold: each level gives only the step the pupil cannot yet manage alone.
  - **"Feedback and assessment"**: "Teachers need to assess the work of students, but assessment has little value without feedback. Feedback should not be confined to giving a grade, but must also provide students with a wide range of information on strengths and areas where they can improve their learning. Evidence from assessment should also give teachers indications as to where they need to improve their teaching."
    — This sheet records the kind of stall, not a grade; what the teacher gets is information for the next lesson.
  - **"Self-directed learning"**: "Students should be encouraged to take responsibility for their own learning. Open-ended questions can be used to promote their interest in learning mathematics. Opportunities should be provided to enable students to plan their own learning and to use mathematics to solve real-life problems."
    — "What I filled in myself" and "what I will do first next time" are the two smallest moves that hand that responsibility back to the pupil.
