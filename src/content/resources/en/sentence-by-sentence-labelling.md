> Labelling sentence by sentence is not an investigation; it is how a pupil can say why a sentence is theirs. Three to five sentences at a time — and the sentence whose reason cannot be given is the one to look at again.

## What this solves

A pupil hands in a piece that reads well and runs smoothly. The teacher cannot see which sentence the pupil wrote, which the tool wrote, and which the pupil wrote before the tool changed a few words. "Who wrote this?" becomes a guess; the pupil cannot say clearly either, and may not remember.

This page narrows the question to one sentence at a time: **write your own draft first, let the tool change three to five places only, then label three to five sentences using three labels and four columns that say how each sentence came about.**

Why the draft comes first: if the tool is opened first, there is no "before the tool" version in the pupil's hands, and afterwards every sentence becomes "I don't remember". The line at the end of the draft — **"Before the tool, I had written this far"** — is the dividing line. It cuts the lesson into two periods, tells the pupil where they stopped, and shows the teacher where the tool came in.

Why the tool changes only three to five places: this page is about how the pupil handles the places the tool touched, not about how much a tool can write. More than five changes and the tool is doing the writing, so the labelling stops meaning anything; fewer than three and pupils usually only swapped punctuation, where nothing can be seen.

Why three to five sentences at a time: labelling a whole piece sentence by sentence turns into a copying-and-sorting exercise, with the time going into the form rather than the reasons. Three to five is the amount that gives evidence — enough to show how the pupil judged, not so much that every row is filled in a hurry.

Read it at three levels. In **literacy and framework** it sits under "human-machine communication and collaboration" and "reflection on the application of AI". In **learning and teaching** it is a writing-lesson routine that changes no curriculum content. In **values** it exercises **responsibility** and **respect for intellectual property**: being able to say why a sentence is yours, and why you kept it or changed it.

## Learning objectives

Pupils will be able to:

1. **Write their own draft first**, in class, and mark the stopping point with the line "Before the tool, I had written this far".
2. Let the tool change **three to five places only**, and no others.
3. Label **three to five sentences at a time** with **three labels**: mine, both, tool.
4. Record each sentence in **four columns**: sentence, reason, what I changed, whether I will keep it.
5. State that a place where the tool changed punctuation only counts as **both**, never as **tool**.
6. Understand that the sheet is not marked and makes no judgement about how the work was produced; it asks for no chat record, screenshot, original draft or account detail, and the tool is always "the tool the school has specified".

The content covers two strands of AI literacy: **human-machine communication and collaboration** (knowing how many places the tool changed and which sentences they were) and **reflection on the application of AI** (knowing which sentence is yours and which you will keep). In values it rests on **responsibility** and **respect for intellectual property**: not deciding whether anything went wrong, but being able to give the source of a sentence and its fate.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson in five parts: 5 minutes of introduction, 20 minutes writing the draft, 10 minutes letting the tool change three to five places, 15 minutes labelling and filling the four columns, and 10 minutes to close. **The order of the five parts does not change**; if time runs short, cut the depth of the demonstration, never the number of labels or columns, and never widen the three-to-five limit.

| Time | Part | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 5 min | Introduction | Hand out the sheet, read the three labels and four columns once, read the three-to-five rule once; state that no conversation is handed over, nothing is screenshotted, the original draft is not collected and nothing is marked. |
| 20 min | Write the draft | Pupils write their own draft by hand in class until time is called, then write "Before the tool, I had written this far" at the stopping point. |
| 10 min | Three to five places | Pupils give the draft to the tool the school has specified and ask for three to five changes only, numbering each change on the page. |
| 15 min | Label and record | Three to five sentences at a time, each with its label and its four columns. |
| 10 min | Close | In pairs, read out the third column only; the teacher collects the record sheets, not the drafts. |

### 1 — Write your own draft first (20 minutes)

- **Write it in class, not at home.** This page needs the "before the tool" version, and writing in class gives the period a clear end.
- **By hand, on paper.** Written on a phone or a computer, the two periods can no longer be told apart afterwards.
- **No tool, no looking things up.** This time is only for what the pupil thinks of.
- **When time is called, start a new line after the last sentence and write:** "Before the tool, I had written this far". That line is not a sentence; it is the dividing line, and nothing written after it belongs to "before the tool".

The stopping point may fall mid-paragraph or even mid-sentence. Write it as it is; there is no need to finish a paragraph first.

### 2 — The tool changes three to five places only (10 minutes)

Give the draft to **the tool the school has specified** with one instruction: **change three to five places.**

- **The changes must be countable.** Number each one on the page ①②③④⑤ — three to five, no more and no fewer.
- **Three to five places, and the rest stays.** If the tool changes more, keep three to five of them and put the rest back as they were; this page is about the pupil's choices, not the tool's output.
- **A change may be:** a different word, an added condition, a sentence deleted, an order rearranged, an example added, punctuation altered.
- **Not a whole paragraph, not the whole piece.** If the tool rewrote a paragraph, take three to five places out of the rewrite and use the pupil's own sentences for the rest.

### 3 — Three labels (three only)

Three labels, **no fourth, and none renamed**:

| No. | Label | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Mine** | I wrote this sentence and the tool did not change it |
| 2 | **Both** | I wrote this sentence and the tool changed it — including punctuation only |
| 3 | **Tool** | The tool mainly produced or rewrote this sentence; I only chose it or adjusted it slightly |

The rules for the three labels:

- **Where the tool changed punctuation only, the label is "both", not "tool".** A comma, a full stop or a quotation mark is still the pupil's own sentence.
- **"Both" does not mean half and half.** If the pupil wrote the sentence and the tool touched it, it is "both"; the number of words changed does not matter.
- **"Tool" is about the body of the sentence.** If the pupil swaps one word in a sentence the tool produced, it is still "tool"; swapping a word does not change where the sentence came from.
- **A sentence that cannot be labelled is left blank for now.** Write "not clear yet" in the fourth column — a blank is more honest than a guessed label.

**Three to five sentences at a time.** The pupil chooses: the sentences they most want to explain. No pupil is asked to label a whole piece, and no teacher should ask for the rest to be filled in.

### 4 — The four-column record (four columns only)

| Column | What the pupil writes | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Sentence | Copy that sentence once, in its final labelled form | One sentence (transcribed) |
| 2 — Reason | Why it carries that label: I wrote it? The tool changed it? The tool produced it? | One sentence |
| 3 — What I changed | What I did to this sentence between the draft and now: nothing / punctuation / a word / deleted it / kept the tool's version | One sentence, an action |
| 4 — Will I keep it | Keep or not, with one reason | One sentence |

The rules for the four columns:

- **Four columns, no fifth.** Anything extra is said aloud, or read out at the close.
- **Column one copies the final version**, not the draft. Where the two differ, the difference goes in column three.
- **Column two gives a reason, not a verdict.** "I wrote this one and the tool only added a comma" is a reason; "this one is good" is not.
- **Column three is an action.** "Changed the punctuation", "swapped in 'therefore'", "deleted a sentence" — not "improved it".
- **Column four must decide.** Keep or not, either is fine, but it needs a reason; "don't know" is not an answer, though "keep it for now and look again next time" is.

### 5 — The teacher's checks

> The circular asks schools to teach pupils to protect personal privacy, to respect intellectual property and to keep to academic honesty.
>
> — Education Bureau Circular No. 156/2026, Appendix 2, section 4, item 4, the student section, "compliance with the law and safety"

That sentence is **the direction pupils are to be taught in**, and this page puts it into one act of labelling: being able to give the source of a sentence is the most concrete step in respecting intellectual property.

> When selecting learning and teaching materials, the source should be verifiable, accurate and credible, and where material is cited, its source or provenance should be given.
>
> — Chinese Language Curriculum Guide (Primary 1-6), section 6.1.1, the last principle for selecting learning and teaching resources

**That principle is a requirement on teachers selecting materials, not a rule for pupils.** It sits in this section because the demonstration material is the teacher's to prepare: what the teacher uses to demonstrate should have a source that can be given. **This page does not turn it into a pupil rule**, and does not ask pupils to account for sources on their drafts.

1. **Read the record sheet; collect neither the draft nor any conversation.** Do not collect, photocopy, ask for a paste of or screenshot any conversation, do not collect the original draft, and do not ask for a tool account or login detail.
2. **Mark nothing, grade nothing, rank nobody.** Check only that three to five sentences are labelled, that only the three labels are used, that each of the four columns carries an entry, and that column four states whether the sentence will be kept.
3. **Make no judgement about how the work was produced.** The sheet records where a sentence came from, not whether a pupil did anything wrong, and it never enters a conduct or examination record.
4. **Compare no pupil with another.** The sheet carries no name, only class and group, and the close works in pairs without reading anyone's sheet aloud.
5. **Labelling only — this page sets out no consequences.** It contains no school rules, no sanctions and no procedure for dealing with anything, and it evaluates no tool, platform or supplier.
6. **The tool is always "the tool the school has specified".** No brand, model, supplier, region or institution appears anywhere on the sheet.
7. **Keep every example fictional, and say so on the page and on the sheet.** The demonstration draft is written by the teacher, never taken from a real pupil's work.
8. **The Chinese Language documents quoted here cover the primary range only.** Both passages come from the Chinese Language Curriculum Guide (Primary 1-6); this page uses the same routine at S1-S6, but the passages must not be presented as a requirement of the secondary Chinese Language curriculum.

### 6 — Closing and extension (10 minutes)

In pairs, **read out the third column only** (what I changed), not the first or the fourth — reading the sentence turns the discussion into "is this well written?".

- **One sentence each.** When it has been read, the partner asks one question: "Did you write this one, or did the tool change it?"
- **The sentence that cannot be answered gets circled.** A circle is not an error; it is the sentence to look at again next time.
- **No marks, no comments, no names read aloud.**

Two extensions, neither adding a label or a column:

- **Run it again on another piece.** The next piece of writing: still three to five places, three to five sentences, three labels, four columns.
- **Do column four only.** Take an old draft and circle only the sentences where "will I keep it" still cannot be answered, with one reason each.

## Answer key and marking notes

The record sheet is **not marked, not graded and not ranked**. What follows is for the teacher's demonstration and for circulating round the room; every example is fictional.

### Three worked rows (fictional)

A fictional pupil's draft, before the tool: "The library is the most important place in the school. We go to the library every day. The library has a lot of books."

The fictional final version, where the tool changed two places and added one sentence: "The library is the school's most important learning space. We go to the library every break. The library has many different kinds of books, and quiet places to sit as well."

| Column | Example one | Example two | Example three |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Sentence | The library is the school's most important learning space. | We go to the library every break. | The library has many different kinds of books, and quiet places to sit as well. |
| 2 — Reason | I wrote this sentence; the tool only added the word "learning". | I wrote this sentence; the tool only added the words "every break". | The tool produced this sentence; I only changed one phrase. |
| 3 — What I changed | Kept the tool's two words. | Kept the tool's three words. | Kept the tool's sentence and swapped one phrase. |
| 4 — Will I keep it | Keep: with "learning" in it, the library is more than a place. | Keep: it says which break, which is more specific. | Not keep: the sentence is too long; I will write it as two. |

The difference is visible: examples one and two are **both** — the pupil wrote the sentence and the tool added a few words; example three is **tool** — the tool produced the sentence, and swapping one phrase does not change that.

An example of punctuation only (fictional): "We go to the library every day" becomes "We go to the library every day," — one comma added, so the label is **both**, not **tool**. The reason column may say: "The tool only added a comma; the sentence is mine."

### Common wrong answers and responses

- **The whole piece labelled sentence by sentence.** Response: "You don't need the whole piece. Pick three to five — the ones you most want to explain."
- **A punctuation-only change labelled "tool".** Response: "The tool changed the punctuation, but you wrote the sentence. That one is both."
- **"Both" explained as half and half.** Response: "You don't count the words. You wrote the sentence and the tool touched it, so it is both."
- **Column two says "this one is better".** Response: "The reason is where it came from: you wrote it, the tool changed it, or the tool produced it."
- **Column three says "improved".** Response: "Write the action: the punctuation, which word, which sentence deleted."
- **Column four says "don't know".** Response: "Keep it for now if you like, but give one reason."
- **A pupil is asked to hand over the chat or the draft.** Don't. This page exists to replace that practice and to collect less pupil data.
