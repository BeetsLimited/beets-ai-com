## What this solves

After pupils use AI for homework, the hardest thing for a teacher to see is often not whether the answer is right, but whether the pupil actually thought about it. Reading through a chat history is neither practical nor good for privacy.

These three short tasks (about ten minutes each) let a teacher see pupils' judgement without uploading any pupil work or reading a chat history:

1. Can they judge, sentence by sentence, which parts of an AI answer are trustworthy and which are doubtful?
2. Can they spot which part of a classmate's answer the writer actually understands and can explain?
3. Do they know what still needs checking before a piece of work is handed in?

Each task comes with a simple scoring guide (strong / developing / needs help) and a follow-up activity, and fits any subject: a teacher only swaps in a short AI answer, a classmate's answer, or a piece of draft work from their own subject.

## Learning objectives

By the end, pupils can:

1. Judge sentence by sentence which claims in an AI answer are trustworthy, and say what evidence is missing.
2. Tell which parts of an answer the writer could explain in their own words, and which parts look copied.
3. List what to check before handing in work (sources, facts, can-I-explain-it), not just spelling and layout.

## How to use it

The three tasks can each run as a ten-minute lesson opener, or all together in one lesson (about 40 minutes).

1. **Introduction (5 min)** — Set the goal: "We are not judging whether AI was used; we are learning to judge." Agree the ground rule: these tasks do not grade whether a pupil used AI.
2. **Task 1: Trustworthy or not? (10 min)** — Hand out the sheet. Pupils circle the claims they trust and the ones they doubt, and write what evidence is missing.
3. **Task 2: What can you defend? (10 min)** — Pupils underline the part of a classmate's answer that the writer could explain in their own words, and write one sentence saying why.
4. **Task 3: Ready to hand in? (10 min)** — Pupils read a draft piece of work and list what to check before handing it in.
5. **Wrap-up and follow-up (5 min)** — Choose one follow-up activity (below) and collect the answers as evidence for what to teach next.

## Answer key and marking notes

All three tasks are open-ended, but good answers share checkable features. The examples below are fictional; teachers can swap in content from their own subject.

### Task 1: Trustworthy or not?

**Fictional example (an AI answer):** "Drinking more water is good for you and can make you smarter. Many studies say water is important, so you should always drink eight glasses a day."

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | Points to "make you smarter" and "eight glasses" as doubtful, and says what evidence is missing (for example "it does not say which studies" or "there is no source for eight glasses"). |
| **Developing** | Can tell which parts sound reasonable and which are doubtful, but cannot say what evidence is missing. |
| **Needs help** | Accepts or rejects the whole answer without going sentence by sentence. |

**Follow-up:** sort the class's "missing evidence" into three kinds — source, data, example — and post them as a classroom poster.

### Task 2: What can you defend?

**Fictional example (a classmate's answer):** "We should recycle rubbish because it keeps the earth less dirty. In addition, through systematic waste separation and resource recovery, we can effectively reduce the burden on landfills."

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | Correctly spots that the first sentence is the writer's own words (plain, close to everyday life) and the second looks copied (technical terms, no explanation), and says why. |
| **Developing** | Spots some of it, but the reason is vague (for example only "this sentence is harder"). |
| **Needs help** | Cannot tell the difference, or thinks the whole answer is either all the writer's own or all copied. |

**Follow-up:** pupils take out their last piece of work and circle the parts they can explain in their own words, practising defending their own answer.

### Task 3: Ready to hand in?

**Fictional example (a draft):** "Dinosaurs died out about 66 million years ago. Some scientists say a large asteroid hitting the earth may have caused it. I think this theory is cool, so it must be right."

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | Lists at least three things to check, including sources and facts (for example "which scientist said this?" and "is this fact or guess?"), and can say which one matters most. |
| **Developing** | Lists only surface checks (spelling, layout) and misses sources and facts. |
| **Needs help** | Thinks "finished writing" means "ready to hand in", and cannot name anything to check. |

**Follow-up:** merge the individual checklists into one shared "before-you-hand-in" checklist and post it in the classroom.

### What not to grade

**Do not grade whether a pupil used AI.** These tasks score judgement — spotting missing evidence, defending understanding, planning a check — not the tool a pupil used. Using AI is not a fault here; the point is to see where the pupil's thinking stops and what to teach next.
