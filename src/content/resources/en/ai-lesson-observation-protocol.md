> What an observation is for is seeing where AI was used in this lesson, who checked it, and what the pupils did themselves — not whether this teacher taught well.

## What this solves

Once a school starts using AI in class, two kinds of observation appear, and neither helps the next lesson:

- **The old observation form.** It asks whether the explanation was clear, whether the questioning had layers, whether the timing was sound. Those still matter, but not one column can show what AI did in the lesson — so the record and the lesson do not match, and the feedback meeting can only talk about technique.
- **The tool review.** The opposite: the whole lesson is spent on whether the tool was easy, whether the interface was smooth, whether pupils were excited. A usable tool is not the same as learning, and the next lesson uses a different tool, so the conclusion is worthless.

This resource offers a third way: **a one-page six-column record that captures only what is visible in this lesson**, with a 30-minute feedback meeting and three tests. The attachment is an editable Word document; the record photocopies onto one page and goes into the room with you.

**Three boundaries must be stated first.**

1. **Nothing is graded and nothing goes into staff appraisal.** The record has no score column, no grade, no scale. Its only purpose is the next lesson. The moment a record can reach a personnel file, an appraisal or a promotion discussion, the lesson becomes a performance and the record stops working.
2. **This is this resource's suggested record, not an Education Bureau observation instrument.** The official documents this resource draws on contain no "AI lesson observation indicators" and prescribe no columns for an observation record. The six columns, the three tests and the meeting below are **this resource's suggestion**, and a school may change them.
3. **This page looks at one lesson.** School-wide or cross-subject coverage counting is a different tool's job; bringing a coverage question into a single lesson's observation ruins both.

## Purpose

With this resource, the observer and the teacher being observed can:

1. Spend 15 minutes before the lesson settling why AI is being used, instead of guessing on the way in.
2. Use a one-page six-column record to capture visible facts rather than impressions and judgements.
3. Use a 30-minute meeting to turn the record into **one** thing to improve and one change that can be made next time.
4. Use three tests to judge whether the AI use in a lesson holds up — not whether the teacher does.
5. Accumulate the "one thing to improve" from several observations into the panel's self-evaluation evidence, joining the school's existing self-evaluation cycle.

## How to use it

### Step 1: The three things to settle before the lesson (about 15 minutes)

All three happen **before** the lesson. Miss one and the record turns into guesswork halfway through.

1. **A 15-minute briefing: the teacher explains the learning objective and why this lesson uses AI.** The observer is listening for which learning objective the tool serves, and what the class would learn less of without it. There is no need to discuss method here; this is about purpose.
2. **Agree how to record: one page, six columns, words only.** No video, no photographs, no pupil names (use seat numbers or codes), no collection of pupil work. The record goes back to the teacher the same day; the observer keeps no copy.
3. **Fix the meeting: within three working days of the lesson, 30 minutes.** Open it by saying that the record is not graded, does not go into appraisal and will not be seen by a third party. Say it once before the lesson and again on the day.

### Step 2: The one-page six-column record (during the lesson)

The order of the columns is deliberate: from the teacher's design, to the pupils, and only then to one thing to improve. Reverse it and the record becomes a conclusion looking for evidence.

| Column | What goes in it | How it reads (example) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · Activity | How the lesson divides up, and how long each part took | "Opening 5 min / group task 20 min / reporting 10 min / close 5 min" |
| 2 · Use of AI | What AI was used for in each part; write "none" where it was not used | "Group task: rewrite three openings with the tool, pupils choose one and explain why" |
| 3 · Teacher's check | Where the teacher made a judgement (choosing the task, removing data, limiting the output, rejecting an answer, requiring a redo) | "Removed the school name from the model text before demonstrating; rejected one of the tool's examples and used their own" |
| 4 · Pupil verification | How pupils checked the tool's output; whether anyone actually stopped to look | "Two groups checked a date against the textbook; one group adopted the answer without checking" |
| 5 · Pupil's own work | Where the pupil wrote or revised something themselves; how much of the product shows their own judgement | "Each pupil wrote 50 characters first, then compared with the tool; three pieces kept the original wording" |
| 6 · One thing to improve | **Facts and location only**: which activity, and what happened | "Activity 2: three of four groups adopted the tool's example without any checking step" |

**Column 6 is the easiest to get wrong and does most to set the tone of the meeting.** Facts and location, no evaluation:

- Write "Activity 2: three groups adopted the tool's example without any checking step", not "pupils are too dependent on the tool".
- Write "Activity 1: the opening ran 12 minutes, leaving 13 for the group task", not "poor time management".
- Write "Activity 3: no one mentioned what they had changed when reporting", not "weak presentation skills".

A fact with a location gives the meeting something to work on; a judgement turns the meeting into a defence.

### Step 3: The 30-minute feedback meeting (within three working days)

Five parts, in this order. The first belongs to the teacher, and it is the most important step in the process.

| Time | Content | Who speaks |
|---|---|---|
| 0-5 min | **The teacher speaks first**: what I meant to achieve, what actually happened, and where it differed from what I expected | The teacher |
| 5-13 min | **The observer reads only the facts on the record**, column by column, adding no evaluation. Then asks: "have I recorded anything wrongly?" | The observer |
| 13-20 min | **Choose one thing to improve together** — one only. Start from column 6, but the teacher chooses, not the observer | Both |
| 20-27 min | **Write one change that can be made next time**: which activity, changed how, and what is needed | Both |
| 27-30 min | **Say where the record goes**: back to the teacher, no copy kept; the "one thing to improve" goes into the panel's self-evaluation record | The observer |

Three rules:

- **The observer does not interrupt the first part.** If the teacher hears an evaluation before finishing the design intent, the whole meeting becomes explanation and defence.
- **One thing only.** A list of three strengths and three weaknesses changes nothing next time; one change that is actually made earns a second observation.
- **The change must be small enough to make next time.** "Redesign the whole unit" is not a change; "add a three-step checking card to the desks in activity 2" is.

### Step 4: The three tests (used in the meeting, never as a score)

The three tests judge whether the AI use in a lesson holds up. Each is either visible or not visible; there is no middle grade.

1. **The learning objective comes first.** The lesson's use of AI can state which learning objective it serves. If it cannot, the activity came before the objective. The question is not "was AI used" but "why was it used".
2. **The check sits at a key point.** Of the three points — input, output, adoption — the teacher made a visible judgement at least once (changed the task, removed data, limited the output, rejected an answer, required a redo). With no judgement at any of the three, the tool is effectively teaching the lesson.
3. **The pupils left their own judgement in it.** Pupils can say what they checked and what they changed, and the product shows their own choices. A product with no trace of the pupil's hand does not show that learning happened.

Three cautions:

- **The three tests are not a marking scheme.** Do not total them into a score or write "all three met". Use them to decide what the meeting discusses.
- **Do not apply them during the lesson.** Record facts while observing; the tests belong to the meeting, otherwise the record is led by the conclusion.
- **They can be used to reject a design.** If the briefing already shows that none of the three would be met, the design is worth changing before the lesson is taught and observed.

### Step 5: Joining the school's self-evaluation cycle (once a term)

The observation is not the point; its output has to answer "what has our subject changed about teaching with AI".

- **Two or three a term, no more.** More observations with hasty meetings are worth less than two done properly.
- **Copy the "one thing to improve" into the panel's annual self-evaluation record**, one line each, with the date, the class and the activity. By the end of the year that page is the evidence of what was changed, and the starting point for the next observation.
- **Join the three school-level nodes: planning, implementation, evaluation.** The observation and its meeting are evidence for implementation; the panel's record is used at evaluation; at planning it decides which subject and which kind of lesson to look at next year.
- **Do not create a second system.** If the school already has an observation arrangement, put the six columns inside it rather than running both.

## Review checklist

| Check | Done properly | Needs work |
|---|---|---|
| Before the lesson | All three settled, including why AI is used, at the briefing | Asking on the way in which tool the lesson uses |
| The record | One page, six columns, every column filled; "none" where AI was not used | Comments on technique only, with no column for the use of AI |
| Column 6 | Facts and location only (which activity, what happened) | "Pupils are too dependent" or "poor time management" |
| Scores | No score, grade or scale anywhere on the record | A score column or a total |
| Appraisal | Said at the start that it goes into no appraisal, and the record goes back to the teacher | The observer keeps the record, or appraisal is mentioned |
| The meeting | 30 minutes, teacher first, observer reads facts only | The observer evaluates first, or it becomes an inquisition |
| The change | One only, naming the activity and the action | Five recommendations, none with an action |
| The tests | Used to choose what to discuss, never totalled | The three tests added up as "how far met" |
| Pupils | No video, photographs, names or collected work | Pupil names or photographs in the record |

> The test: **when the meeting ends, the teacher is holding a next action, not a set of comments.** Without one change that can be made next time, the observation was only an inspection.
