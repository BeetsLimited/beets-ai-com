> When a sentence is wrong, the part worth recording is not the sentence but how you found out. Five cells for one error, one of four types, three follow-up questions.

## What this solves

A pupil uses a tool for homework and one answer is wrong. Marking the work shows the teacher the mistake, not whether the pupil ever noticed it: whether they spotted it themselves, copied it down, or never knew. Two pieces of work then differ only in how many errors they contain, not in whether anyone checked.

This page does one thing: **it takes one error that has already happened and looks at it once, afterwards, on a five-cell sheet.** It is not prevention, not marking and not blame. The error has happened, the work has come back, and the pupil now sits down to write the episode out plainly.

Because the page sits after the event, its division of labour with three others has to be read with it:

- The routine for checking before you use an answer belongs to the multi-source verification page; this page neither rewrites nor alters it.
- The routine for checking before you forward a screenshot belongs to the three-step screenshot page; this page neither rewrites nor alters it.
- The ten-minute judgement tasks belong to the quick-checks page; this page neither rewrites nor alters them.

This page records one past error, once. The same applies to vocabulary: **a hallucination is not a word to memorise here** — it is the path a pupil writes in cell two, the source, the material or the calculation through which they found that the sentence did not hold. To verify and to cross-check both appear on this page, but only in that one after-the-event use.

Read it at three levels. In **literacy and framework** it sits under the three learning strands of AI literacy. In **learning and teaching** it is a one-lesson classroom routine that changes no curriculum content. In **values** it exercises critical discernment and responsibility: being willing to correct an error once it is found, rather than leaving the wrong sentence in place or handing the responsibility to the tool.

## Learning objectives

Pupils will be able to:

1. Record one past error in **five cells**, one sentence each, all five filled.
2. Place that error in **one of four types**: not true to the facts, overstated in scope or degree, out of date, or someone else's work presented as unattributed.
3. Use **three** fixed follow-up questions to say why they believed it at the time, how they found out, and what they would do earlier next time.
4. Name what they changed: the sentence, the one place in it, or the way they worked.
5. Understand that the sheet is not marked, evaluates no tool, judges nobody's honesty, and asks for no chat record, screenshot or account detail.

The content covers the three strands of AI literacy: **how AI basically works** (output is generated and can be wrong), **human-machine communication and collaboration** (the pupil knows which cell they have to check), and **reflection on the application of AI** (knowing how to catch it earlier next time). In values it rests on **critical discernment** and **responsibility**: not treating fluent writing as true, and being willing to correct an error and say which part was changed. Data bias surfaces naturally when cell three is cross-checked — the same type of error looks different in different subjects — and how far to take that is the teacher's judgement for the class.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson in five parts: 5 minutes of introduction and the three questions, 10 minutes of demonstration, 25 minutes of writing, 10 minutes of cross-checking cell three, and 10 minutes to close. **The order of the five parts does not change**; if time runs short, cut the depth of the demonstration, never the number of cells, types or questions.

| Time | Part | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 5 min | Introduction and the three questions | Hand out the sheet, read the five cells and the four types once, read the three follow-up questions once; state that no chat record is collected, no name is written and nothing is marked. |
| 10 min | Demonstration | The teacher fills all five cells with a fictional example and explains why cell three takes one type only. |
| 25 min | Pupils write | Parts 1, 2 and 3: the five cells, then the type, then the three questions. |
| 10 min | Cross-check cell three | In pairs, looking only at cells two and three: does the evidence support the type? |
| 10 min | Close | The class reads the three sentences; the teacher collects the record sheets only. |

### 1 — The five cells (fixed)

One error per sheet. Choose the most recent one the pupil can genuinely account for; if there is none, use the teacher's fictional example.

| Cell | What the pupil writes | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — What it said | Copy that sentence as it stood, without tidying it | One sentence (transcribed) |
| 2 — How I found out | The source, the material or the calculation that showed it was wrong | One sentence, naming what they used |
| 3 — Which type of error | One of the four types, by name | One type |
| 4 — What I changed | The sentence, the one place in it, or the way they worked | One sentence |
| 5 — Earlier next time | The one thing they would do earlier on the same kind of task | One sentence, an action |

The rules for the five cells:

- **One sentence per cell, and no sixth cell.** Anything extra is said aloud, not written on the sheet.
- **Cell one is transcribed.** It may be clumsy; it must not be tidied into a sentence that sounds reasonable, because this cell is what was actually there.
- **Cell two names what they used.** "It felt wrong" is not an answer; "I checked page 12 of the textbook" is. This cell is the trace of verification.
- **Cell three takes one type only.** Two types means the episode is not yet thought through, not that the answer is detailed.
- **Cell five is an action, not an attitude.** "Be more careful" is not an action; "open a source I can check the moment I see a year" is.

### 2 — The four error types (fixed)

The four are a **classification**, not a severity scale and not a judgement about anyone. Getting the type right or wrong carries no mark — nothing on this sheet is marked.

| Type | In one sentence | What usually appears on the sheet |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Not true to the facts | The sentence contradicts something checkable: a date, a number, a name, a relationship | Cell two names a source that can be checked |
| 2 — Overstated in scope or degree | The fact may be right, but the sentence says "all", "always", "the most", "every" | Cell two gives one exception or one uncovered case |
| 3 — Out of date | It was true once and is not now: a policy, a list, a timetable, a version | Cell two names the current version or date |
| 4 — Someone else's work, unattributed | The sentence comes from another source and the output did not say where | Cell two says which book and which page |

The rules for the four types:

- **One type only.** A sentence can carry two problems; take the one the pupil noticed first.
- **Type four records the output, not the person.** The pupil writes "this sentence comes from chapter 3 of the textbook" and judges nobody's honesty; no name is written anywhere. The type is kept because it is the one most easily missed when looking back, not because it is the most serious.
- **If no type fits, add one sentence to cell two.** That usually settles it.

### 3 — The three follow-up questions (fixed)

The order is fixed, and **no fourth question is added, none is dropped**:

1. "Why did I believe this sentence at the time?"
2. "What did I use to find out it was wrong?"
3. "Next time, what would I do earlier on the same task?"

Why the order cannot change: asking why they believed it first makes the pupil describe the judgement they actually made. Asking how they found out first invites a sentence that is wise only after the fact. The third question must end in an action, because the point of the page is not to record one error but to catch the next one earlier.

One sentence per question is enough. A paragraph means the third question never landed on an action.

### 4 — Cross-checking cell three (10 minutes)

In pairs, swap only cells two and three and **do not look at cells one or four** — seeing the original sentence turns the discussion into "was this obvious?" rather than "which type is it?".

- **The evidence supports the type**: the partner can say "you found it through this source, so I agree it is that type", and puts a small tick beside cell three.
- **The evidence does not support it**: together they look at what cell two still lacks, then change the type or add the source. Changed is changed; nobody counts who changed more.
- **The pair disagrees**: both versions stay, each says which source it rests on, and it goes to the teacher. Disagreement is not an error.

Cross-check here means something narrow: **the same classification, looked at once through another pair of eyes.** It is not peer marking, not a comparison of who wrote better, and no name is read aloud.

### 5 — The teacher's checks

> The Bureau's circular asks teachers to maintain professional gatekeeping when they apply generative artificial intelligence: to respect intellectual property, to keep their professional insight, to review AI-generated teaching content rigorously, and to guard against potential data bias and "AI hallucination".

The circular's requirement is **a requirement on teachers**, not a rule for pupils to follow; it sits in this section because the demonstration and the reading of the sheets are the teacher's work.

1. **Read the five cells, not the tool's screen.** Do not collect, ask for a paste of, or photograph a chat record, and do not ask for a tool account or login detail.
2. **Evaluate no tool, platform or supplier.** The sheet records that a sentence did not hold, not that a tool is unreliable, and it is never written as a tool lying or deliberately misleading anyone — output can be wrong without that judgement being made here.
3. **Judge nobody's honesty.** Type four records where a sentence came from, not anyone's character, and the sheet never enters a conduct or examination record.
4. **Mark nothing.** Check only that the five cells are filled, that cell two names a source, that cell three carries one type, and that cell five is an action.
5. **Compare no pupil with another.** The sheet carries no name, only class and group, and the cross-check runs in pairs without reading anyone's sheet aloud.
6. **Keep every example fictional, and say so on the page and on the sheet.** The demonstration uses invented sentences, never a real pupil's work.
7. **Never present any of it as a Bureau requirement.** The five cells, the four types, the three questions and the 60-minute shape are this resource's suggestion and a school may adapt them; no legal provision is cited anywhere.

### 6 — Closing and extension (10 minutes)

The class reads three sentences aloud, as written, with no commentary:

1. "I believed it because ____."
2. "I found out it was wrong through ____."
3. "Next time I will ____ earlier."

Two extensions, neither adding a cell, a type or a question:

- **Record another one in another subject.** The back of the same sheet, still five cells, four types, three questions. The subject changes; the numbers do not.
- **Copy cell five onto a small card.** Just that one action, stuck inside the exercise book, read once before the next piece of homework.

## Answer key and marking notes

The record sheet is **not marked, not graded and not ranked**. What follows is for the teacher's demonstration and for circulating round the room; every example is fictional.

### Three worked examples of the five cells (fictional)

| Cell | Example one | Example two |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — What it said | "This footbridge was completed in 1998." | "Every secondary school in Hong Kong already has an AI policy." |
| 2 — How I found out | I checked the yearbook in the library; it says 1996. | I asked pupils from three schools; two said they had none. |
| 3 — Which type of error | Not true to the facts | Overstated in scope or degree |
| 4 — What I changed | Changed the year to 1996 and added the yearbook's title. | Changed it to "the few schools we asked differed". |
| 5 — Earlier next time | When I see a year, open a source I can check before writing it down. | When I see "all" or "every", ask how many it actually covers. |

| Cell | Example three |
|---|---|
| 1 — What it said | "The library is open Monday to Friday, 8 am to 6 pm." |
| 2 — How I found out | I checked the current timetable on the school website; Friday now ends at 5 pm. |
| 3 — Which type of error | Out of date |
| 4 — What I changed | Corrected it to the website's timetable and wrote the date I checked. |
| 5 — Earlier next time | When I see a time, a list or a policy, open the school website first. |

An example of type four (fictional): "This paragraph is almost the same as a sentence in chapter 3 of the textbook, but the output did not say where it came from." Cell two reads "I found the sentence in chapter 3 of the textbook"; cell four reads "added 'from chapter 3 of the textbook'"; cell five reads "when a whole paragraph reads very smoothly, search one of its sentences".

Type four has to be written as neutrally as that so that a classification never turns into an accusation: the pupil writes **where the source is**, not **who copied whom**.

### Three worked answers to the follow-up questions (fictional)

1. "I believed it because the sentence had a year and a place name, and read as if someone had checked." That describes the judgement made at the time.
2. "I found out through the diagram on page 12 of the textbook." One sentence, naming the source.
3. "Next time I see a year, I will open the textbook and check it before I write it down." An action.

### Common wrong answers and responses

- **Cell two says "it felt wrong".** Response: "What made it feel wrong? Write down the material you checked." Nothing is marked; one cell is filled in.
- **Cell three carries two types.** Response: "Two types means it is not chosen yet. Which did you notice first?"
- **Cell five says "be more careful" or "stop using it".** Response: "Neither is an action. What would you do earlier on the same task?" Not using a tool can be a legitimate choice, but this cell asks for an action.
- **Type four is written as an accusation against a classmate.** Response: "This cell records the source, not the person. Where the source is, is enough."
- **A pupil is asked to paste the chat.** Don't. This page exists to replace that practice and to collect less pupil data.

### Checklist (run through once before the lesson)

- □ The sheet has exactly five cells: what it said, how I found out, which type of error, what I changed, earlier next time.
- □ The sheet states one error per sheet and one sentence per cell.
- □ All four types are present: not true to the facts, overstated in scope or degree, out of date, someone else's work unattributed.
- □ The three follow-up questions are present, in the order why I believed it, how I found out, what I would do earlier.
- □ Nothing asks for a chat record to be pasted, photographed or handed over, and nothing asks for an account or login detail.
- □ Nothing evaluates a tool, platform or supplier, and nothing is written as a tool lying or deliberately misleading.
- □ Nothing judges honesty, cheating or plagiarism, and no legal provision is cited.
- □ The sheet has no name field, only class and group.
- □ No pupil is compared with another, and there is no score, grade or rank.
- □ No brand, model, supplier, region or institution is named; the tool is always "the tool the school has specified".
- □ Every example is fictional, and the page and the sheet both say so.
- □ Nothing is presented as a Bureau requirement; the five cells, four types, three questions and the 60-minute shape are this resource's suggestion.

> The point: **five cells for one error, one of four types, three questions for one path.** If cell two can name what the pupil used, the page has done its work; if it cannot, the pupil is still at "it felt wrong".
