> A pupil in the first key stage is not a smaller secondary pupil: they can read the answer a tool gives, but not always tell whose sentence it is. So supervision is not mainly about whether the tool may be used. It is about somebody being present every time, a limit on the minutes, and one question afterwards that somebody writes down.

## What this solves

The commonest problems in an AI lesson in the first key stage are not about using the tool too deeply. They are four things nobody wrote down:

- **A demonstration becomes half the lesson.** The teacher means to show one thing, hands go up, and fifteen minutes pass. The bell goes and nobody remembers that the answer on the screen was written by the tool.
- **A group shares one account.** Six pupils to one device, one account logged into three times in a row, and nobody can say who asked what or who saw what.
- **A parent thinks they are supervising.** The parent sits alongside but is looking at their own phone while the child asks one question after another. Afterwards the parent remembers only that "he loved it".
- **Homework becomes ghostwriting.** The worksheet goes home, the question is pasted in, and the answer comes back far neater than the child's own writing, so the teacher marks the neatness.

None of these is an attitude problem. Each is a missing column: **who operates, who is present, how many minutes**. This page adds those three columns, then five rules, one page for parents, and four record items.

**Three things to say plainly first:**

1. **The name of this page (a supervision guide), the four-setting split, the five rules and the parents' page are a suggestion from this resource, not an Education Bureau requirement.** What the Bureau's documents set out for the first key stage is a **learning-and-teaching approach** - demonstration and first-hand experience, initial contact under the teacher's guidance, and avoiding overuse and premature dependence - together with **home-school cooperation**. They prescribe no supervision form, no number of minutes and no format for a parent consent sheet.
2. **"No more than ten minutes at a time" is a suggestion from this resource, not an Education Bureau requirement.** A school may adjust it to its classes, group sizes and equipment; once adjusted, write the actual number of minutes on the settings table and on the parents' page so that teachers and parents read the same figure.
3. **The Primary Information and Innovation Technology curriculum framework is a trial version, and there is no equivalent official framework at secondary level, so this page does not apply to secondary schools.** A trial version is not final and a school should adjust it to its own situation; the three columns and the five rules here are written for P1 to P3 only.

## Purpose

By the end of this guide a school will be able to:

1. Use **four settings** (class demonstration, group activity, use at home, after-lesson task) to put "how it is used" on one table whose every cell has three columns: who operates, who is present, how many minutes.
2. Use **five rules** to turn the common floor under all four settings into five sentences that can be checked on the spot, rather than a paragraph of principle.
3. Use the **parents' page** to set out three things to do and three to avoid, sent with the parent letter, with no reply slip and no collection of a pupil's name or photograph.
4. Use **four record items** to leave a trace that it happened: one line in the class log, one form-teacher lesson, one record of the parents' page going out, and one interface for anything unusual.
5. Treat five existing school documents as **interfaces only**: cite them, change none of their wording, and open no second set.

The guide starts from **nurturing the whole person, with technology as an aid**, and takes as its values the four principles of **technology for good, nurturing the whole person, equity and access, and forward-looking innovation**. It is written around the three learning strands of AI literacy, and the strand it serves is **reflection on the application of AI**. The three strands are the fundamental operating principles of AI, human-AI communication and collaboration, and reflection on the application of AI. The four attitude sets - **critical discernment, timely and appropriate use, human-AI collaboration and technology for good** - look, with a seven-year-old, most concretely like the question "who do you think wrote that answer?" and a teacher who lets the pupil answer it. This is a **supervision** document rather than a permission slip because at this age a pupil needs somebody in the room, not a switch.

## How to use it

The file is a Word document in six parts: the five facts at the top, the four settings, the five rules, the parents' page, the four records and interfaces, and a one-page proofreading list. The curriculum lead or a form teacher should lead it: agree it once in 30 minutes, then read it with the class in one 30-minute form-teacher lesson each term, and send the parents' page out with the parent letter.

### Step 1: four settings, and only four

The four are fixed; none is added and none removed. Each setting fills three columns only: who operates, who is present, how many minutes. The fourth column, the one question, is for the adult in the room, not for the pupil to fill in.

| Setting | Who operates | Who is present | Minutes | One question afterwards |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Class demonstration | The teacher, on a school account, projected for the class | The teacher; pupils watch and do not operate | 5 to 8 | "Was that answer written by the tool or by the teacher?" |
| 2 Group activity | Pupils take turns under the teacher's guidance, one device per group, on a school account | The teacher or a teaching assistant, inside the group | 10 | "Which word did you change, and how did the answer change?" |
| 3 Use at home | The parent, or parent and child side by side | The parent, without leaving the seat | 10 | "Did anything in that answer seem odd to you?" |
| 4 After-lesson task | Pupils complete it in the lesson under the teacher's guidance; it is not sent home to be done alone | The teacher | 10 | "Which part did you write, and which part did the tool write?" |

Three things worth saying about using them:

- **The fourth setting happens in the lesson.** Once a task at this age goes home, nobody knows what was asked or how much was copied. Putting it in the last ten minutes of the lesson means the teacher sees the whole process, and no parent has to enforce a rule at home.
- **"How many minutes" is a number, not a range.** "Ten minutes" is enough; "about ten minutes" is no limit at all, and both pupil and parent will add time.
- **Where one device per pupil is impossible, reduce the number of pupils, not the accounts.** Split a group of six into two rounds of three; a shared account makes the record impossible to match to a person, and "who asked what" can never be answered.

### Step 2: five supervision rules, and only five

The five are a fixed number; none is added and none removed. Each is written as one sentence that can be checked on the spot, with the reason beside it.

| Rule | How it reads | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 The teacher operates, or a school account | Demonstrations are run by the teacher; when pupils operate they use a school account, and are not asked to open an account or register in the lesson | The school holds the account and can close it afterwards; an account a pupil opens follows them up the school |
| 2 No identifiable data goes in | No name, class, class number, photograph, address, phone number or family detail; where an example is needed, use invented details | At this age the first thing typed in is a pupil's own name and class, because those boxes are already printed on the worksheet |
| 3 No more than ten minutes at a time | One continuous use stops at ten minutes; it does not run on because "we are nearly finished" | Ten minutes is **this resource's suggestion**, not an Education Bureau requirement; written as a number, it is something the adult can stop at |
| 4 One question afterwards | The adult asks one question, the pupil answers one, "I don't know" counts as an answer, and it goes in the class log | The point of the check is not to catch a mistake but to show the pupil that somebody reads the answer |
| 5 Stop and record anything unusual | Inappropriate content, a conversation that upsets a pupil, or a suspected personal detail: stop there, write one line, hand it to the form teacher | Stopping is far easier than explaining afterwards, and one line is enough to account for it |

Three things to add under the rules page:

- **None of the five says "must not be used".** For a pupil this age, contact with a tool under the teacher's guidance is part of the learning; what has to be written is "under the teacher's guidance" and "how many minutes", not a prohibition.
- **Rule 3 can change, and the change must be written down.** If a school moves the limit to eight or twelve minutes, change the settings table and the parents' page at the same time, so all three carry one number.
- **Rule 5's "record" is one line, not a form.** "An unsuitable reply appeared during a group activity in class X; use stopped and the form teacher was told" is enough; the detail is handled under the school's existing procedures, and no second incident form is opened.

### Step 3: the parents' page, three to do and three to avoid

This page is printed single-sided and sent with the parent letter. It does not explain what the tool is. It sets out three things a parent can do at home and three it is better to avoid.

**Three things to do:**

1. **Sit with them.** Same table, same screen. You do not need to know how to operate it; you need to be there.
2. **Ask before, ask after.** Before: "what do you want to ask it?" After: "how much of that answer do you believe?"
3. **Say where the limits are.** Tell the child that it writes confidently and is not always right, and say what the time limit at home is - the same number as at school.

**Three things to avoid:**

1. **Do not open an account for the child, and do not upload their photograph.** Both are hard to undo, and the school account is enough for classroom use.
2. **Do not become the tutor.** A parent's job is to sit alongside and ask one question, not to explain the tool's answer again; explained twice, the child learns that there are two versions of an answer.
3. **Do not hand in generated work as the child's own.** Work handed in says which part the pupil wrote and which part the tool wrote; the pupil fills that line in.

The parents' page has three things to do and three to avoid - six items, no more and no fewer. It carries no box for a name, a class or a photograph, and no reply slip; a parent who wants to respond uses the contact route already on the parent letter. The wording deliberately contains no prohibition, because at this age what a child needs most is somebody sitting alongside, not another house rule.

### Step 4: four record items

Four records, each one line or one page, and no new form.

| Item | What it holds | Who writes it | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 One line in the class log | Which class, which setting, how many minutes, anything unusual; no pupil's name | The teacher present | The same day |
| 2 One form-teacher lesson | Once a term, 30 minutes, reading the four settings and the five rules with the class | The form teacher | Once a term |
| 3 The parents' page going out | The date and the class; no parent's name, and no reply slip collected | The form teacher | With the parent letter |
| 4 The interface for anything unusual | One line of description, recorded in the whole-school AI risk register and handled under the school's existing procedures and the requirements of the law | Whoever found it | The same day |

The interfaces with six existing resources are listed, not changed: the **parent communication pack** explains the school's overall AI use and consent arrangements, and this page only adds the three classroom columns; the **student AI-use code and classroom posters** are the P1 to S6 version, and this page is that code made concrete for P1 to P3; the **data-minimisation checklist** lists the categories that may and may not be typed in, and is cited here rather than repeated; the **whole-school AI risk register and incident process** is the only record of anything unusual, and this page opens no second form; the **assessment-integrity policy** deals with task design at secondary level, and this page stays inside its boundary for a P1 after-lesson task; the **hallucination hunt** is a multi-source verification routine for secondary pupils, and this page only demonstrates once, in the last ten minutes, that an answer has to be read by somebody. None of those six has its wording changed, and none has its items or counts altered because of this page.

### Step 5: P1 to P3, and the difference from secondary

The four settings and the five rules are written for P1 to P3 only. The first key stage works through demonstration and first-hand experience, initial contact under the teacher's guidance, and avoiding overuse and premature dependence, which is why "pupils operate" appears here only inside the group activity and the after-lesson task, and both require the teacher to be present. There is no equivalent official framework at secondary level, and the room for a pupil to operate and judge independently is different, so this page does not apply there; secondary schools should use the student AI-use code and the assessment-integrity policy.
