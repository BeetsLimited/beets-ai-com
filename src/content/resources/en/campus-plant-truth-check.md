## What this solves

Students will soon photograph a plant and ask an AI, "What is this?" The real skill is not memorising plant names, but **observing with your own eyes first, then using that observation to check the AI's answer**.

This lesson deliberately puts observation before asking the AI: pupils first go to the school grounds (or a window) and record a plant's leaf shape, leaf edge, leaf arrangement, flowers or fruit, and where it grows, and only then look at what an AI says about a photo. In doing so they discover for themselves that **an AI's answer is second-hand information, while their own eyes are first-hand information**.

## Learning objectives

Pupils will be able to:

1. Describe a plant using five features — leaf shape, leaf edge, leaf arrangement, flowers or fruit, and where it grows — without guessing a name.
2. Judge whether an AI identification claim matches the photo or their observation.
3. Tell apart three cases: the AI is right, the AI is wrong, and the photo is too unclear to decide.
4. Explain that a plant's "name" is a claim that needs a source, and know to ask where that source is.

## Teaching steps

1. **Hook (5 min)** — Ask: "If you photographed a plant and asked an AI 'What is this?', would you trust its answer? How would you check it?" Collect answers without judging them.
2. **Outdoor observation (15 min)** — Each group chooses one plant on the school grounds (or from a window) and does just one thing: fill in the five features on the observation record sheet. Emphasise: do not guess a name, only describe what you can see.
3. **Back in class, check (10 min)** — Hand out the "plant photo cards" (three AI answers) and judge each one: does it match the photo? Is it right, wrong, or impossible to tell?
4. **Trust judgement (5 min)** — Fill in the "which source do I trust here" summary: when the AI's claim differs from what my eyes show, which do I trust first? What source would I still need to confirm a name?
5. **Wrap up (5 min)** — Together, write the prompt: observe first → then check → then ask for the source.

## Answer key and marking notes

The key points for judging the three plant photo cards:

- **Card A (right)**: the AI's description matches the photo (for example the leaves are opposite and the edges are smooth), and it honestly admits that it cannot name the species without a flower or a clear photo. A good answer = "Higher confidence: the description matches the observation, and it did not force a name."
- **Card B (wrong)**: the AI's description contradicts the photo (the photo plainly shows smooth, entire leaf edges, but the AI says "the edges are serrated and the leaves are alternate") and it gives a scientific name anyway. A good answer = "Do not trust it: the description disagrees with what the eyes see — seeing is believing — and the name has no source."
- **Card C (cannot tell)**: the photo is too blurry for any feature to be read, yet the AI still gives a confident answer. A good answer = "Cannot tell: it is not that the AI is necessarily wrong, but that the photo itself is unclear; the honest answer is 'I cannot see it clearly enough'."

**When a pupil's AI app gives a different answer** (a four-step guide for the teacher):

1. Ask the pupil to **read the AI's answer aloud**, then ask another pupil to read out the features they observed.
2. Ask the class: "Which of the two can be **checked on the spot with your eyes**?" — the observation can; the AI's name cannot.
3. Ask: "Did the AI say where its information came from?" — an answer with no source is just a claim.
4. Close with: "It is not that the AI must be wrong, nor that your eyes must be right; it is that we need a **method** to check. A name needs a source, and observation is the first step."

Core idea: **a plant's name is a claim that needs a source**, not something that can be settled from one blurry photo. Observation is first-hand information; an AI's answer is second-hand. When the two conflict, re-check your own observation first (you may have misread it), then ask whether the AI has a source.
