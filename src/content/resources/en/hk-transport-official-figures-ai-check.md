**The data on this page means official statistics published by government departments; a figure a tool supplies is only a claim until it has been checked.**

## What this solves

"How many people use public transport in Hong Kong each day?" "How long is an average journey?" "What is the split between modes of travel?" Put those three questions to a tool and it returns three tidy figures, usually with no year, no table number and no statement of scope. The three figures appear to support one another, so the whole passage goes into the project.

The second gap is the unit. Daily passenger journeys can be counted in passenger trips or in thousands of passenger trips; average journey time can be given in minutes or in hours; the split between modes can be a percentage or a daily count. Two figures in different units cannot be compared directly, and a tool rarely states the unit in full.

The third gap is scope. The same "daily passenger journeys" can mean all public transport or one mode only; it can mean the whole territory or one district; it can be local or include cross-boundary journeys. Same number, different meaning. This is the item most often let through as "the numbers are close enough".

The fourth gap is the verdict. Asked whether the account can be used, the usual answer is "it should be about right". With no verdict the checking produces no result: the figures sit side by side on the sheet and nobody says which side can be used.

This page does one thing only: four ways of recording a figure, four checks, one source check and one verdict. It does not teach tool operation, does not teach statistical method, does not calculate averages or percentages, does not draw charts, does not mark answers right or wrong, does not rank, and does not deal with the writing of the project. It never asks for tool output, screenshots or account details to be handed in, uploaded or photocopied. The page contains no real figure at all, and every example is labelled as fictional.

When a tool states a figure confidently and no source can be found for it, that is an AI hallucination; the only way to remove it is to fact-check the figure against the official statistics, item by item, and to verify each one. The same tool asked the same question can also answer differently depending on how the question is put, and that systematic leaning is where data bias shows itself. The page also reminds pupils not to drift into over-reliance: copying the figure, converting the unit and writing the verdict are all done by hand.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, pupils can:

1. Copy three figures out of the official statistics published by the Transport Department and the Census and Statistics Department: daily passenger journeys, average journey time, and the split between modes of travel.
2. Record each figure four ways: source, year, table number or page name, and unit.
3. Put the same question to the school-approved tool and carry out four checks item by item — number, unit, year, scope — as a cross-check against their own record.
4. Carry out one source check: whether the tool states a source, and whether the same figure can be found in that source.
5. Write one verdict — usable, needs fixing or unusable — with one reason that names one of the four checks.

The page addresses the strand **reflection on the application of AI** at key stage 3 (S1-S3): what is being checked is not the tool but the figures it produced, and whether the pupil went back and found them. Three values are practised. **Critical thinking** asks "which year and which scope does this figure belong to" before the figure is used. **Being led by the evidence** means writing "not found" rather than supplying a number that looks reasonable. **Timely and appropriate use** means knowing which check is worth stopping for and which can wait for the next lesson.

This page describes learning elements such as statistics and data handling in outline only, with no quotation and no official element names filled in; a school writes those names from its own curriculum document.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson. Four ways of recording, four checks, one source check and three possible verdicts; the counts are fixed and nothing is added or removed.

### 1. Three questions that can be measured (each pupil picks one)

Write one question first, and one question asks about one measure only. Choose one of the three directions and see it through; all three do not have to be done:

1. **Daily passenger journeys** — one mode of public transport, averaged over the days of a year.
2. **Average journey time** — one route or one district, averaged over weekdays.
3. **Split between modes of travel** — within one district, the share taken by each mode.

Two sentences to remember: the question has to name its object and its time range, or the figure found cannot be compared with anything; and one lesson does one set of figures from start to finish, without changing the question halfway.

### 2. Recording a figure four ways (fixed at four)

| Item | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Source | Which government department published which set of statistics — the department's full name and the name of the statistics |
| Year | The year or period the figure belongs to |
| Table number or page name | The table number or page name the figure sits in |
| Unit | The unit as officially written; pupils do not convert |

Three sentences to remember: with one of the four missing, that figure counts as unfinished; the four items exist so that another person can find the same figure by following the same four; and each of the three figures gets its own four items, never one shared row.

### 3. The four checks (fixed at four)

| Check | The question to ask | What commonly happens |
|---|---|---|
| Number | Are the two values the same? | Close but not equal, for example one side rounded |
| Unit | Are both sides in the same unit? | Passenger trips and thousands of passenger trips; minutes and hours |
| Year | Are both sides from the same year or period? | The tool gives another year, or no year at all |
| Scope | Are both sides about the same scope? | All modes and one mode; whole territory and one district; local and including cross-boundary |

Two sentences to remember: write "match" or "does not match" for each of the four, and add one sentence beside any "does not match" saying where the difference lies. Of the four, scope is the one most often let through, because the figures look the same.

### 4. One source check

Circle one of the three:

- **Source found**: the tool states a source, and the same figure can be found in it.
- **Source not found**: the tool states a source, but the same figure cannot be found in it.
- **No source**: the tool states no source at all.

Two sentences to remember: the source check is done once, written under the four checks; and "source found" is not the same as "all four match" — the four are still checked one by one.

### 5. The three verdicts and one boundary (fixed at three)

| Verdict | When it is written |
|---|---|
| Usable | All four checks match, and the source check is "source found" |
| Needs fixing | Exactly one of the four does not match and the other three do — say which one to fix; or all four match but there is no source — say that the source and table number must be added |
| Unusable | Two or more of the four do not match, or the source check is "source not found" |

Three sentences to remember: the verdict is one sentence naming the level and the reason; there is no fourth level such as "partly usable" or "roughly usable"; and the reason names one of the four checks, never a feeling.

**One boundary:** charts and conclusions use the official statistics only; the tool can help us frame the question and check the arithmetic. The three sets of figures may carry three different verdicts, and there is no rule that every set must be "unusable" — where all four checks match, write "usable" and say which four the figure matched.

### 6. The 60-minute shape, the offline version and the close

Explanation and demonstration 10 min, look up the official statistics and record the four items 18 min, ask the tool and run the four checks 10 min, the four checks and the verdict 14 min, read out the spread of the verdicts and close 8 min.

**Offline version:** the teacher prints statistics extracts and one demonstration answer from a tool beforehand, both prepared by the teacher; pupils still do the four ways, the four checks and the verdict. Neither the page nor the attachments carries a real figure in advance.

Three closing sentences:

1. Record a figure four ways and it can be compared with something.
2. Of the four checks, scope is the one most easily let through.
3. There are only three verdicts, and a reason is what makes the check finished.

Homework: take another question, for example a set of statistics from another government department, and do the exercise once more on the same sheet, writing only the four ways and one verdict.

## Answer key and marking notes

This page is not an assessment instrument: it gives no score, carries no grade, produces no percentage ranking, is not entered in a report card, and is not a conduct or honesty record. "Finished" is judged only by whether the record sheet can be written at all.

**What a finished sheet looks like:**

- Three figures, each recorded four ways, with source, year, table number or page name, and unit all filled.
- The tool's account copied as it stands, not edited to agree with the official statistics.
- "Match" or "does not match" written for each of the four checks, with a sentence beside any mismatch.
- One of the three source-check options circled.
- One verdict naming a level and a reason, and the reason names one of the four checks.

**A fictional example (to show the format, not to be used as data)**

| Item | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|
| Number | 4,321 |
| Year | 20XX |
| Table number or page name | Example Statistical Table 7 |
| Unit | Thousands of passenger trips |
| The tool's account (fictional) | About 4,300 passenger trips, with no year, table number or scope |
| Number | Does not match (4,300 and 4,321 are not the same) |
| Unit | Does not match (passenger trips and thousands of passenger trips) |
| Year | Does not match (the tool gives no year) |
| Scope | Does not match (the tool gives no scope) |
| Source check | No source |
| Verdict | Unusable: three of the four do not match and the tool states no source, so the official statistics have to be checked again. |

> Every figure, year and table number above is a **fictional example** written for this exercise. None of it represents a real statistic, a real year or a real published figure.

**A second fictional example, showing "needs fixing":** the tool writes "4,321 thousands of passenger trips, 20XX, all modes across the territory". All four checks match, but no table number and no source are given, so the verdict reads "needs fixing: the table number and the source must be added before it can be used".

**Common answers and responses:**

- **Only the source and the number are recorded.** Response: with no year or unit the figure counts as unfinished; go back to the statistics and find the year and table number for that same figure.
- **"Match" written for all four without looking.** Response: write them one at a time, and for scope say whether it is the whole territory or one district, all modes or one mode.
- **The verdict says "roughly usable".** Response: there are three levels only; choose one and give the reason.
- **The tool gave no source and the pupil still wrote "usable".** Response: with no source "usable" cannot be written; where all four match, write "needs fixing" and say the source and table number must be added.
- **The numbers were compared but not the years.** Response: two figures from different years cannot be compared directly; check the year first, then the number.

Any question may be swapped at the teacher's choice; the format does not change, and the numbers in any worked example must stay fictional.
