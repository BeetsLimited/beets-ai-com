> An answer that reads smoothly has not necessarily answered the question. Split it into one sentence per line, keep five lines, and then ask the three questions it never asked itself.

## What this solves

A pupil gets a paragraph from a tool, copies it into the homework and hands it in. What the teacher sees is fluent writing, not whether the pupil read it or thought about it. The step that actually got skipped is usually not verification but **questioning**: the assumption the answer never states, the people it never mentions, the condition that would break it — none of those were asked.

This page does one thing: **split the answer into lines, ask three questions, and record it.** It does not train pupils to write better prompts, it does not ask them to rate a tool, and it asks for no conversation to be handed over.

Splitting matters because it moves attention onto one sentence at a time. A paragraph reads smoothly partly because its sentences prop each other up; laid out one to a line, it becomes visible which sentences stand on their own and which merely follow on. Five lines is the ceiling because the object of study is the answer's frame, not its full text.

The three questions are fixed because each one does a different job: the first covers **premises**, the second covers **omissions**, the third covers **conditions**. The three columns are fixed because the record has to keep three things: which line was asked about, what was asked, and what came back. Fixed numbers keep pupils working on the questions rather than on the layout of a form.

Read it at three levels. In **literacy and framework** it sits under "human-machine communication and collaboration" and "reflection on the application of AI". In **learning and teaching** it is a one-lesson classroom routine that any subject can use and that changes no curriculum content. In **values** it exercises critical discernment: not treating a fluent sentence as true, and not stopping at an answer simply because it looks complete.

## Learning objectives

Pupils will be able to:

1. Split an AI answer into **one sentence per line**, keeping **five lines at most**, without rewriting the sentences.
2. Take one of those lines and ask the **three fixed questions**: what did it assume, who did it not mention, would it still hold if one condition changed.
3. Record the work in **three columns**: that line, my question, after asking.
4. Put **one of three outcomes** in the third column only: expanded, unchanged, or not asked.
5. Take **one question** back to the tool and write **one sentence** on how the new answer differed.
6. Understand that the sheet is not marked, judges nobody's honesty, and asks for no chat record or screenshot; the tool is always "the tool the school has specified".

The content covers the three strands of AI literacy: **how AI basically works** (an answer is a generation, not a check), **human-machine communication and collaboration** (the pupil knows which line to ask about and what to ask), and **reflection on the application of AI** (whether the answer changed, and where). In values it rests on **critical discernment**: judging whether a sentence holds up, not how well it is written.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson in five parts: 5 minutes of introduction, 10 minutes of demonstration (splitting and one question), 20 minutes of splitting and the three questions, 15 minutes of one question back to the tool and one sentence, and 10 minutes to close. **The order of the five parts does not change**; if time runs short, cut the depth of the demonstration, never the number of questions or columns, and never widen the five-line limit.

| Time | Part | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 5 min | Introduction | Hand out the sheet, read the five-line rule, the three fixed questions and the three columns once; state that no conversation is handed over, nothing is screenshotted and nothing is marked. |
| 10 min | Demonstration | With a fictional answer the teacher splits the lines, models the first question, and shows what the three third-column outcomes look like. |
| 20 min | Pupils split and ask | Pupils split the answer into five lines at most, ask the three questions of one line, and fill the columns. |
| 15 min | Back to the tool | Pupils put one question back and write, in one sentence, what differs in the new answer. |
| 10 min | Close | Three groups read out their third column; the teacher collects the record sheets only. |

### 1 — Split it: one sentence per line, five lines at most

There is one method: **break the answer at its full stops, question marks and exclamation marks, and give each sentence its own line.**

- **Five lines at most.** Count from the start of the answer and copy the first five; nothing after the fifth is copied, and nothing is summarised into a single line.
- **Copy, do not rewrite.** The pleasantries at either end ("Of course", "Hope that helps") may be dropped, but every line kept must read as the original does.
- **One sentence per line, not a paragraph per line.** If a sentence contains a semicolon or a colon, it stays on one line as it stands.
- **Fewer than five is fine.** An answer of three sentences is three lines; no line is invented to fill the sheet.

Worked example (fictional): an answer about extending the library's opening hours, split into five lines — "Extending opening hours would raise usage"; "Most pupils only have time to study in the evening"; "Longer hours need extra staff"; "Hiring staff from outside adds cost"; "The school could try it for one term first".

### 2 — The three fixed questions (three only)

The order is fixed, and **no fourth question is added, none is dropped**:

1. "What does this line assume?" — the one thing that has to be true already for the sentence to work.
2. "Who does this line not mention?" — the people, subjects, situations or departments it does not cover.
3. "If one condition changed, would it still hold?" — a different number, time, place, group size, or a different school.

The order matters because of what each question does: premise first, then omission, then conditions. Asked the other way round, pupils start by changing a condition and then treat "it no longer holds" as the answer, with the premise and the omission left unwritten.

**All three questions go to the same line.** Pick the one line out of the five you most want to pursue — usually the line the whole answer leans on — and ask all three of it, so the three rows of the record agree.

### 3 — The three-column record (three columns only)

| Column | What the pupil writes | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — That line | Copy the line being questioned, once | One sentence (transcribed) |
| 2 — My question | One of the three questions, written as a full question | One sentence |
| 3 — After asking | One of three outcomes: expanded / unchanged / not asked | One word or phrase |

The rules for the three columns:

- **Three columns, no fourth.** Anything extra is said aloud, or goes in the single line at the foot of the table.
- **Column one is a transcription.** All three rows carry the same line, which is correct rather than a copying error.
- **Column two must be a question.** "Assume?" is not one; "Does this line assume pupils are always at school in the evening?" is.
- **Column three is one phrase only.** Expanded, unchanged or not asked — one of the three.

**One line at the foot of the table: the question I would add.** Beyond the three fixed questions, a pupil may write **one line** of their own at the end of the table. One line only — it does not become a fourth question and it adds no column — and it needs no answer.

### 4 — The three outcomes for column three (three only)

Column three is not an evaluation; it is one of three plain outcomes:

| Outcome | What it means | What the pupil does next |
|---|---|---|
| Expanded | After asking, the new answer contains something the original did not | Write, in one sentence, what was added |
| Unchanged | The new answer is much the same, or only reworded | Write, in one sentence, how you asked, then stop |
| Not asked | The tool did not respond or could not answer, or there was no chance to ask (time ran out) | Write "not asked", with no explanation needed |

**"Unchanged" is a valid outcome, not a failure.** An answer that gains nothing when questioned is itself a finding: the line may be sound, or the question may not be the one that opens it up. Either is worth recording. Writing "unchanged" up as a failure, or as "the tool is no good", throws away the record this page exists to make.

**"Not asked" is a valid outcome too.** What is being recorded is that the pupil asked, not that the tool answered well.

### 5 — One question back to the tool (15 minutes)

With the three columns filled, take **one question** — any of the three, or the pupil's own line at the foot — back to **the tool the school has specified**, and then:

1. Look at how the new answer differs from the original line.
2. Write it in **one sentence**, on the "one-sentence summary" line under the table; no extra column.
3. The sentence states **where the difference is**, not that it is better or worse. For example: "After asking, the answer added a condition — longer hours need staff first — which the original did not say." Or: "After asking, the answer did not change; it only put the same line another way."

**No chat record is handed over and nothing is screenshotted.** The pupil writes one sentence of their own, and that sentence is what the teacher reads.

### 6 — The teacher's checks

1. **Read the record sheet, not the tool's screen.** Do not collect, ask for a paste of, screenshot or transcribe any conversation, and do not ask for a tool account or login detail.
2. **Mark nothing, grade nothing, rank nobody.** Check only that the answer is five lines or fewer, that all three questions are present, that each of the three columns carries one entry, that column three holds one of the three outcomes, and that the summary is one sentence.
3. **Evaluate no tool, platform or supplier.** The tool is always "the tool the school has specified"; no brand, model, supplier, region or institution appears anywhere. "Unchanged" is not a tool failing and "not asked" is not a tool being poor.
4. **Judge nobody's honesty.** The sheet records the relation between a question and an answer, not whether a pupil used a tool, and it never enters a conduct or examination record.
5. **Compare no pupil with another.** The sheet carries no name, only class and group.
6. **Keep every example fictional, and say so on the page and on the sheet.** The demonstration answer is written by the teacher, never taken from a real pupil's work.
7. **Never present any of it as a Bureau requirement.** The five lines, the three questions, the three columns, the three outcomes and the 60-minute shape are this resource's suggestion; a school may adapt them.
8. **The Chinese Language document quoted here covers the primary range only.** The passage comes from the Chinese Language Curriculum Guide (Primary 1-6); this page uses it as a cross-subject learning-and-teaching principle. A secondary class may use the same routine, but the passage must not be presented as a requirement of the secondary curriculum.

### 7 — Closing and extension (10 minutes)

Three groups read out their third column, one sentence each, as written and with no commentary:

1. "I asked what it assumed, and the answer expanded: ____."
2. "I asked who it did not mention, and the answer was unchanged."
3. "I asked whether one condition would change it, and it was not asked."

Two extensions, neither adding a question or a column:

- **Take another line.** The second line of the same answer, still three questions and three columns.
- **Do the second question only.** Take a paragraph from a textbook and ask only "who does this not mention", in one sentence.

## Answer key and marking notes

The record sheet is **not marked, not graded and not ranked**. What follows is for the teacher's demonstration and for circulating round the room; every example is fictional.

### Worked example: splitting and the three questions (fictional)

The original answer (fictional, already split into five lines):

1. Extending the library's opening hours would raise usage.
2. Most pupils only have time to study in the evening.
3. Longer hours need extra staff.
4. Hiring staff from outside adds cost.
5. The school could try it for one term first.

| Column | Example one (line 1) | Example two (line 2) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — That line | Extending the library's opening hours would raise usage. | Most pupils only have time to study in the evening. |
| 2 — My question | Does this line assume that low usage is caused by the hours being too short? | Who does this line not mention? |
| 3 — After asking | Expanded | Expanded |

Example one's summary, in one sentence: "After asking, the answer added a premise — you would first need to know why usage is low, or longer hours may change nothing — which the original did not say."

Example two's summary, in one sentence: "After asking, the answer added pupils who take the school bus home, whom the original did not mention."

The division of labour between the questions should be visible: example one asks about a **premise** (why usage is low), example two about an **omission** (which pupils). Example three, the condition question (fictional): "If one condition changed, would it still hold? Change 'in the evening' to 'before seven in the morning' and the line fails, because the school buses have not started."

### Worked examples of the three outcomes (fictional)

- **Expanded:** "After asking, the answer added one thing: first count how many pupils stay at school until the evening."
- **Unchanged:** "I asked who it did not mention; the answer only changed 'most pupils' to 'many pupils', which means the same, so it counts as unchanged."
- **Not asked:** "There was no time left, so I did not ask; I wrote the question down to ask next lesson." Writing "not asked" with no explanation is equally acceptable.

All three outcomes are valid. Seeing "unchanged" on a sheet is no reason to send a pupil back to ask again, and "not asked" needs no replacement work.

### Common wrong answers and responses

- **A whole paragraph on one line.** Response: "One sentence per line. How many sentences are on this line?"
- **More than five lines.** Response: "Keep the first five. Nothing after the fifth needs copying — the frame is what we are looking at."
- **Only two of the three questions.** Response: "One more: what did it assume, who did it not mention, would it still hold if one condition changed." Add the missing one; add nothing else.
- **Column two says "assume?" or "mention?".** Response: "Write it as a full question I can read and understand."
- **Column three says "better" or "useful".** Response: "Three outcomes: expanded, unchanged, not asked. Which one is it?"
- **"Unchanged" is written up as a failure.** Response: "Unchanged is a result, not a failure. Writing how you asked is enough."
- **A pupil is asked to hand over the chat.** Don't. This page exists to replace that practice and to collect less pupil data.

### Checklist (run through once before the lesson)

- □ The sheet states one sentence per line, five lines at most.
- □ All three questions are present, in the order what it assumed, who it did not mention, what changes if one condition does.
- □ All three columns are present: that line, my question, after asking.
- □ Column three accepts one of three outcomes only: expanded, unchanged, not asked.
- □ The foot of the table has exactly one line for a pupil's own question, not a fourth question and not a fourth column.
- □ Nothing asks for a conversation to be pasted, screenshotted or handed over, and nothing asks for an account or login detail.
- □ Nothing is marked, graded or ranked, and nothing judges honesty or originality.
- □ The sheet has no name field, only class and group.
- □ No brand, model, supplier, region or institution is named; the tool is always "the tool the school has specified".
- □ Every example is fictional, and the page and the sheet both say so.
- □ Nothing is presented as a Bureau requirement; the five lines, three questions, three columns and the 60-minute shape are this resource's suggestion.
- □ The passage quoted from the Chinese Language Curriculum Guide (Primary 1-6) is marked as covering the primary range only and as used here as a cross-subject principle.

> The point: **five lines or fewer, three questions on one line, three columns for one round, one sentence on what differed.** If column three can be filled in, the page has done its work; if it says "better", the pupil is still copying.
