> Five boxes hold five things: what I thought of first, what I asked, which step the tool supplied, whether I accepted or rejected it, and the final solution with the question I still have. A teacher reads those five boxes and needs no screen.

## What this solves

A pupil uses a tool while solving a problem, and what comes back is one answer. The teacher sees the answer and none of the steps between: which step the pupil reached alone, which step the tool supplied, and whether the pupil noticed when the tool's step was wrong. Two pupils can hand in identical answers and have thought about the problem in completely different ways.

Asking for the full working as a chat record is neither practical nor private, and a screen proves only that something was asked, not that anything was thought. This page fixes the reasoning on paper with three steps — **think first, think aloud, look back** — records it in five boxes, and then puts three follow-up questions to it: was this step yours, or the tool's?

The five boxes are the whole record for the lesson. The teacher reads the five boxes and never the tool's screen; the tool's answer is never written down as the right answer, because what the pupil writes is whether they accepted or rejected each step, and why. A maths solution has right and wrong steps, but what this page assesses is whether a pupil can name where each step came from — which is what **critical discernment** looks like in a mathematics classroom, and what **a person-centred** approach means here: a tool may do the calculating, the judgement stays with the pupil.

The page is written for S1-S6. The demands differ between junior and senior secondary, so a teacher may adjust the difficulty of the problem and the depth of the explanation, but the three steps, five boxes and three follow-up questions keep their number and their order.

## Learning objectives

Pupils will be able to:

1. Write down their **own first idea** — one sentence, or an unfinished expression, in their own words — before opening a tool.
2. Think aloud: for every step, say whether the step was theirs or the tool's, and write it in box three.
3. Write in **one sentence** which step the tool supplied, without transcribing the tool's whole solution.
4. State, for each of the tool's steps, whether they **accept or reject** it, and give the reason.
5. Write the final solution and the question that remains, and answer the three follow-up questions.

The content covers the reflection on the application of AI strand of AI literacy, and in values it rests on critical discernment and on putting the person first: critical discernment is telling "the tool calculated it correctly" apart from "I understand why", and putting the person first is keeping the judgement and the responsibility with the pupil.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson in five parts: 5 minutes of introduction, 10 minutes on the three steps, 25 minutes of thinking aloud and filling the record, 10 minutes on the three follow-up questions, and 10 minutes for the teacher's checks and the close. **The order of the five parts does not change**; if time runs short, set fewer problems, never fewer steps, boxes or questions.

| Time | Part | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 5 min | Introduction | Hand out the sheet and explain the three steps and five boxes; state that no screen is collected, no name is written, and how much a tool was used is not marked. |
| 10 min | 1 — Three steps | Model "think first" once; pupils write their first idea before filling anything else. |
| 25 min | 2 — Five boxes | Pupils solve one problem, thinking aloud, filling the boxes as they go; the teacher circulates and reads the boxes only. |
| 10 min | 3 — Three questions | Pupils put the three questions to each other in groups and write the answers under box five. |
| 10 min | 4 and 5 — Checks and close | The teacher collects the sheets and reads the five boxes; the class reads three closing sentences. |

### 1 — Three steps (three steps, fixed)

1. **Think first.** Before opening a tool, write your own first idea: one sentence, or an unfinished expression. An incomplete solution is normal here — this step asks what you meant to do.
2. **Think aloud.** For every step you take, say one sentence: was this step mine, or the tool's? Then write it in box three — **one sentence only**.
3. **Look back.** When you have finished, set your first idea beside the final solution and say, step by step, which steps you accepted, which you rejected, and why.

The three steps are an **order**, not three options: without thinking first, box three cannot tell your steps from the tool's; without looking back, box four says only "accepted".

### 2 — The five-box record (five boxes, fixed)

| Box | What the pupil writes | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — My first idea | How I meant to do it, before using a tool | One sentence, or an unfinished expression |
| 2 — What I asked | The sentence I typed, transcribed | One sentence (transcribed) |
| 3 — Which step the tool supplied | The one step the tool supplied (not its whole solution) | **One sentence only** |
| 4 — Accept or reject | Whether I accept or reject that step, and why | One sentence plus the reason |
| 5 — Final solution and question | The final solution, and one question that remains | Two sentences |

Four rules for the five boxes:

- **Five boxes, no sixth.** Anything extra goes in the space below the sheet, or is said aloud.
- **Box three is one sentence.** Transcribing the tool's whole solution means nothing has been judged; the box asks which step it supplied.
- **Box four must give the reason.** "Accepted" or "rejected" on its own is not finished; write "because I checked it by substituting back", or "because it skipped a step".
- **Box five may not be left blank.** If nothing is in doubt, write which part you checked and confirmed — that is an answer too.

### 3 — Three follow-up questions (three questions, fixed)

A classmate in the same group asks the three questions; not a supervisor, and not the tool. Read them as written and do not reword them.

| Question | How to ask it (read aloud) | What it is meant to hear |
|---|---|---|
| One | Why did you think of it that way? | The pupil's own reason, not the tool's |
| Two | Was this step yours, or the tool's? | The source of each step |
| Three | When did you notice the tool was wrong? | Whether anything was verified, and at which step |

Three rules of use:

- **The order is fixed.** Reasons first, then sources, then verification; change the order and pupils answer "I spotted it" before they can say why.
- **Not being able to answer is normal.** If a pupil cannot say when they noticed, they write "I did not notice; I saw it when I checked" — that sentence is itself the outcome of the lesson.
- **No evaluation.** A classmate asks and does not comment on whether the answer is good, and no evaluative sentence is written on the sheet.

### 4 — The teacher's checks (the five boxes, never the screen)

1. **Read the five boxes, not the tool's screen.** Do not collect, ask for, or photograph a chat record or a screenshot; no pupil has to show any screen.
2. **Do not mark how much a tool was used.** How many prompts were sent is not what is assessed; what is assessed is whether the five boxes name the source of each step.
3. **Never write the tool's answer down as the right answer.** The notes below demonstrate how to judge, not that the tool's method is correct; a pupil who rejects a step of the tool's for a sound reason has an equally good answer.
4. **Make no judgement about honesty, and compare no pupil with another.** The record carries no name, only class and group, and discussion stays at group level.

### 5 — Close and extension

The class reads three sentences aloud (as written, without commentary):

1. "I thought first, before I asked the tool."
2. "This step was mine, and this step came from the tool."
3. "I can say which steps I accepted and which I rejected, and why."

Two extensions, neither adding a box or a question:

- **A different problem, two boxes only.** Next time fill boxes three and four alone, and see whether the source of every step can be explained in five minutes.
- **Turn the same sheet on a classmate's solution.** Take one fictional solution and fill only boxes three and four — again with no name, and with no comment on the classmate.

## Answer key and marking notes

The record **does not mark how much a tool was used, and sets no grade or ranking**. One fictional problem below shows what the five boxes can look like; the numbers and the situation are invented. What the teacher reads for is not which solution is "correct" but whether the five boxes agree with each other: the step named in box three should have a matching accept-or-reject and reason in box four.

### A fictional problem

> A rectangular flower bed is 3 metres longer than it is wide, and its perimeter is 26 metres. Find its area.

### The five boxes, filled in (a fictional pupil's answers)

| Box | What the pupil wrote |
|---|---|
| 1 — My first idea | Let the width be x, so the length is x + 3; the perimeter is 2(x + x + 3) = 26, so solve for x first. |
| 2 — What I asked | "A rectangle is 3 m longer than it is wide and the perimeter is 26 m. What is the area?" |
| 3 — Which step the tool supplied | It stated x = 5 straight away, without showing the step from 2(2x + 3) = 26 to x = 5. |
| 4 — Accept or reject | I accept x = 5, because I put 5 back myself and 2(5 + 8) = 26 works; I reject its area line, because it wrote 5 × 8 = 40 without saying where the 8 came from. |
| 5 — Final solution and question | Width 5 m, length 8 m, area 40 square metres. The question I still have: if the perimeter were not a whole number, would the same expression still hold? |

What is worth noticing here is not that the arithmetic is right, but that **box three says which step the tool skipped and box four says why the step was accepted and why the other was rejected**. The question in box five is an outcome too: it links this problem to the next one.

### The three follow-up questions, answered

1. **Why did you think of it that way?** "Because the length is 3 more than the width, one unknown is enough; there is no need for two."
2. **Was this step yours, or the tool's?** "Setting x was mine; going from 2(2x + 3) to x = 5 came from the tool, and I substituted back to verify it myself."
3. **When did you notice the tool was wrong?** "When I substituted 5 back, I saw the area line never said where the 8 came from, so I wrote it in."

### Common wrong answers and responses

- **Box three transcribes the whole solution.** Response: "One sentence is enough: which step did it supply? Rub out the rest."
- **Box four says only "accepted".** Response: "Why did you accept it? Which part did you verify?" (Without a reason the box is not finished.)
- **All five boxes written by the tool.** Response: "Box three is the tool's part; boxes one, four and five are yours, and rough is fine."
- **Treating the tool's answer as the right answer.** Response: "A tool calculates quickly; that is not the same as you understanding it. Can you say which step it skipped?"
- **Asking a pupil to show the screen.** Do not. Replacing that practice is what the five boxes are for.

### Checklist (the teacher runs it once before the lesson)

- □ The sheet has five boxes only: my first idea, what I asked, which step the tool supplied, accept or reject, final solution and question.
- □ The sheet states that box three takes one sentence only.
- □ All three steps are present, in the order think first, think aloud, look back.
- □ All three follow-up questions are present, in the order why did you think of it that way, was this step yours or the tool's, when did you notice the tool was wrong.
- □ The sheet has no name field, only class and group.
- □ Nowhere does the material ask for a chat record or a screen to be handed in, uploaded or photographed.
- □ No line marks how much a tool was used, and no line presents the tool's answer as the right answer.
- □ No line makes a judgement about honesty.
- □ No line compares one pupil with another.
- □ No tool, platform, brand, model or supplier is named anywhere; the wording is always "the tool the school has specified".
- □ Every example is fictional, and the page says so.
- □ Nothing is written as an Education Bureau requirement; the three steps, five boxes, three questions and the 60-minute plan are this resource's suggestion.
- □ Where the class is S1-S3, no sentence from the Mathematics Curriculum and Assessment Guide (Secondary 4 to 6) is used as the basis for that stage.

> The core: **think before asking, name the source of every step, and give a reason for accepting or rejecting.** Fill the five boxes and no teacher needs the screen; transcribe the whole solution into box three and the lesson has not happened.
