> Three lines go on paper before the tool is opened. If the three lines cannot be written, the lesson runs without a tool — the pair ask each other instead.

## What this solves

At the moment a pupil opens the tool, the question usually has not formed yet. "Do this question for me" is not a question, it is a request. The tool receives a request, decides for the pupil what to answer and how far to go, and hands back a finished answer; the pupil copies it, hands it in, and there is no point in the process that is his own.

The three lines deal with that second: **before the tool is opened, the question, the thinking already done, and the one thing wanted from the tool are written on paper.** All three lines are short — a minute's writing. The difficulty is not the writing; it is writing first. What the pupil then holds is not a prompt but a position he can defend: he knows what he is asking, and he knows how much he has already worked out.

**Four kinds of questioning** give the act of asking four shapes: compare, ask why, ask for an example, check. One lesson uses two of them, because using all four at once turns them into four sentences to be copied rather than four different ways of pushing further. A checking question is never used alone: "is there anything wrong with this sentence" only means something once the pupil has written a sentence.

**Three situations** describe the three cases in which this page does not open a tool: the second line blank, the third line saying "everything", the first line not yet a sentence. None of them is a penalty; in all three the same sheet goes to the next pupil and the pair ask each other. The teacher does not fill in the second line — once the teacher writes it, it is no longer what the pupil had already worked out.

**The fourth line** is written after the tool has been put away: which point the tool gave that I had not thought of, and which point I disagree with or am still unsure about. Its purpose is to leave a judgement that belongs to the pupil. If the fourth line is blank, the planning for that lesson counts as unfinished and is completed in the next one.

**Three things to read first:**

1. **The names "three-line plan", "four kinds of question" and "the fourth line" are this resource's suggestion; the Education Bureau's documents do not use them.** The basis is what those documents do require — thinking and attempting first, referring to AI-generated content when it is needed, and telling when AI help is appropriate and when the work should be the pupil's own. No document prescribes the number of lines, the number of question types, or a lesson sequence.
2. **This page is not marked.** The three lines are not graded, the prompt is not graded, and nothing enters a mark book; pupils are never asked to hand in, upload or photocopy a conversation record, a screenshot or account details, and the fourth line never copies the tool's own words.
3. **All three passages quoted on this page come from the AI component of the Primary Information and Technology Curriculum Framework (pilot edition).** That framework is a primary pilot and covers Primary 1 to Primary 6 only; this page is for P4-S6, so the secondary years borrow the principle of thinking before asking and nothing here presents the framework as a secondary requirement.

This page does not say that tools may not be used, and it does not say that a pupil who uses one has not thought. It asks for one thing: **three lines on paper before the tool is opened.**

## Learning objectives

By the end, pupils can:

1. Name the three lines, in order, and say how much each line needs before it counts as written.
2. Write "what my question is" as one sentence containing an answerable target, rather than a sentence beginning "do this for me".
3. Write at least two points they have already worked out in the second line, and say "I had not thought about it" when they cannot write two.
4. Write one thing only in the third line, and ask it using one of the four kinds of question.
5. Add the fourth line after putting the tool away: one point they had not thought of, and one point they disagree with or are unsure about.

The page addresses **human-machine communication and collaboration** and **reflection on the application of AI**, and sits between thinking before asking and using technology at the right time. Three values are practised at once: **critical thinking** means not taking the tool's first answer as one's own; **responsibility** means writing down the point one disagrees with on the fourth line; **using technology at the right time** means knowing which part to ask a tool about and which part to finish alone. The three lines make **outsourcing of thinking** and **over-reliance** visible: the first happens at the moment the question forms, and the second can already be seen when the second line is blank.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson, five fixed parts: the three-line plan, the four kinds of question, the three situations, the fourth line, and closing with extension. There are three lines and no more, four kinds of question and no more, and the fourth line has two items and no more; nothing is added or dropped, and no sub-item is invented. The tool is always written as "the tool the school has designated", and no brand, model, service or organisation is named anywhere on this page.

### 1 The three-line plan (three lines, fixed order)

All three lines go on one sheet, with no blank line between them; no name is written, only class and group. All three are written before the tool is opened.

| Line | What goes on it | What does not count |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · What my question is | One sentence containing an answerable target; no "do it for me", and not two questions | Two questions; only "write this for me"; not a sentence at all |
| 2 · What I have already worked out | At least two points, in keywords, a diagram or a calculation | Blank, or only one point — that is "I had not thought about it" |
| 3 · The one thing I want the tool to do | One thing only: compare, ask why, give an example, or check | "Everything", or "finish it for me" |

- **At least two points on the second line.** Keywords, a diagram or a calculation all count; failing to write two points means "I had not thought about it", not "I could not think of anything".
- **One thing only on the third line.** "Everything" or "finish it for me" counts as unfinished: the pupil goes back to the second line, rewrites, and asks again.
- **The tool opens only after the three lines are written.** If a pupil opens it before then, the planning counts as unfinished from the fourth line onwards and is completed in the next lesson.

### 2 The four kinds of question (four, and only two per lesson)

All four sit on one card, and the card carries only the question, never an example — an example on the card gets copied, and the pupil stops looking at his own second line.

| Kind | The question, word for word | Where it is used |
|---|---|---|
| Comparing | "Is what I have done right?" | Put one point from the second line into it |
| Asking why | "Why is it like this?" | Ask for a reason, not for an answer |
| Asking for an example | "Give me a similar but different example" | Never "write it again" |
| Checking | "Is there anything wrong with this sentence?" | Only on a sentence already written, and never alone |

- **Two kinds per lesson.** The teacher names them, or each group chooses; they are not changed mid-lesson and a third is not added later.
- **A checking question is never used alone.** It goes with one of the other three, and only after the pupil has written a sentence of his own.
- **The four kinds are for asking.** They are never used to ask the tool to write a whole piece of work; asking for the whole piece is not questioning.

### 3 Three situations, and no tool this lesson

All three are visible when the three lines are collected, so one walk round the room finds them. They apply to this page's routine only: they are not a school rule, and they do not say that tools may not be used.

| Situation | What the sheet shows | What happens this lesson |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · Not thought about yet | The second line is blank, or has one point | Ask within the pair first, and add one point to the second line together |
| 2 · Asking for the work to be done | The third line says "everything" or "finish it for me" | Rewrite it as one thing: compare, why, an example, or a check |
| 3 · The question has not formed | The first line is not a sentence | Write it on the paper first and keep revising until it is one sentence |

- **No tool is not a penalty.** In all three situations the pupil works with a partner: the same sheet, one point added to the second line by each of them.
- **The teacher does not fill in the second line.** The teacher asks "which two points have you got" and does not write the answer for the pupil; once written by the teacher, the second line is no longer the pupil's.

### 4 The fourth line, after the tool is put away

The fourth line is written after the tool has been put away, not while looking at the screen. Two items, one point each, in the pupil's own words.

- **Which point the tool gave that I had not thought of.** One point, not copied from the tool.
- **Which point I disagree with, or am still unsure about.** One point; if there is genuinely no judgement yet, write "I don't know".
- **Look back at the second line afterwards.** Read the two side by side and say which point was your own.
- **A blank fourth line means the lesson's planning is unfinished.** That is not a deduction; it is unfinished work, completed on the same sheet next lesson.

### 5 Closing and extension

The 60 minutes are allocated as follows; seven segments, 60 minutes in all.

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 8 min | Demonstration: the teacher writes the three lines for a fictional question in front of the class, and shows once what happens when the third line says "everything" |
| 10 min | Lines 1 and 2: written individually; anyone who cannot find two points puts a hand up and the pair ask first |
| 8 min | Line 3: one thing only for each pupil, then swap with a neighbour and read one line |
| 10 min | The four kinds of question: each group takes two and asks once about a point from the second line, with no tool open |
| 14 min | The tool is used once; then it is put away and the fourth line is written |
| 5 min | Closing: the class reads the three sentences together (below) |
| 5 min | Explaining the extension: one sheet across four lessons, comparing only the number of points on the second line |

**Three closing sentences** (read together):

1. "My question is this sentence."
2. "I have already worked out these two points."
3. "I want the tool to do this one thing."

**Extension: one sheet across four lessons.** Lessons two to four compare only the number of points on the second line, from two up to five; in lesson five the same sheet gains one column, "the kind I did not ask this time", naming the one of the four the pupil has not used. The sheet is not replaced and the three lines are not rewritten — the order on the paper is itself the record.

## Answer key and marking notes

**One completed example (entirely fictional, to show the format)**

| Line | What is on the paper | Why it counts |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | "How far apart are the answers to this question and the last one?" | One sentence, one answerable target, no "do it for me" |
| 2 | "both numbers have to be found first" · "the difference is a subtraction" · "the last one was in centimetres" | Three points, all the pupil's own; keywords are enough |
| 3 | "Comparing: is what I have done right?" | One thing only, and it names the kind |
| 4 | "I had not thought of changing both to the same unit" · "I disagree that it has to be done that way" | Two items, one point each, in the pupil's own words |

**The three situations, and what the teacher says:**

| Situation | One sentence from the teacher | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Second line blank | "Say it to your partner first, then write it down." | If it can be said, it can be written; not being able to write it is what "not thought about yet" means |
| Third line says "everything" | "Choose one of the four, then open the tool." | "Everything" is a request to have the work done, not a question |
| First line is not a sentence | "Write it on the paper as it comes, even if it is clumsy; we will fix it together." | Before the question has formed, a tool decides the question for you |

**Common wrong answers:**

- **Two questions in the first line.** Response: one sentence holds one target; the second question waits for the next lesson.
- **The second line repeats what the tool would say.** Response: the second line is what you had worked out, not what the tool thought of. What had you got before the tool was opened?
- **"Check the whole thing for me" in the third line.** Response: one thing only, and a checking question goes on one sentence you have already written.
- **The fourth line copies the tool's sentence.** Response: the fourth line is your own sentence; a copy is the same as no fourth line.

**What counts as finished**: all three lines filled (at least two points on the second, one thing on the third), one or two of the four kinds used, and one point in each of the fourth line's two items. Finished is judged on whether it is written: it is **not marked, the prompt is not graded, and nothing enters a mark book** or a conduct record.
