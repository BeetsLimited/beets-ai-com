> The hardest step in peer critique is getting the comment to land on the sentence rather than on the person who wrote it. Point the whole class at one shared output and that step happens by itself.

## What this solves

Peer critique usually fails in class not because pupils will not speak, but because everyone is holding their own piece of work. The moment you comment on a classmate's sheet, you are commenting on the person rather than the words, so the comment turns into "yours is fine, actually" or a polite sentence that says nothing. The teacher collects a tidy set of feedback and can use none of it.

This page changes the thing being critiqued: **the whole class reads the same five to eight sentences**, prepared by the teacher and allowed to be invented. Each pupil critiques three sentences only, and each sentence is critiqued once on that sheet. One ruler across one set of sentences means the argument lands on the sentences, not on who wrote them.

There are exactly three comment types, because a sentence can fail to stand up in only three ways: it has no checkable basis; it has a basis that was never written down; or its basis holds only under one condition. So the judgement a pupil makes is not "good or bad" but "which of the three is this?". Once the judgement becomes a classification, there is no need to comment on a classmate's ability, attitude or character.

The four-column sheet narrows it once more: column two takes one type only, column three takes one kind of evidence only — one number, one example, one counter-example or one condition — plus one sentence of explanation. A comment therefore reads as a request rather than a verdict: you are not saying the sentence is wrong, you are saying what it needs before it can stand.

The last stretch is how feedback becomes revision: four-person groups argue for five minutes, the class casts one vote for the sentence that most needs evidence, and then the class revises that one sentence. The object of revision is a single sentence, not a paragraph and not the whole output — which is why it fits inside one lesson.

Read the page at three levels. At the **policy** level, Education Bureau Circular No. 156/2026 asks schools to build AI learning elements organically into every learning area, subject and cross-curricular domain; this page does that inside one lesson, using an output the teacher prepared, with no tool for pupils to open. At the **literacy and framework** level it corresponds to the "human-machine communication and collaboration" and "reflection on the application of AI" strands: pupils judge whether a machine-written sentence stands up, which is critical discernment. At the **learning and teaching** level it borrows a principle from two arts education documents — open questioning and peer exchange help pupils build knowledge, and peer assessment raises the effectiveness of collaborative learning — and puts one sentence of it on a four-column sheet. Reflection on this page is not a paragraph about feelings; it is writing down which evidence is wanted.

## Learning objectives

By the end, pupils can:

1. Choose three sentences to critique from the **same five-to-eight-sentence output**, writing each sentence **once on one sheet**, and never critiquing the whole text sentence by sentence.
2. Give each sentence one of the **three comment types** only: it holds up, it needs evidence, it does not hold if the object changes — crossing at least two types across the three sentences, and never using one type for all three.
3. When choosing "it needs evidence", name whether **a number, an example or a counter-example** is wanted, and never write "it is unclear".
4. When choosing "it does not hold if the object changes", name **which condition changed** — year group, place, time or object.
5. Fill all four columns of the critique sheet, with column four saying only agree or disagree; a disagreement carries one reason and no names.
6. Treat the sheet as something never scored, never named, never used for conduct or honesty, and never entered in a report card; the tool is always "the tool the school has specified".

The material corresponds to the "human-machine communication and collaboration" and "reflection on the application of AI" strands of AI literacy. What pupils judge is a sentence someone else produced, not one they wrote themselves — which is what reflection means here: not looking back at what I did, but asking where a sentence's basis is.

## Teaching steps

One 60-minute lesson in seven segments: 8 minutes to explain, 12 to demonstrate, 10 for practice, 5 for the group argument, 5 for the class vote, 15 to revise one sentence, 5 to close. **The order does not change**; if time runs short, cut the depth of the demonstration — never a sentence, never a type, never a column, and never merge the argument with the vote.

| Time | Segment | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 8 min | Explain | Hand out the critique sheets and the three comment-type cards, read the three types once, and state three sentences each, no names, no score. |
| 12 min | Demonstrate | Using the first two sentences of the shared output, show how one sentence can fall into different types, and how far column three has to go. |
| 10 min | Practice | Each pupil writes three rows only: three different sentences, one type and one kind of evidence each. |
| 5 min | Group argument | Three minutes for each pupil to read out the two sentences they most disagree with; two minutes for the group to argue over those sentences once, with no vote and no comment on a person. |
| 5 min | Class vote | The class casts one vote for the sentence that most needs evidence; a tie is settled by the teacher and is not re-voted. |
| 15 min | Revise one sentence | The teacher writes the chosen sentence on the board; each pupil writes one version, one line only, and the paragraph is not rewritten. |
| 5 min | Close | Three closing sentences; note which two outputs could be used later in the same term. |

### 1. One output, one ruler for the whole class

- **Five to eight sentences, one copy for the class**, prepared by the teacher. It may be invented, and the page and the sheet must say so: the content involves no real school, class, teacher or pupil.
- **Each sentence is critiqued once**: one sentence occupies one row on that sheet; a mistake is crossed out and rewritten, never added as an extra row.
- **Three sentences each**: three rows mean three different sentences — never the whole text sentence by sentence, and never all five to eight copied out.
- **No judging the tool**: not which tool is better, and no tool name is written down; everything says "the tool the school has specified".
- **The teacher numbers the sentences**, one to eight; column one reads "number plus the first four words", for example "3 A survey showed".
- **One class uses three outputs in a term**: they are changed when used, never run twice on the same output, and never handed to another class as a comparison.

### 2. Three fixed comment types (three only)

| Type | When it applies | How far to write it |
|---|---|---|
| It holds up | The sentence has a checkable basis, and you can point to where it is | Say where the basis is, for example a particular record or a particular measurement |
| It needs evidence | The sentence may be right, but no basis can be found | Say which evidence is wanted: a number, an example or a counter-example |
| It does not hold if the object changes | The sentence is true under one condition and falls over when that changes | Say which condition changed: year group, place, time or object |

No fourth type is added. The rules for the three sentences:

- **At least two types**, and never all three from one type.
- **The second type must name the evidence wanted**; "not clear enough" or "could be better" does not count.
- **The third type must name the condition that changed**; "maybe not" does not count.
- Copy the type names as they appear on the card; do not rewrite them as "right", "wrong" or "partly right".
- One sentence takes one type: if a sentence both lacks evidence and falls over when the object changes, choose the one you think needs handling first and put the other in the column-four note.

### 3. The four-column sheet (four columns only)

| Column | What goes in it | The rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The sentence | Number plus the first four words, for example "3 A survey showed" |
| 2 | Which type I chose | One type only, worded as on the card |
| 3 | Which evidence I want | One number / one example / one counter-example / one condition — one only, plus one sentence of explanation |
| 4 | A group member agrees or disagrees | Disagreement carries one reason and no name |

- **One sheet carries three sentences**, so three rows; no fourth row and no continuation sheet.
- **Disagreements go in column four**, not on a second sheet and not in the margin.
- **The sheet carries class and group only**: there is no name field and no class-number field.
- Column two takes one type and column three takes one kind of evidence: nothing becomes a long piece of self-review, and no pupil writes all four kinds at once.
- If column two says "it holds up", column three reads "one example — where the basis is"; the question is the same in every row: what does this sentence need before it can stand?

### 4. Four-person argument, five minutes, and one class vote

- **Three minutes**: each pupil reads out the **two sentences they most disagree with**, giving the number and the comment only, not the whole sheet.
- **Two minutes**: the group argues over those sentences once; no vote, no comment on a person, and no drifting onto other sentences.
- **One vote**: the class votes for one sentence only — "the sentence that most needs evidence" — by number; a tie is settled by the teacher and is not re-voted.
- **The argument is about sentences**: nobody comments on a classmate's ability, attitude or character, and nobody says "you got it wrong".
- **The teacher chairs and does not adjudicate**: it is not the teacher's job to decide which sentence is right, only to record the number the class chose, in a corner of the board.

### 5. Closing and follow-up

- **The class revises that one sentence**: the teacher writes the chosen number and the original sentence on the board, and each pupil writes one version, **one line only**, without rewriting the paragraph or the whole output.
- **Three closing sentences**: which sentence did not stand up, which evidence was missing, and what the sentence becomes once that evidence is added.
- **The follow-up is one move only**: add the evidence to the sentence from the previous lesson; no new output, no new sentence and no new column.
- Three outputs a term, one revised sentence each: nothing beyond those three sentences changes, and the rest waits for next time.

## Answer key and marking notes

This page is **not scored, not levelled and not ranked**, and it makes no judgement about honesty or copying, so there is no answer key — only the difference between a classification that holds and evidence specific enough to act on. Every example below is invented and involves no real school, class, teacher or pupil.

### A shared output (six sentences, invented)

1. Library borrowing rose last month compared with the month before, because pupils' reading habits have improved.
2. Borrowing rose, so the library should stay open for longer.
3. A survey showed that some pupils find the reading reward scheme helpful.
4. The reading reward scheme works best when it is run with the junior year groups.
5. The library added fifty new books last month, and the number is recorded in the purchase record.
6. Pupils do not have enough reading time, and this is a problem many schools share.

| Sentence | The type that most needs handling | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | It needs evidence | It attributes the rise to habits, but a number or a counter-example is needed: what else changed in the same month |
| 2 | It does not hold if the object changes | If the library is already open all day, or the class is very small, the step does not follow |
| 3 | It needs evidence | The survey gives no sample size and no count of who agreed |
| 4 | It does not hold if the object changes | Change the year group, or the time it was run, and it may not hold |
| 5 | It holds up | It has a checkable basis and can point to where: the purchase record |
| 6 | It does not hold if the object changes | Change the school or the year group and the sentence is no longer safe |

Only one of the six stands up. For the other five, what is missing is not better writing but one specific thing — which is the distinction this page exists to make visible.

### A completed sheet (three rows, invented)

| Sentence | Which type I chose | Which evidence I want | A group member agrees or disagrees |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 A survey showed | It needs evidence | One number — how many pupils answered, and how many agreed | Agrees |
| 2 Borrowing rose | It does not hold if the object changes | One condition — whether the library is already open all day | Disagrees: the group member thinks the year group is what changes, not the opening hours |
| 5 The library added | It holds up | One example — where the basis is: the purchase record | Agrees |

The three rows cross two types, each row names one kind of evidence, and column four carries no name — that is a sheet that passes. Passing does not mean "well written": it means the classification and the evidence can both be pointed at.

### Common wrong turns and what to say

- **Critiquing all five to eight sentences.** Say: "Three sentences each. Which three do you most want to talk about?"
- **All three sentences from one type.** Say: "At least two types. Could your second sentence be 'it does not hold if the object changes'?"
- **Column three says "not clear enough".** Say: "Name it: a number, an example or a counter-example."
- **The third type says only "maybe not".** Say: "Which condition changed — year group, place, time or object?"
- **Column four says "you wrote the same".** Say: "Column four is agree or disagree; a disagreement gives one reason and no names."
- **Asking pupils to hand over a chat record.** Do not: this page uses the teacher's output only, and collects no draft, no chat record and no screenshot.

### Checklist (run through once before the lesson)

- □ The output is five to eight sentences, one copy for the class, numbered one to eight.
- □ The page and the sheet say the output is invented, involving no real school, class, teacher or pupil.
- □ All three comment types are present, and only three: it holds up, it needs evidence, it does not hold if the object changes.
- □ All four columns are present, and only four: the sentence, which type I chose, which evidence I want, a group member agrees or disagrees.
- □ Three sentences each, and each sentence appears once on a sheet.
- □ Column two takes one type and column three one kind of evidence, with nothing written as a long self-review.
- □ Nothing comments on a classmate's ability, attitude or character, and no name appears anywhere.
- □ Nothing is scored, levelled or ranked, nothing judges honesty or copying, and nothing enters a report card.
- □ Nothing asks for a chat record, a screenshot, account details or a pupil's own draft to be handed over, uploaded or photocopied.
- □ No brand, model, supplier, institution, country or city is named; the tool is always "the tool the school has specified".
- □ The sheet carries class and group only, with no name field.
- □ Nothing is written as a Bureau requirement, and nothing turns this into peer comment on character or attitude.

> The core is **one output, three sentences, three types, four columns, one vote, one sentence.** Put the whole class in front of the same sentences and the comments land on the sentences; add the one piece of evidence and the sentence stands.
