## What this solves

The hardest thing in senior-secondary history is often not whether pupils use AI, but that the AI's answer comes back as a **fluent paragraph with no provenance**. Pupils cannot tell which sentence is backed by evidence and which only sounds right; reading through a chat history is slow, and it does not show whether the pupil actually made a judgement.

This workshop makes provenance something pupils can see and write down: three linked tasks take one AI history answer and **split the claim, trace the evidence, then rewrite it into a version the pupil can stand behind**. The teacher can then see where each pupil's thinking stops — whether they cannot separate fact from interpretation, do not know where to look for a source, or dare not cut a claim that has no trail.

The stimulus uses a famous, textbook-level historical episode, and is deliberately written as a "can this claim stand" judgement rather than a task that asks pupils to verify a date or figure no one can check. Every school and example is fictional.

The workshop can sit inside a school's digital education planning and be run in a senior-secondary history lesson, assessed through process evidence rather than learning data alone; the fluent but unsourced answer it works on is exactly the shape an AI hallucination takes, and pupils learn to verify each claim and fact-check it against a source.

## Learning objectives

By the end, pupils can:

1. Split an AI history statement sentence by sentence into "fact claim" and "interpretation", and mark every place a source is missing.
2. For two claims they judge traceable, say what kind of primary or secondary source would settle each one and where in Hong Kong they would look for it (for example the school library, Hong Kong Public Libraries, the Government Records Service, or a museum archive).
3. Rewrite the AI paragraph into a version they can stand behind, deleting or clearly hedging every claim with no trail.

It targets the reflection on the application of AI strand of AI literacy and builds critical thinking about Technology for Good.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson, in four segments.

1. **Introduction, ground rules and splitting the claim (15 min)** — Set the goal: "We are not judging whether AI should be used; we are learning to trace where a claim comes from." Agree the ground rule: these tasks do not grade whether a pupil used AI. Hand out the sheet and the evidence-trail record. Pupils read a short AI-generated history statement (fictional) and label each sentence "fact claim" or "interpretation", marking every place a source is missing.
2. **Find the trail (20 min)** — Pupils choose two claims they judge traceable and write what kind of source would settle each one and where in Hong Kong they would look. The teacher runs through what each kind of archive or library is for, without naming a specific document.
3. **Defend or drop it (18 min)** — Pupils rewrite the AI paragraph into a version they can stand behind: keep what has a trail, delete or hedge what does not, and account for each item on the record.
4. **Wrap-up and follow-up (7 min)** — Choose one follow-up activity (below) and collect the record as evidence for what to teach next.

## Answer key and marking notes

All three tasks are open-ended, but good answers share checkable features. The examples below are fictional; teachers can swap in a famous episode from their own course.

**Stimulus (a fictional AI answer)**

> Hong Kong grew from a small fishing village into an important trading port (entrepot) during the nineteenth century. This change happened mainly because the British brought a policy of free trade, so Hong Kong's economy took off at once. Most historians agree that Hong Kong's prosperity was caused by the advantage of its location. We can therefore say that, without British rule, Hong Kong would have remained a small fishing village for ever.

**The evidence-trail record (for pupils)**

Columns: claim | fact or interpretation | source that would settle it | kept / hedged / dropped.

**Worked example (completed)**

| Claim | Fact or interpretation | Source that would settle it | Kept / hedged / dropped |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hong Kong grew from a fishing village into a trading port in the nineteenth century | Fact claim (checkable) | Primary: port and trade records in the Government Records Service; secondary: a Hong Kong history book | Keep (but note the time span and the source) |
| The change happened mainly because the British brought free trade | Interpretation (a causal claim with no source) | Sources that show the link between policy and economic change, such as trade statistics and government records | Hedge (rewrite as "some scholars argue...", and note the direct evidence is still missing) |
| Most historians agree prosperity was caused by location | Interpretation (a claimed consensus with no source) | The works of several historians, to check whether this consensus really exists | Drop or hedge (no historian is named) |
| Without British rule Hong Kong would have stayed a fishing village for ever | Interpretation (a counterfactual that cannot be tested) | No source can settle "for ever" | Drop |

**Marking guide**

### Task 1: Split the claim

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Strong | Separates "fact claim" from "interpretation" sentence by sentence, and correctly flags the causal sentences ("because", "so", "therefore") and the unsourced consensus line ("most historians agree"). |
| Developing | Spots the obvious causal sentences, but also treats a checkable fact like "grew from a fishing village into a trading port" as interpretation, or misses the counterfactual "for ever". |
| Needs help | Treats the whole paragraph as fact or the whole paragraph as interpretation, without going sentence by sentence. |

### Task 2: Find the trail

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Strong | For each claim says what kind of source (primary or secondary) would settle it, and names one place in Hong Kong where they would look (school library, Hong Kong Public Libraries, the Government Records Service, a museum archive), without inventing a specific document. |
| Developing | Can name a place but not the kind of source, or writes only "search online". |
| Needs help | Cannot connect the claim to any kind of source or place. |

### Task 3: Defend or drop it

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Strong | Every sentence in the rewrite has a source or is clearly hedged, the counterfactual and the unsourced consensus are cut, and the pupil can say why each item was kept, hedged or dropped. |
| Developing | Cuts the most obvious counterfactual but keeps a line like "most historians agree" without hedging it. |
| Needs help | Copies the paragraph almost unchanged, or deletes the whole thing without explaining the judgement. |

**What not to grade**

**Do not grade whether a pupil used AI.** This workshop scores evidence work — splitting a claim, saying what kind of source would settle it, cutting or hedging what has no trail — not the tool a pupil used. Using AI is not a fault here; the point is to see where the pupil's thinking stops and what to teach next.

**Follow-up**

- Have pupils run their last history essay's concluding paragraph through the same record, sentence by sentence.
- Turn the class's list of "where in Hong Kong to look" into a poster of which kind of source to seek and where.
