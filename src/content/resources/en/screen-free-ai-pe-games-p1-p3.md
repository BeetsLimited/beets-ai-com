## What this solves

Young pupils often treat "AI" as meaning "anything electronic" or "any machine", and they do not know that AI learns from examples. If a wrong idea is fixed at the start, it is harder later to tell the limits and risks of AI apart.

This resource uses **two screen-free movement games** so that P1–P3 pupils "run out" two key ideas in a hall or playground: first, AI is not every machine — it is tools that "decide for themselves"; second, AI learns from examples (data), so if the examples are wrong, the AI learns the wrong thing.

No device or screen is needed at any point — only ordinary PE kit — so it works in classrooms without tablets or a network, and in classes with a wide range of learning needs.

## Learning objectives

By the end, pupils can:

1. Say how "AI" differs from an ordinary tool: not everything electronic uses AI.
2. Sort everyday examples into "uses AI" and "does not use AI".
3. Act out "learning from examples": AI learns to judge from examples (data).
4. Say that a wrong example makes the AI learn wrongly ("bad data in, bad answer out").

## Teaching steps

(The timings below add up to one lesson of about 35 minutes; Games 1 and 2 can be swapped or shortened depending on the class.)

1. **Warm-up and introduction (5 min)** — Ask: "What things can 'decide for themselves'?" (for example an automatic door, a voice assistant). Collect answers without judging.
2. **Game 1 — "AI or not AI?" (12 min)** — See the teacher instructions below. Pupils run to the labelled cone to sort each card.
3. **Game 2 — "Train the model" (12 min)** — The whole class is the "training data"; one pupil is the "model". Watch how one wrong example makes the model go wrong.
4. **Tidy-up and the two-minute check (6 min)** — Use the check questions in "Answer key and marking notes" to confirm the two key ideas.

### Game 1 — "AI or not AI?"

**Equipment:** two cones, one with a large "Uses AI" symbol and one with a large "Does not use AI" symbol, placed at opposite ends of the space; a set of picture cards (see the printable).

**How to play:**

1. The teacher holds up a card (for example "robot vacuum") and the whole class thinks for a second.
2. The teacher calls "Go!", and pupils run to the cone they think is correct.
3. The teacher reveals the answer, asks anyone who is wrong to run to the correct side, and gives a one-sentence reason (for example "A robot vacuum avoids obstacles by itself, so it uses AI").
4. Repeat with 6–8 cards.

**Teacher instructions:**

- Start with obvious cards (pencil, traffic cone vs robot vacuum, voice assistant), then bring in cards that are electronic but do NOT use AI (a plain light switch, a camera with no automatic function). This is the key distinction.
- Give only one sentence of explanation per card; no technical detail.

### Game 2 — "Train the model"

**Equipment:** two floor mats or hoops as answer zones ("Animal" and "Not an animal"); a set of animal / not-animal picture cards (see the printable).

**How to play (four steps):**

1. **Teach (about 4 min)** — One pupil is the "model", standing with their back to the class (or with eyes closed). The teacher shows cards one by one (cat, dog, fish → animal; ball, car → not animal); the whole class says the correct label out loud so the "model" hears it. Repeat 4–5 cards, all correct.
2. **Test (about 2 min)** — The teacher shows a new card the model has not seen (for example "bird"). The class stays silent; the model judges "animal / not animal" and steps into the matching zone. Usually correct.
3. **Add one wrong example (about 2 min)** — The teacher quietly shows a "ball" card but signals the class to say the label "animal" on purpose. Test a new ball card again — the model now judges it as "animal".
4. **Discuss (about 4 min)** — Ask: "Why did the model get it wrong just now?" Guide pupils to see that one wrong example mixed into the teaching made the AI learn the wrong thing.

### Inclusion notes

- **Pupils who cannot run or move far:** they can point, hold up a coloured card, or be paired so a friend runs on their behalf; shorten the distance between the cones.
- **Pupils who need visual support:** every card carries a large picture plus a word; use large, clear symbols on the cones; say each card aloud as you show it.
- **Pupils who are deaf or hard of hearing, or who have a speech difficulty:** model every action; allow answers by action or sign rather than speech.
- **Pupils who need support with attention:** use fewer cards per round and shorter distances.

## Answer key and marking notes

### Game 1 — card sorting (answers)

| Card | Group | One-sentence reason |
|---|---|---|
| Pencil | Does not use AI | It does not decide for itself |
| Traffic cone | Does not use AI | It is just an object |
| Traffic light | Does not use AI | It changes on a fixed timer |
| Light switch | Does not use AI | It is only an on/off switch |
| Robot vacuum | Uses AI | It avoids obstacles by itself |
| Voice assistant | Uses AI | It understands speech and answers |
| Camera autofocus | Uses AI | It finds the face or subject by itself |
| Step-counting watch | Uses AI | It tells walking from running by itself |

> The key distinction: **not everything electronic "uses AI"**. Only tools that decide for themselves count.

### Game 2 — train the model (answers)

- After the correct "teaching", the model can judge a new, unseen card correctly, because it learned the features of "animal" from the examples.
- After one wrong example is added, the model judges a new "ball" as "animal". This shows: **if the training data has a mistake, the model learns the mistake** ("bad data in, bad answer out").
- A one-line chant the class can remember: "Right examples, right AI; wrong examples, wrong AI."

### Two-minute understanding check (hands up or thumbs up/down)

1. Does a pencil use AI? — **No.**
2. Does a robot vacuum use AI? — **Yes** (it avoids obstacles by itself).
3. If I mix one wrong example into the "teaching", what does the model do? — **It learns wrong / gets it wrong.**

> Pupils should get at least questions 2 and 3 right to show they hold the two core ideas of the lesson.
