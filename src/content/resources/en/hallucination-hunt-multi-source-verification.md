> A confident sentence is not evidence. Four steps, six columns, four forms: cut one sentence, open the sources, compare them, then judge the claim and rewrite it into a sentence you are willing to sign.

## What this solves

An AI tool is most convincing exactly when it writes most confidently: a complete-looking answer, a few source names that look real, and a figure with one decimal place in the middle. The problem is not that the tool is "unreliable". It is that **a confident sentence is not evidence** — it may be right, or it may only read as if it were. This page does not teach pupils to distrust everything. It teaches one routine they can repeat: trace a sentence back to its sources, and only then decide whether to use it.

That is also why asking again is not checking. Put the same prompt in three times and you get three differently worded answers saying much the same thing, which shows the tool is consistent, not that it is correct. Holding those three answers up against each other still leaves you with one source.

**The official documents ask for multi-source checking and critical discernment**, and warn against outsourcing thinking; they prescribe no checking table and no number of sources. The four steps, six columns and four forms here are one repeatable routine this resource has put together. They are a suggestion, not a requirement.

This page serves teaching and learning, not a test of information literacy. Assessment does not turn on whether pupils can name the four forms; it turns on column six, the sentence they hand in, and whether that sentence is genuinely more signable than the original. A teacher can see this in column three (was the source actually opened) and in column six (is the rewrite more careful), and classroom observation shows whether a group stopped to open a link at step two. Feedback names one thing to change, so that the next hunt really happens. The step is also values work: critical discernment is refusing to treat a confident sentence as a fact; responsibility is checking before the sentence reaches a classmate or a teacher; and using AI when it fits means not turning this into a formality where checking adds nothing.

**Three boundaries, please read them together.** First, the name "tracking sheet", the six columns and the order of the four steps are a suggestion from this resource, not an Education Bureau requirement. Second, this page teaches one repeatable in-school practice; it does not evaluate any tool or supplier, and it does not argue that pupils should therefore stop using AI. The conclusion is one line throughout: until it has been checked, do not use it as a fact. Third, every example here is fictional and printed to show the format only. The page supplies no ready-made questions, no fake sources and no fake web addresses; the teacher prepares one or two sentences that can be checked on the day.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, pupils will be able to:

1. Copy one sentence containing a single claim out of an answer.
2. Ask the tool for its sources, open them, and record the domain, the title and the posting date.
3. Decide whether the sources they hold are independent of one another, and say which of them is a first-hand official source.
4. Choose one of the four judgements, and rewrite the original sentence into one they are willing to sign.

## How to use it

The order of the four steps is the order of the columns. One page, six columns, prints on its own; the count and the order are fixed, with nothing added and no sub-items.

**Step 1 — cut one sentence.** Copy out of the answer one sentence that carries a single claim. A sentence carrying two claims (for example, "most pupils in a certain city use these tools daily and their results have fallen as a result") becomes two rows, hunted separately. Copy it word for word: rewrite it and you are no longer checking what the tool said, only your own version of it.

**Step 2 — find the sources.** Ask the tool for its sources first, then **open every one of them** and write three things in column three: the domain, the title, the posting date. A source you cannot open is not a source; a page you can open that does not say that sentence leaves it unchecked. This line is printed on the sheet itself: **the link opens but the page does not say it = not verified**.

**Step 3 — weigh more than one independent source.** Three independent sources, at least one of them first-hand and official. Sources that quote the same original text count as one: three reports all relaying the same press release are one source, not three. **A school may adjust this to two, decided by the teacher before the lesson.** That is a suggestion from this resource, not an Education Bureau requirement, and neither the page nor the sheet may present it as an official rule.

**Step 4 — judge, then rewrite.** Pick one of four: verified, partly correct, cannot be verified, wrong. Then rewrite the original sentence into one you can sign — keep the part you could check, drop the part you could not, add no new figures, and do not replace checking with "it is said".

**The six-column tracking sheet** (column names and order fixed)

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| 1 · The sentence | One sentence from the answer, one claim only, copied word for word |
| 2 · The sources it gave | Names and locations, copied as given, without judging whether they are real |
| 3 · Opened | Domain / title / posting date; write "could not open" when it does not open |
| 4 · Independent sources | How many independent sources; one is first-hand and official [ ] |
| 5 · Judgement | Verified / partly correct / cannot be verified / wrong — one of four |
| 6 · The rewritten sentence | One sentence you can sign; this is what the pupil hands in |

**One example row** (entirely fictional, printed to show the format)

| 1 · The sentence | 2 · The sources it gave | 3 · Opened | 4 · Independent sources | 5 · Judgement | 6 · The rewritten sentence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Last year 62% of secondary pupils in a certain city used AI tools for more than two hours a day." | Two web addresses and the name of an organisation (fictional) | The first opens, but the page only mentions "using AI tools" and carries no percentage; the second does not open | One independent source; no first-hand official source found | Cannot be verified | "Some reporting mentions secondary pupils in a certain city using AI tools, but this page found no first-hand official data for the 62% or the two hours, so neither figure is used here." |

**The four forms of hallucination** (the form and how to spot it, with no fake citation to copy)

| Form | How to spot it |
|---|---|
| A made-up reference | The source looks specific - an organisation, a year, page numbers - but following it up finds no such document, or finds the document without that sentence in it |
| Number drift | The figure changes as it is passed on: a different sample, a different year, a different unit, or a percentage taken against a different base |
| "Usually" written as "always" | A tendency with exceptions is written as a rule without them: "most" and "often" come back as "always", "all", "inevitably" |
| People and dates mixed up | People, posts, events or sequences from different periods are run together; writing the timeline out and checking each year and each post makes it visible |

**One 60-minute lesson, four parts**

| Part | Minutes | What happens | What it produces |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demonstration | 10 | The teacher works the four steps on a sentence prepared in advance; at step three, two sources are opened on the projector | One worked sheet, kept for reference |
| Group hunt | 25 | One sentence per group, four roles: copy the sentence, open the sources, keep column three, prepare the rewrite | One completed six-column sheet per group |
| Peer review | 15 | Two groups swap and check only columns three and six: were the sources actually opened, is the rewrite more careful than the original | One specific suggestion, written on the sheet |
| Close and homework | 10 | The four judgements are collected, and the one boundary: cannot be verified is not the same as wrong; homework is to hunt one sentence from a homework task | One line of homework instruction |

**The offline version.** Where the room has no internet, the teacher downloads two sources in advance (one first-hand official, one second-hand) and step two becomes checking the domain, the title and the posting date on paper. Step three uses two sources, as this page already allows at school level. The four steps and the six columns do not change.

## Answer key and marking notes

**The four steps: what right looks like, and the usual wrong answer**

| Step | What right looks like | The usual wrong answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · Cut one sentence | One sentence, one claim, copied word for word | Pasting the whole paragraph into column one, or rewriting it so the object of the hunt becomes the pupil's own version |
| 2 · Find the sources | Every source genuinely opened, with domain, title and date | Copying the names the tool listed without opening them, or treating a home page as a source |
| 3 · Weigh the sources | Naming which of them share one origin, and marking the first-hand official one | Counting three reports of the same press release as three sources |
| 4 · Judge | One of four, plus a rewritten sentence that can be signed | Hiding behind "partly correct", or using "cannot be verified" in place of "wrong" |

**Telling the four judgements apart**

| Judgement | What it means | The usual misjudgement |
|---|---|---|
| Verified | A first-hand official source says exactly this | Writing "verified" after seeing it only in a second-hand report |
| Partly correct | A source exists, but the figure, the scope, the date or the subject differs from it | Copying the drifting figure and adding "about" in front of it |
| Cannot be verified | No openable source, or a page that opens without saying it | Recording it as "wrong" |
| Wrong | A first-hand source says the opposite | Calling it "wrong" because no source could be found |

**Cannot be verified is not the same as wrong.** A claim may be true or false and simply not checkable today. Calling it wrong takes contrary evidence; without that evidence, record "cannot be verified" and do not use it as a fact. Say this out loud during peer review, because merging the two judgements is the commonest slip.

**What a good rewritten sentence looks like**

- One sentence, not a paragraph.
- It keeps the part that could be checked and drops the part that could not.
- It adds no new figure, year or organisation name.
- It does not replace checking with "it is said", "some people think" or "it is generally believed" - that hands the judgement back to somebody with no name.
- It can be signed: you are willing to put your name after it.

**Common errors**

- **Treating repeated asking as several sources.** The same prompt three times is still one source.
- **Treating the home page as the source.** Open the page that carries the sentence; a home page that opens proves nothing about its content.
- **Writing "cannot be verified" as "wrong".** The two need different evidence, and the slip teaches pupils to reach for a heavier word instead of checking.
- **Quietly loosening the claim on the rewrite.** The original says 62%; the rewrite says "more than half"; the figure is still unsourced.
- **Hunting only the most suspicious sentence.** The suspicious one usually yields a finding; the one that slips through is the one that sounded most reasonable.

**What counts as meeting the standard here**: all six columns filled (column three may read "could not open"), one of the four judgements in column five, and one signable sentence in column six. The sheet is a record of an operation, not something marked with a score; peer review reads columns three and six only.
