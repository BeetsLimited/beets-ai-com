## What this solves

Every pupil already owns a story worth writing — a grandparent's move to Hong Kong, a family trade, a neighbourhood that changed. But the gap between the spoken account and a written paragraph is exactly where P4-P6 writers stall: the words came out of a real person's mouth, and the page is supposed to be the pupil's own.

This unit turns that gap into a staged writing task and gives AI a deliberately narrow, honest role:

1. **Prepare** — each pupil chooses one family or community story and writes their own interview questions (the sheet carries a question bank plus a rule: ask about a time, a place and a change, not facts you can look up).
2. **Record** — pupils take notes or record a short clip WITH the family member's spoken permission. The recording stays with the family or pupil; it is never uploaded to any AI tool.
3. **Draft** — pupils write the story in the first person, in their own words. AI may be used for three named jobs only: suggesting an opening line, rephrasing a sentence that sounds like a transcript, and checking that a paragraph keeps a consistent tense. AI may not add an event, place, name or detail the family member did not say — the class writes that rule together.
4. **Compare voices** — pupils compare how the same event sounds in the family member's spoken words and in their own written version, and note in one sentence what changed and why. This is the heart of the resource: writing is translation, not transcription.

The unit can sit inside a school's digital education planning and run in a Chinese Language lesson, with a school-based agreement on what AI may and may not do: the AI helps with three named jobs only, working from the prompt the pupil writes, and the pupil reflects on which parts of the story are their own voice.

## Learning objectives

By the end, pupils can:

1. Write their own interview questions using the rule "ask about a time, a place and a change", not facts they could look up.
2. Record a spoken story (notes or a short clip) with the person's spoken permission, and explain why the recording must not be uploaded to any AI tool.
3. Write the story in the first person in their own words, marking which sentences came from the person and which are their own.
4. Tell the three jobs AI may do apart from the things it must never do (adding a detail the person did not say).
5. Compare the same event in the person's spoken words and in their own written version, and say in one sentence what changed and why.

Written for the upper-primary learning stage, it covers the reflection on the application of AI strand of AI literacy, building empathy, responsibility and critical thinking.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson, in four segments.

1. **Choosing a story (10 min)** — Open with "everyone is carrying a story worth writing." Pupils pick an interview subject from a family member, a neighbour, a school staff member, an object with a story, or a place in the neighbourhood; it does not have to be a relative.
2. **Set the boundary and write the questions (20 min)** — The class writes three rules together: the recording and the person's details are never uploaded to any AI tool; AI does only the three named jobs; AI may not add a detail the person did not say. Using the question bank and the "time, place, change" rule, each pupil writes three to five questions and gets the person's spoken permission.
3. **Share, record and draft (20 min)** — Pupils share what they gathered (notes or a short clip; the clip stays with the family or pupil and is not uploaded). The teacher reminds them: the recording is for writing, not for uploading. Pupils write the story in the first person in their own words; they may use AI for one of the three named jobs, and mark each sentence "from the person" or "mine".
4. **Compare voices and wrap up (10 min)** — Pupils write one sentence on how the person's spoken words differ from their own written version, and why; then they give one response using the peer-response frame.

## Answer key and marking notes

Most items are open-ended, but good answers share checkable features. All examples below are fictional.

### Interview questions

A good question asks for a story, not a fact you can look up.

| Level | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | The question asks about a time, a place and a change — for example "When did you first come to Hong Kong?" and "How did that street change later?" — rather than "How old are you?" or "Which district do you live in?" (the latter can be looked up and rarely lead to a story). |
| **Developing** | Asks about a time or a place, but not a change, or leans on facts. |
| **Needs help** | Only asks for look-up-able facts (name, age, address) and draws out no story. |

### The AI-use boundary checklist (correct answers)

"AI may do" holds exactly three items:

1. Suggest an opening line.
2. Suggest how to rephrase a sentence that sounds like a transcript.
3. Check that a paragraph keeps a consistent tense.

"AI may not do":

- Add an event, place, name or detail the person did not say.
- Have the person's recording or personal details uploaded.
- Write the whole story for the pupil.
- Change the meaning of what the person said.

### Drafting marks

A good draft labels each sentence: which are "from the person" (facts and details) and which are "mine" (the retelling and the links between them). If a "mine" sentence was rephrased by AI, that should be marked honestly too.

### Comparing voices

A good one-sentence answer says what changed (spoken register to written register, first person to reported speech, links added), why (writing is translation, not transcription), and what was kept (the person's details and meaning).

**Fictional example:** the person says "back then we lived on the rooftop, and in summer it was so hot we could not sleep"; the pupil writes "Grandmother remembers that the place she lived in as a child was so hot in summer that she often could not sleep." What changed is the register (spoken to written) and the person (the "Grandmother remembers" frame was added); what did not change is that the facts (the rooftop, the summer heat) still come from the person. Note: neither way of speaking is "wrong" — spoken and written language are two registers, and writing translates the spoken into the written.

### Marking guide

| Skill | Strong | Developing | Needs help |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interview questions | Asks about a time, a place and a change, with open questions that draw out a story | Asks about a time or a place, but not a change | Only asks for look-up-able facts |
| Privacy and boundary | Can say the recording is not uploaded, AI does three jobs only, and no detail the person did not say is added | Can name some boundaries, but misses some | Cannot tell what AI may do from what it may not |
| First-person writing | Complete paragraph, own words, clearly marked "from the person / mine" | Paragraph mostly complete, but marking incomplete | Copies the spoken words with no writing of their own |
| Comparing voices | Says what changed, why, and what was kept | Says something changed, but not why | Sees no difference, or treats "writing it down" as writing |
