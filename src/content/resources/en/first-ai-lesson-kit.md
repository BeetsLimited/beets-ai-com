## What this solves

Many primary teachers (especially non-ICT subject teachers who also support learning diversity) want to use AI with their class once, but do not know where to start: they worry the tool will fail, the class will get out of hand, or pupils with learning needs will fall behind. This kit needs **no trainer present** — a teacher can run the full 40-minute lesson just by following it.

The kit uses a **Humanities (General Studies) "Hong Kong transport"** demonstration topic. It was chosen because the answers are concrete and checkable in everyday life, so pupils can judge an AI answer using common knowledge or a single source card — the safest kind of first AI lesson. The "ask, then check, then trust" method transfers to any subject.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, pupils can:

1. State the three steps "ask, then check, then trust": ask the AI a clear question, check the answer, then decide whether to believe it.
2. Use a source card to check an AI answer and find at least one "right" and one "wrong / not sure" item.
3. Know that when unsure they should "ask the teacher" or "check a second source", rather than copying the AI.

After completing the kit, the teacher can:

1. Use the checklist to prepare, including a backup plan for when the AI tool is unavailable on the day.
2. Teach slide by slide from the outline, without designing the lesson from scratch.

## Teaching steps

1. **Introduction (5 min)** — Ask: "Have you heard of AI? What has it done for you?" Collect answers without judging. Make the point: "AI can be wrong too, so we check first, then trust."
2. **Demonstration (10 min)** — Project Example 1 (the AI says "Sha Tin has trams"). Read the answer together, then model the check card: circle the AI's claims → compare with the source card → mark ✓ / ✗ / ?, and find the mistake.
3. **Pair work (15 min)** — In pairs, pupils use Example 2 on the worksheet and source card B to complete the check card. Circulate, supporting the pairs that need help.
4. **Sharing and wrap-up (10 min)** — Two pairs share the mistake they found. The teacher restates the three-step prompt "ask, then check, then trust", and asks: "What do we do when we are not sure?" (Answer: ask the teacher / check a second source.)

## Answer key and marking notes

**Example 1 (trams)**

- The AI's claims: Hong Kong trams pass Central, Causeway Bay and Sha Tin.
- Source card: trams run only along the northern shore of Hong Kong Island (Kennedy Town to Shau Kei Wan); Sha Tin is in the New Territories and has no trams.
- Right: Central, Causeway Bay. Wrong: Sha Tin.
- A good answer: says "what is wrong" plus "how to fix it" — replace "Sha Tin" with a Hong Kong Island location (for example North Point).

**Example 2 (harbour ferry)**

- The AI's claims: the harbour ferry connects Hong Kong Island and Kowloon, for example Central to Cheung Chau.
- Source card: harbour ferries connect Hong Kong Island and Kowloon (for example Central to Tsim Sha Tsui); Central to Cheung Chau is an outlying-island ferry.
- Right: harbour ferries connect Hong Kong Island and Kowloon. Wrong: Central to Cheung Chau is not a harbour ferry, it is an outlying-island ferry.
- A good answer: point out that "Central to Cheung Chau" is not a harbour ferry, and change it to "Central to Tsim Sha Tsui".

**Marking notes (how the teacher judges whether a pupil has met the goal)**

- Can they circle at least one claim the AI makes?
- Can they compare with the source card and mark ✓ / ✗ / ? correctly?
- Can they say, in one sentence, "what is wrong and how to fix it"? Writing only "the AI is wrong" without saying where is not enough; stating the error and proposing a fix meets the goal.
- A good answer to the extension (challenge) question includes: the specific error + a suggested fix + a guess at why the AI got it wrong.
