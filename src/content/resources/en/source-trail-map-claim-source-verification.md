> The trouble in humanities is rarely that a pupil cannot find a source. It is that nobody has asked them to write "the tool says this" and "this is what I actually opened" as two separate things. Write it once, split across two lines, and the chain joins up.

## What this solves

A pupil uses a tool to look something up for a humanities topic. The answer reads smoothly and mentions "according to a survey report". You ask which page of that report, and the pupil points back at the tool window. That is not laziness: **the chain is broken.** Claim, source and verification are three steps, and the middle two have never been written down separately, so the pupil cannot say what they actually checked.

Most claims in humanities are about people, time and communities: how many people somewhere, when something started, which places a boundary includes. The place such a claim usually drifts is not the wording but **the number, the range and the date** — three in ten becomes three times as many, some schools becomes all schools in the territory, one year becomes recent years. Pupils can read perfectly well; nobody has asked them to put the two versions side by side, so the drift gets smoothed over.

This page writes the three steps as three cells: the first takes one claim copied word for word, the second takes two separate lines — line A for the source the tool claims, line B for the source I actually opened — and the third takes how I verified it. Once the three cells sit next to each other, the pupil can see where the gap is. Very often line B is empty, or line B names a source that never said the thing.

Then come four verdicts: it matches, it partly matches, the source never said this sentence, or it says the opposite / no source found. Only the second half of the fourth verdict is "no source found", and that is not the same as "the opposite". That distinction is worth more than every other line on the sheet.

Last comes the rule of two independent sources, one of them primary. A humanities claim is easy to "find" on ten websites, and all ten may be copying the same press release — so finding many is not the same as having verified anything.

The three cells, the four verdicts, the three forms of drift and the two-source rule are all **this resource's suggestion**. They are not an Education Bureau requirement and not a requirement of any curriculum document. The page does one thing only: it trains pupils to keep a sentence and the basis for that sentence apart on paper. On this page verify, cross-check, fact-check and tracing a source are actions you can write down, not attitudes. Reflection here also has a narrow meaning: not a paragraph about how you felt, but a line saying which step I tried and which step is still missing.

## Learning objectives

By the end, pupils can:

1. Copy **one claim word for word** out of a tool answer, thirty characters at most, without changing a word, translating it, or adding their own punctuation, and note which paragraph it came from.
2. Write the source on **two separate lines** of the same sheet: line A is the source the tool claims, line B is the source I actually opened — domain, page title, date consulted.
3. Give each chain **one of four verdicts** with one reason, and add no fifth verdict.
4. Name which of **the three forms of drift** — number, range or date — has occurred, and say where.
5. Give each chain **two independent sources**, one of them primary, and explain why those two count as independent.
6. Treat the sheet as **never scored, never used for honesty or copying, and never a judgement on what the topic should be**, and as something that never asks for a screenshot, a page copy or a chat record; the tool is always "the tool the school has specified".

The material corresponds to the "reflection on the application of AI" strand of AI literacy. The question pupils practise is not the big one about whether a tool is reliable, but a small one about one sentence: does this have a source, and did that source say it? That is the part of reflection that can be written on one sheet.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson in seven segments: 8 minutes to explain, 10 to demonstrate, 7 to copy one claim, 15 for the three-cell chain, 8 for the verdicts, 7 for the drift, 5 to close. **The order does not change**; if time runs short, cut the number of demonstration sentences — never a cell, never a verdict, never the second source.

| Time | Segment | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 8 min | Explain | Hand out the chain sheets and the four verdict cards; state one claim each, one chain each, and that no screenshot or chat record is collected. |
| 10 min | Demonstrate | With one invented claim, show how lines A and B are written separately, and what to add when line B says "I could not find it". |
| 7 min | Copy one claim | Each pupil picks one sentence from the tool answer and copies it word for word into cell one, thirty characters at most, noting the paragraph. |
| 15 min | Three-cell chain | Fill cell two, lines A and B, and cell three, how I verified it, then add the second independent source. |
| 8 min | Four verdicts | Each pupil chooses one verdict and writes one reason; group members check only whether the reason can be pointed at. |
| 7 min | Drift | Using three invented examples, name whether the number, the range or the date drifted, and write it in the margin. |
| 5 min | Close | Three closing sentences; the class reads out only the chains whose line B is empty, with no names. |

### 1. One claim copied word for word (thirty characters at most)

- **One sentence only**, copied word for word: no rewording, no translation, no adding your own punctuation.
- **Thirty characters at most** (each Chinese character counts; punctuation does not). If it is longer, take thirty consecutive characters from the middle and write "(extract)" at the end of cell one.
- **Say where it came from**: the paragraph or sentence number in the answer, for example "third paragraph, second sentence". Never copy the whole paragraph or the whole answer.
- **Copy a claim about the topic**, not the tool talking about itself (sentences such as "I am an AI model" are not copied).
- **Copy the error as it stands.** Correcting it is often exactly the moment the drift disappears.
- The tool is always "the tool the school has specified": no brand, no model and no supplier, and no comparison of which tool is better.

### 2. The three-cell chain (claim / source on two lines / how I verified it)

| Cell | What goes in it | The rule |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The claim | Word for word, thirty characters at most, with the paragraph noted at the end |
| 2 | The source | **Line A**: the source the tool claims (what the tool says it relied on); **Line B**: the source I actually opened — domain, page title, date consulted |
| 3 | How I verified it | Which step I took: read that paragraph of the original / found another independent source / compared the numbers; written so someone else could repeat it |

- **Lines A and B are separate lines**, never merged into "Source: a report". Merged, the gap becomes invisible and the sheet stops working.
- **Line A follows what the tool said**: quote it, or write "the tool says it relied on a survey report, without naming one". Never invent a source on the tool's behalf.
- **Line B carries three things only**: domain, page title, date consulted. No full address, no screenshot, no saved page copy, no chat record.
- **If you cannot find it, say so**: line B reads "I could not find it", and cell three names the two search routes tried. Not finding it is a result, not a failure, and there is no need to invent a source.
- **Cell three describes an action, not an opinion**: "I read paragraph three; the original says three in ten", not "I think it is unreliable".
- One chain carries one claim. Not finishing two chains in the lesson is normal — this page asks for one.

### 3. Four verdicts (four only)

| Verdict | When it applies | How far to write it |
|---|---|---|
| It matches | The source on line B really does say this, in meaning and in range | Point to the paragraph and quote the key words |
| It partly matches | The source says something close, but one of number, range or date differs | Say which one differs, and how the source actually puts it |
| The source never said this sentence | The source on line B exists, but the sentence is not in it | Say what the source does say, and how it differs |
| It says the opposite, or no source found | The source says the opposite; or line B found no source at all | For the opposite, quote that sentence; for no source, name the two search routes tried |

- **No fifth verdict**, and in particular no "broadly correct" or "medium reliability".
- **"No source found" is not "the opposite"**: both sit in the fourth verdict, but the reason must say which one, because what you do next is completely different.
- **The verdict is about this sentence and this source**, not about whether the tool is reliable overall and not about what the topic should say. This page makes no judgement on the topic's position.
- **The reason must be pointable**: after reading it, a group member should know which paragraph to look at. "It feels wrong" does not count.
- The claim in cell one is **not edited after the verdict**. The verdict may change; the copied sentence does not.

### 4. The three forms of drift (number / range / date)

Drift is usually not a whole sentence invented but one of three positions slipping a step. All three examples below are **invented** and point to no real institution, region or event; they illustrate the form only, and are not used to judge whether any real claim is true.

| Form | Invented example | Where the drift is |
|---|---|---|
| Number | The source says "three in ten respondents"; the retelling says "three times as many respondents" | A proportion becomes a multiple; three in ten and three times are not the same quantity |
| Range | The source says "some schools in this district"; the retelling says "all schools in the territory" | Some becomes all; the boundary moved out one level |
| Date | The source says "a survey carried out in 1998"; the retelling says "a recent survey" | A year becomes a vague stretch of time, and a reader can no longer trace it |

- After each chain, spend one minute on three positions only: the number in the sentence, the range word (some / all / most), and the time word (a year / recent years / lately).
- If you find drift, write it in the margin, name the form, and leave cell one untouched.
- **The three forms are not three grades**: any one of them makes a sentence impossible to trace, and none is milder than another.
- Use invented claims in the examples only. Do not put a real institution, a real region or a real event into an example, and do not use the class to judge whether any real claim is true.

### 5. The two-source rule, and closing

- **Every chain carries two independent sources**, one of which **must be primary**.
- **Primary**: the publisher is the origin of the data — the original survey report, the original statistics table, a document issued by the body concerned. A reprint, a summary, a news report or an aggregator's write-up is not primary.
- **Independent**: the two sources are not reprints of the same document, not one body's press release on two websites, and not quoting each other. Each has to stand on its own.
- **Both sources must have been opened**: line B and line C (the second source) each carry a domain, a page title and a date consulted. One opened source is not two.
- **When the two disagree**: do not average them and do not pick "the more credible one". Write both, give the verdict "it partly matches", and say which item differs.
- **Three closing sentences**: where the claim and the source differ; which independent source I added; which cell I will look at first next time.
- **The follow-up is one move only**: replace the second source on the same chain with another genuine primary source; no new sentence and no new column.

## Answer key and marking notes

This page is **not scored, not levelled and not ranked**, makes no judgement about honesty or copying and no judgement on what the topic should say, so there is no answer key — only the difference between three cells that are complete and a verdict that can be pointed at. Every example below is invented and involves no real institution, region or event.

### A sample claim (invented)

Cell one: "A survey shows that seven in ten residents in the district use the community library three or more times a week."

### A completed chain (invented)

| Cell | Content |
|---|---|
| 1 The claim | A survey shows that seven in ten residents in the district use the community library three or more times a week. (Copied from the second paragraph, first sentence.) |
| 2 Line A (tool's claim) | The tool says it relies on "a community survey report (2024)". |
| 2 Line B (what I opened) | Domain: community-survey.example (invented); page title: Survey of the Use of Community Facilities; date consulted: 20 November 2026. |
| 3 How I verified it | I read the "frequency of use" section: the original says "about three in ten respondents use it three or more times a week", and the sample was visitors to one library in the district, not residents of the whole district. |

### The verdict and its reason (invented)

| Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|
| It partly matches | The source does have a "three or more times a week" item, but the figure is three in ten, not seven in ten, and the sample was visitors, not all residents. |

### Drift and independence (invented)

- Form of drift: **number** (three in ten becomes seven in ten), and **range** as well (library visitors becomes all residents). A pupil who names both is fine; naming one is not wrong either.
- Two independent sources: line B is the survey report itself (primary); the second source is the library operator's own visitor statistics for the same year (primary, a different publisher). Neither is a reprint of the other, so they are independent.

### Common wrong turns and what to say

- **Lines A and B merged into one.** Say: "Two lines. Line A is what the tool said; line B is what you opened. Which page did you open?"
- **Line B says only "information online".** Say: "Three things: domain, page title, date. Which page online?"
- **"Could not find" written as "the opposite".** Say: "Is it 'not found' or 'the opposite'? The opposite needs the sentence quoted."
- **A fifth verdict, "broadly correct".** Say: "Four only. Which is it closest to?"
- **A whole paragraph copied, well over thirty characters.** Say: "One sentence, thirty at most. If you are extracting, write (extract)."
- **Two reprints from the same body.** Say: "The same body twice is not two. Try the original."
- **Asking pupils to hand over a chat record or a screenshot.** Do not: the sheet records a domain, a page title and a date only, and collects no screenshot, page copy, chat record or account detail.

### Checklist (run through once before the lesson)

- □ All three cells are present, and only three: the claim, the source (lines A and B), how I verified it.
- □ Cell one is copied word for word, thirty characters at most, with the paragraph noted.
- □ Cell two keeps lines A and B separate, and line B carries only domain, page title and date.
- □ Cell three describes an action, not an opinion.
- □ All four verdicts are present, and only four; the reason points to a paragraph in the source.
- □ "Not found" and "the opposite" are written as different things.
- □ All three forms of drift are present — number, range, date — and every example is marked invented.
- □ Each chain has two independent sources, one primary, with a reason why they count as independent.
- □ Nothing asks for a screenshot, a page copy, a chat record, account details or a pupil's own draft to be handed over, uploaded or photocopied.
- □ No brand, model, supplier, institution, country or city is named; the tool is always "the tool the school has specified".
- □ Nothing is written as an Education Bureau requirement; the three cells, four verdicts, three forms of drift and two-source rule are marked as this resource's suggestion.
- □ Nothing is scored, levelled or ranked, nothing judges honesty or copying, and nothing judges what the topic should say.

> The core is **one claim, three cells, four verdicts, three forms of drift, two sources.** Split "the tool says this" from "this is what I opened" onto two lines and the gap shows itself; add the second independent source and the sentence has actually been verified.
