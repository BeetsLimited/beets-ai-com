> A tool's answer reads certain whether or not anyone has checked it. This page gives pupils three levels of tone, five sentence patterns, and one rule for anything a tool has written.

## What this solves

English rewards the firm claim. "X is the main cause" sounds like better writing than "X may be one cause", and a pupil who hedges every sentence is told to be decisive. So pupils learn to write with more certainty than their evidence carries — and a sentence a tool produced carries that certainty for free, even when nobody in the room has checked it.

The fix is not to make every sentence vague. It is to make the tone match what the writer can actually point to: something looked at in this lesson, something with a basis but not yet checked, or nothing yet. Those are three levels, and they are marked on the sentence, not on the pupil.

The writing task is deliberately small: three sentences taken from the pupil's own exercise book, one level marked on each, one sentence rewritten, and one line of reason. Five sentence patterns supply the wording, so a pupil who knows the level still has something to write.

**Three things to read first:**

1. **The three levels and the five patterns are this resource's suggestion.** The Education Bureau's documents do not use these names and do not prescribe how many levels or patterns a class should have; what they do ask for is verifying information and thinking critically about it, and this page turns that into one lesson's worth of marking and rewriting.
2. **Nothing on this page is marked, graded or compared.** There is no score, no level award and no comment on a pupil's ability; pupils hand in nothing but their own sheet, and no tool output, screenshot, conversation record or account detail is ever collected.
3. **Two quoted passages come from a primary framework.** They are from the AI component of the Primary Information and Technology Curriculum Framework (pilot edition), which covers Primary 1 to Primary 6 only, and each is labelled as such where it appears. This page is for S1-S6: secondary classes borrow the principle, and nothing here presents that framework as a requirement for secondary schools. **No English Language curriculum guide is quoted anywhere on this page** — none is held locally — and no Chinese Language or Putonghua guide is used in its place.

The page judges sentences. It does not judge pupils, and it does not judge tools.

## Learning objectives

By the end, pupils can:

1. Name the three levels of tone and say what each one allows a sentence to claim.
2. Mark one level on each of three sentences taken from their own written work, and say in one line why each sentence sits at that level.
3. Choose one of the five sentence patterns and rewrite exactly one of their three sentences so that its tone matches the level they marked.
4. Write one line of reason: what changed in the sentence, and what made the earlier wording too strong or too weak.
5. State the rule for a sentence a tool produced and nobody has checked, and name where that sentence would be checked.

The page addresses **human-machine communication and collaboration** and **reflection on the application of AI**: the sentences come from the pupil's own writing, and the question is what a writer may claim when a machine has supplied the words. Three values are practised: **critical thinking** is asking what a sentence can actually support before polishing it; **responsibility** is writing the level on your own sentence rather than leaving it to the reader; **using technology at the right time** is knowing that a quick answer still has to be checked before it is written as fact.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson in six parts, in this order: the three levels, the five patterns, three sentences from your own work, one rewrite, the rule for tool-generated sentences, and the close. The three levels are three and the patterns are five — do not add or drop any, and do not reorder the parts.

### 1 Three levels of tone, fixed

A level is a statement about a sentence, not about a writer. Mark it in the margin; do not rewrite yet.

| Level | When a sentence may sit here | How the sentence reads |
|---|---|---|
| Can be checked now | You have looked at a source in this lesson and can name it in the same line | Plain present tense, with the source named: "According to ___, ..." |
| Hedged | You have a basis, but you have not checked it in this lesson | A verb that leaves room: "may", "appears to", "suggests"; the basis is named |
| Not established | There is no basis yet, or two sources disagree | Marked as unknown, plus what would settle it: "It is not yet known whether ..." |

Two rules go with the table, and the lesson is weaker without them:

- **The level is about the evidence, not the confidence of the writer.** A sentence does not become checkable because it is well written, and it does not become unestablished because the writer is unsure of the topic.
- **Marking a sentence as hedged is a correct answer, not a failure.** Most sentences in a first draft sit at the hedged level, and saying so is the work.

### 2 Five sentence patterns, fixed

Five groups, five jobs. They are not five levels: patterns 2 and 3 belong to the hedged level, pattern 4 to the not-established level, and pattern 1 only to a sentence you have actually checked. Pattern 5 can be added to any level, because it says where the checking would happen.

| Group | What it does | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| 1 A fact with a source | Reports something already checked | "According to ___, ..." / "___ states that ..." |
| 2 A hedged claim | Says what the evidence allows, no more | "It appears that ..." / "___ may ..." / "The evidence suggests ..." |
| 3 A limit or a condition | Narrows where the sentence holds | "This applies only when ..." / "___ may not hold for ..." |
| 4 A marked uncertainty | States plainly what is not known | "It is not yet known whether ..." / "I could not confirm ..." |
| 5 Where to check | Names the place the sentence could be settled | "This can be checked in ___." / "Before using this, I would check ___." |

- **One pattern per rewritten sentence.** Two patterns in one sentence usually means two ideas, and the reason line then has nothing specific to point at.
- **Pattern 1 needs the source on the same line.** "According to the textbook, page 42" is checkable; "according to research" is not, and belongs at the hedged level.

### 3 Three sentences from your own work, fixed

Open your own exercise book and take exactly three sentences from work you have already written this term — not sentences copied from a tool, and not sentences written for this exercise.

- **Mark one level on each**, in the margin, using the three names from part 1. Write nothing else yet.
- **Three sentences, not more.** Three marked carefully are worth more than a page of ticks, and the lesson has time for three.
- **If all three land on the same level, that is a result.** Write it down as it is; do not move one sentence to make the sheet look varied.

### 4 One rewrite and one reason, fixed

Choose exactly one of your three sentences. Rewrite it so that its tone matches the level you marked, using one pattern from part 2. Then write one line of reason underneath.

- **One sentence only.** The other two stay as they are, with their levels marked — that is the record of what you judged.
- **The reason line says what changed and why**: which words carried too much certainty, or too little, and what in the evidence decided it.
- **The rewrite may go down a level as well as up.** If the marked level was "can be checked now" and you cannot name a source, the honest rewrite is the hedged one.

### 5 The ceiling for tool-generated sentences

If a sentence came from a tool and nobody has checked it, its tone may not be written above the hedged level, and the sentence must say where it would be checked — pattern 5, on the same line.

- **This is the page's own rule, not an Education Bureau requirement.** It is one classroom way of keeping an unchecked answer from being written as fact.
- **Do not paste the tool's output onto the sheet.** Describe the sentence in your own words; the sheet records your sentence and your level, not the tool's text.
- **The ceiling lifts only when someone checks it.** Then the sentence may be written at the "can be checked now" level, with the source named — and the checking is named too, not implied.

### 6 The 60-minute lesson

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 8 minutes | The three levels: read the table, and mark one example sentence together |
| 10 minutes | The five patterns: match each pattern to a level, and say which one needs a source |
| 10 minutes | Choose three sentences from your own book and mark a level on each |
| 10 minutes | Rewrite exactly one sentence, then write the one-line reason |
| 10 minutes | Read your rewritten sentence and your reason to one other pupil; no names are read out |
| 12 minutes | Close: the teacher reads two or three rewritten sentences aloud, and the class reads the three closing lines |

**Three closing lines** (the class reads them together):

1. "This sentence can be checked here."
2. "This one may be true, but I have not checked it."
3. "This one is not known yet."

## Answer key and marking notes

**A worked sample (fictional, to show the format)**

| Sentence from the pupil's own book | Level marked | What happens to it |
|---|---|---|
| "Plastic waste in Hong Kong has risen every year." | Not established — no source in the lesson | Rewritten with pattern 4: "It is not yet known whether plastic waste has risen every year; the figures would need checking." |
| "The school canteen stopped using straws in September." | Can be checked now — the notice was read in class | Left as it is, with the source named on the same line |
| "Most pupils prefer the new timetable." | Hedged — the class survey was small | Rewritten with pattern 3: "This may not hold for the whole school, because only one class was asked." |

**One reason line, written well:** "I changed 'has risen every year' to 'is not yet known whether', because I had no figure in front of me and the first version claimed a trend I could not show."

**Common errors and what to say:**

- **Every sentence marked "can be checked now", with no source named.** Response: the level needs a source on the same line; without one, the sentence is hedged.
- **The rewrite made vaguer rather than more accurate.** Response: a hedged sentence still says what it rests on; "maybe something happened" says nothing and is not the hedged level.
- **Two sentences rewritten.** Response: only one. The other two keep their marks; that is the record.
- **The reason line praises the writing.** Response: write what changed and what decided it, not that it reads better.

**What counts as done:** three sentences marked with a level, one of them rewritten with one pattern, and one reason line that names what changed. Nothing is scored, no level is awarded, and no comparison between pupils is made.
