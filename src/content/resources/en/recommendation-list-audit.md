> What a ranked list hides is not usually a mistake in the ranking. It is that the ranking only covers what it could see. Three questions and four places turn "why is this all I can see?" into a lesson that fits one period.

## What this solves

Secondary 1 to 3 pupils look at ranked lists every day: search results, recommendation strips, popularity charts, activity lists. A list looks like an answer — somebody has already put it in order, and all we have to do is read it from the top. This lesson does not try to expose it. It tries to find its edges.

Three habits keep an audit shallow:

- **Treating a rank as quality.** First looks best, tenth looks worst. But a rank only says the item matched some standard; the list rarely says what that standard was, or who set it.
- **Treating "not on the list" as "does not exist".** Whatever is missing, pupils assume is not there, and so they believe the choice is ten items wide.
- **Assuming the list made itself.** As if it were simply "what most people like", with no human choice in it. But a list was always ranked by somebody: it has a source, a standard, an act of ranking, and things left outside.

**The aim of the lesson is not to find a lie. It is to find the edges.** A list can be honest, and reasonably ordered, and still cover only part of what exists. Those two statements are not in conflict, and pupils should be able to say both.

**Three statements to read first, and to print on every pupil sheet:**

1. **The three questions, the four places, the audit sheet and the re-ranking activity are this resource's suggestion. They are not an Education Bureau requirement.** What the official documents ask of this ground is that pupils think critically and identify error and false information, and that they comply with the law. No document prescribes three questions, four places, or a lesson sequence.
2. **This page does not explain how a ranking system works.** It says one thing only: a list **may have a standard, and the standard may not be published**. What goes on inside that system is not the aim of this lesson, and not something a pupil must know in order to audit a sheet of paper.
3. **Everything on this page and in its attachments is invented.** The ten items, the activity names, the people, the school, the courses and the videos are all fictional; there is no real school name, person, course name or video title. No platform, brand, shop or channel is named anywhere — not even in the form "for example a certain search engine". **Pupils audit the sheet their group was given, not their own phone** — this page never asks anyone to reveal an account, a viewing history or a usage habit.

**Three things this page deliberately does not say:** it does not say "the algorithm always produces bias" — we cannot see that system, so saying it would be a guess; it does not say "the list is always advertising" — some lists are and some are not, and that sentence would replace the pupil's judgement rather than support it; and it does not present itself as an Education Bureau requirement.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, pupils can:

1. State the **three audit questions** — who ranked it, what they ranked by, who is missing — and keep them in that order.
2. Point to one piece of evidence in each of the **four places** (source, standard, order, absence). Where there is none, write "cannot find out" and say what was tried.
3. Complete one **nomination of an absence**: name an item that should be on the list and is not, and write one line on how they know it exists.
4. Re-rank the ten items on a **single standard**, and write three lines of reason: what changed, why, and who is affected.
5. Say in one sentence that the list **covers only part of what exists** — and that this does not make it false.

The page maps to the AI literacy strand **basic principles of how artificial intelligence works**: pupils do not need the technical detail, but they do need to know that a ranked result always rests on choices of data and standard, and that those choices decide who gets seen. Of the four attitudes, this page sits closest to **critical thinking** and **respect for others**: critical thinking is not mistaking an order for a fact; respect for others is remembering that there are people outside the list.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson in four parts: **5 minutes of framing, 20 minutes for activity one (finding the gaps), 20 minutes for activity two (re-ranking), 15 minutes for the teacher's close**. The worksheet is a few pages, each opening with one instruction a pupil can read and act on.

### Step one: the three questions, in a fixed order

The three questions are **fixed in order, never swapped, never merged**. The order is part of the content: you ask who made the list before you ask what they sorted by, and knowing there is a standard is what makes you think about who is missing.

| Question | What it asks | What the pupil writes |
|---|---|---|
| One — **who ranked it** | Who made this list? Where does it sit? How much do we actually know? | One line, separating what is known from what is not |
| Two — **what they ranked by** | What standard put the items in this order? Is the standard published? | One line: a guess at the standard, plus one piece of evidence for it |
| Three — **who is missing** | What should be on the list and is not? How would we know it exists? | One nomination, or "cannot find out" |

Three points to make to pupils:

- **Question one is not "can this list be trusted".** That is a later judgement; question one is only about the source.
- **Question two allows a guess, but the guess needs evidence.** "Maybe it is ordered by how easy it is to join" must be followed by which two items on the list support that.
- **Question three is the important one.** Most pupils write "nothing" the first time, because they are looking inside the list. The question looks outside it.

### Step two: four places, and only four

The audit sheet has five boxes: **four places, plus one nomination of an absence**. The four places are fixed.

| Place | What can be seen on the list | What to write when it cannot be found |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | Where the list comes from, where it is posted, whether the data source is stated | "Cannot find out" plus what was tried |
| **Standard** | Whether the standard is written down | "Cannot find out" plus what was tried |
| **Order** | Whether the rank matches the description beside it (for example, "easiest to join" sitting last) | "Inconsistent: item __ says __ but ranks __" |
| **Absence** | Whether similar items exist outside the list | One nomination plus one line on how you know it exists |

**"Cannot find out" is a valid answer.** The lesson does not require pupils to find a hidden standard; it requires them to record honestly what they found and what they did not. An empty box is what is unfinished.

### Step three: activity one — finding the gaps (20 minutes)

One list and one audit sheet per group.

1. The group reads the ten-item list once. Ten items, in the order printed.
2. **Write one piece of evidence in each of the four places.** Where there is none, write "cannot find out" and add what was tried ("asked a classmate", "looked at the rest of the same sheet").
3. **The fifth box is the nomination of an absence**: name an item that should appear but does not, and write one line on how you know it exists — "because a classmate did something like it last term", "because another part of the same sheet mentions it". If nothing comes, write "cannot find out" and what was tried.
4. Three minutes of comparison inside the group: which of the four places was hardest to fill, and why?

**Rule:** do not look up where the list really came from. The whole list is invented, so failing to find it is the expected result. The object of the audit is the evidence on the paper, not the truth outside it.

### Step four: activity two — re-ranking (20 minutes)

1. **The group chooses one standard.** One only, and it must be written down and measurable: the time an activity takes, the number of conditions before joining, the amount of equipment needed.
2. **Re-rank the ten items on that standard** and write the new order.
3. **Write three lines of reason**, all three:
   - **What changed**: which item moved from which position to which.
   - **Why**: which standard, and which line on the list supports it.
   - **Who is affected**: which kind of pupil now sees something different — the pupil with no equipment, the pupil who can only stay at lunchtime.

**Three rules:** no standard may be "good", "popular" or "interesting" — those are feelings, not standards; re-ranking is not a vote, so the group need not agree on every position but must hand in one order; and once the order is handed in, the group must be able to say why the first item is first.

### Step five: the teacher's close (15 minutes)

Close with three sentences, written on the board one at a time:

1. **This list was ranked by somebody else.**
2. **A ranking can be reasonable and still cover only part of what exists.**
3. **Three questions are worth more than ten remembered positions.**

Then ask: "If you were making a list for next year's Secondary 1, would you say what standard you used?" Leave the answer open — it is the hook for the next lesson or for homework.

The ten items below are invented for this resource; any resemblance to a real activity, course or school is coincidental. The list does not state its standard.

| Rank | Item | The line printed beside it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lunchtime chess club | No sign-up needed, Tuesdays at lunch |
| 2 | Campus gardening group | Parent consent form required |
| 3 | Small robotics workshop | Twelve places |
| 4 | Debate practice | Fridays after school, briefing first |
| 5 | Campus TV production | Teacher recommendation required |
| 6 | Introductory sign language | Eight sessions per term |
| 7 | Long-distance running team | Wednesdays after school |
| 8 | Choir | Audition required |
| 9 | Library volunteering | Thursdays at lunch |

Before a recommendation list goes to parents or students, verify every entry against the original source, cross-check at least two independent sources for any figure or date, and record who gatekeeps the final list and on what basis.

## Answer key and marking notes

The answers below are **one reasonable reading, not the only one**. Marking looks at whether the three questions were worked through in order, whether each of the four places has evidence, and whether the re-ranking used one standard — not at whether the group reached the same conclusion.

### One reasonable answer to the three questions

| Question | One reasonable answer |
|---|---|
| One — who ranked it | Known: an activity list for this school, printed on one sheet, unsigned. Not known: which member of staff made it, or whether pupils were asked. |
| Two — what they ranked by | Guess: the number of conditions before joining — item 1 needs nothing, item 2 needs a consent form, item 5 needs a recommendation, item 8 needs an audition. The sheet does not state a standard. |
| Three — who is missing | Nomination: an activity that needs its own equipment and happens after school (for example filming), because item 10 already says "bring your own camera", which shows equipment is a condition, yet after-school times appear only at items 4 and 7. |

### One way to fill the four places

| Place | One reasonable entry |
|---|---|
| Source | A list posted in school, unsigned, with no stated source. Write "cannot find out where the data came from". |
| Standard | Not written on the sheet. Write "cannot find out", plus "but the length of the note beside items 1 to 8 has no visible relation to the rank". |
| Order | Item 2 needs a consent form and sits one place below item 1, which needs nothing — consistent with the guess that it is ordered by how easy it is to join. |
| Absence | Nominate an after-school activity that needs its own equipment; how we know: "item 10 says bring your own camera, and that kind of activity usually runs after school". |

### One worked re-ranking

Standard: **number of conditions before joining**, fewest first.

One possible new order: chess, sign language, library volunteering, running, gardening, robotics, debate, choir, campus TV, photography.

The three lines:

- **What changed**: library volunteering moved from 9th to 3rd; photography stayed last.
- **Why**: volunteering only needs the pupil to be in the library at lunch, with no other condition; photography needs a camera, which is the most conditions of any item.
- **Who is affected**: a pupil without a camera used to see photography in the middle of the list; now it sits last, so the items that pupil can actually join appear earlier.

### Four marking points

- **Order of the questions**: all three, in sequence. Skipping question two and writing "the list is biased" is unfinished.
- **The four places**: evidence in each, and "cannot find out" carries a note on what was tried. The bare three words are half-finished.
- **The nomination**: it must say how the pupil knows the item exists. A name with no reason is a guess, not a nomination.
- **The re-ranking**: one standard, three lines of reason. Two standards at once ("easy and fun") is unfinished, because the orders cannot be compared.

### One common wrong answer, and how to reply

A pupil will say: "If it is not on the list, it is not there — how would I know about something else?" The reply is not to send them looking. Shrink the question: **we do not need to know everything, only to be able to say "there may be more, and I do not know it"**. Demonstrate once: sort the ten items by their first letter, then ask "is there an eleventh? How do you know?" Pupils find that they were already using knowledge from outside the list — and that is where a nomination begins.
