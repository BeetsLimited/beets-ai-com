**On this page a price means a meal price a pupil has recorded inside the area the teacher sets; a figure a tool supplies is only a claim until it has been verified.**

## What this solves

"How much does a meal cost around here?" Put that question to the school-approved tool and it returns a figure, usually with no date, no statement of where the district boundary falls, no indication of whether the meal was eaten in or taken away, and no indication of whether the figure is an average, the lowest entry, or a rough estimate. The number looks reasonable, so it goes into the report.

The second gap is the conditions. The same "meal" covers a different price range at breakfast and at lunch, a different range eaten in and taken away, and a different range with a drink and without one. Two prices collected under different conditions cannot be compared directly, and if that is not written down, no amount of collected prices can be merged.

The third gap is the portion basis. A box, a bowl and a portion are three different amounts for the same price; leave the portion basis unwritten and two prices look identical when they are not the same thing at all.

The fourth gap is the wording. The tool says "average" when we hold only a handful of records; it says "lowest" when that entry may belong to one day in one place; it says "about" when we cannot tell what range that "about" came from. An average is one representative value, and the highest and lowest entries are two individual examples; neither of them means "this is what everyone pays".

The fifth gap is the verdict. Asked whether the account can be used, the usual answer is "it should be about right". With no verdict the verification produces no result: the table is neatly filled in and nobody can say whether the tool's sentence may be used.

This page does five things only, in fixed numbers: one, three conditions (which meal, eat in or take away, with or without a drink); two, pupils collect their own records in four cells (date, place, price and unit, portion basis); three, the class merges them into one table and finds the highest and the lowest entry, with P5-P6 also working out one average; four, four things to verify (date, district, meal, wording) plus one source item; five, one verdict at three levels. **This page builds no model and draws no graph.** It does not teach statistical method, does not calculate percentages or grades, does not score, does not rank, does not compare pupils' spending with one another, and never asks for tool output, screenshots or account details to be handed in, uploaded or photocopied.

The page contains no real price at all, and every example is labelled as fictional. No restaurant, food-delivery platform, chain, app, website or model is named anywhere; a tool is always "the school-approved tool".

When a tool states a price confidently and no source can be found for it, that is an AI hallucination; the only way to remove it is to fact-check the price against the pupils' own records, item by item, and to verify each one. Questioning is what makes that possible: the same question put a different way can return a different price. The page also reminds pupils not to drift into over-reliance — filling the four cells, merging the table and writing the verdict are all done by hand.

**Lines that go on the worksheet and are not relaxed:** the teacher sets the survey area, choosing one of three arrangements — the school tuck shop, price material the teacher supplies, or a designated route the teacher leads. Pupils never decide for themselves to leave the school grounds. No photographing price tags or shops, no shop names, no names of any person; throughout, only the date, the place, the price and the portion are recorded. The place cell carries the area and the code the teacher set, never a shop name.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson pupils can:

1. Write the three conditions and confirm that the whole class shares them.
2. Collect records inside the area the teacher sets — two entries per pupil for P4-P5, three for P5-P6 — with all four cells filled in for each entry.
3. Merge the class records into one table and find the highest and the lowest entry; P5-P6 also work out one average and write the sentence "an average cannot stand for every person".
4. Verify four things (date, district, meal, wording) and record the source item as either "given" or "not given".
5. Write one verdict — usable, usable only with a condition added, or unusable — with one sentence of reasoning.

This page covers the learning strand **reflection on the application of AI**, at the upper primary key stage (P4-P6). Lower primary (P1-P3) does not run this price survey; if the format is ever moved down, only the three conditions and two entries are kept, with no average. Three values are practised: **critical discernment** asks which day and which area a price belongs to before it is used; **human-AI collaboration** keeps the tool to being asked once, while recording, merging and judging stay with the pupils; **timely and appropriate use** is knowing which verification item is worth stopping for.

This page sits inside the scope of the AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools. The learning and teaching focus of the lesson is the order of the verification, not the level of the prices, so the feedback a teacher gives during the demonstration and the close talks only about recording and verifying, never about whose price was cheaper.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson. Three conditions, four cells, four things to verify, one source item and a verdict at three levels: fixed numbers, nothing added and nothing dropped.

### One. The three conditions (three, fixed, the same for the whole class)

| Condition | Options | Why the whole class must share it |
|---|---|---|
| Which meal | Breakfast / lunch / dinner | The three sit in different price ranges; mixed together they cannot be compared |
| Eat in or take away | Eat in / take away | The same meal can carry a different price each way |
| With or without a drink | With a drink / without a drink | A drink is a separate item; a different wording makes the prices incomparable |

Three things to hold on to: the teacher writes the three conditions on the board before the lesson and the class copies the same wording; if a condition changes, the records already collected are redone; if the three are not written in full, the table cannot be merged.

### Two. Collecting the records (four cells)

| Cell | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Date | The day of collection, with year, month and day |
| Place (area set by the teacher) | The area and code the teacher set, for example the school tuck shop, or the first point on the designated route; never a shop name |
| Price and unit | The price as the price list shows it, copied as it stands, in dollars; no conversion, no estimating |
| Portion basis | A box, a bowl, a portion — as the price list words it |

**Stage arrangement (fixed):**

- **P4-P5**: two entries per pupil, finding only the highest and the lowest, with no average.
- **P5-P6**: three entries per pupil, plus one average.
- A class uses one arrangement only, chosen by the teacher and then kept by everyone; P5 may take either, and once chosen it is followed to the end.

Four things to hold on to: all four cells are filled in for every entry; the three conditions do not change during the lesson; only these four items are recorded — no shop names, no names of people, no photographs; the price is copied from the price list and never adjusted.

### Three. Merging into one table

The class puts its records on one table, or draws a table on the board and fills it in entry by entry. Before merging, look once: do the three conditions match across the class? Any entry that does not match is redone and is not merged.

Once the table is filled in, two things are done:

1. **Find the highest and the lowest entry.** Write down the date, the place, the price and the portion basis of that entry — never "a certain pupil".
2. **P5-P6 work out one average.** Add the prices collected under the same conditions, divide by the number of entries, and write the unit (dollars) and the number of entries; then write the sentence **"an average cannot stand for every person"**, and beside it the highest and the lowest entry with a note on why.

Three things to hold on to: an average is one representative value, not what each person pays; the highest and the lowest entry are two examples, not a ranking; prices collected under different conditions are never merged, and two classes or two days are never averaged into one figure.

### Four. Four things to verify, plus one source item

Put one question to the school-approved tool, once, for example "at the three conditions on the board, how much does a meal cost around here?" Copy the tool's wording exactly as it stands, then verify item by item:

| Item | The question to ask |
|---|---|
| Date | Does the tool's account state a date or a period? Is it the same period as the days we collected? |
| District | Does the tool's account state where the district boundary falls? Is it the same area the teacher set? |
| Meal | Does the tool's account state which meal, eat in or take away, and with or without a drink? |
| Wording | Does the tool say "average", "lowest" or "about"? Which of the three does our table actually support? |

Only that one question is put to the tool, with no prompt added to steer it towards a particular figure; a figure that differs from our table is exactly what the lesson is looking at.

**The source item** is recorded as "given" or "not given" only — whether the tool says where its figure came from. No website, app or shop name appears on this page, so the source item goes no further than that.

Three things to hold on to: the wording item is the one most often let through — an average is not the lowest entry, and the lowest entry is not "about"; each of the four is marked "same" or "not the same", with one sentence on where the difference lies; where the source is "not given", the verdict is never "usable".

### Five. One verdict (three levels, fixed)

| Verdict | When it is written |
|---|---|
| Usable | All four items are "same" and the source item is "given" |
| Usable only with a condition added | One or two items are "not the same"; state which condition must be added — date, district, meal or wording. All four "same" but no source also takes this level, with a note that the source must be added |
| Unusable | Three or more items are "not the same", or the wording item does not match our table while the tool still says "average" or "lowest" |

Three things to hold on to: one verdict sentence, with its level and its reason; no "roughly usable" or "partly usable" beyond the three levels; the reason points at one of the four items, never at a feeling.

### Six. The 60 minutes, the offline version and the close

Explanation and demonstration 8 min → collecting 12 min → merging the table and finding the highest and lowest 12 min → asking the tool and verifying four items 14 min → verdicts and reading them out 8 min → close and homework 6 min.

**Offline version:** the teacher prints the price material beforehand (their own material, possibly fictional sample prices) together with a sample tool reply (also fictional); pupils then do the four cells, the four items and the verdict just the same. Neither the page nor the attachment carries any real price.

Three closing sentences:

1. Write the three conditions in full and the prices can be compared.
2. An average is one representative value; the highest and the lowest entry are two examples.
3. There are only three verdicts, and a verification is finished only when the reason is written.

Homework: take a different meal, such as breakfast, and run the sheet once more, writing only the four cells and one verdict; the class merge is not repeated.

- **Against "What we throw away: building a verified waste chart"**: that page has pupils turn official data into a line graph, and its point is the graph and the trend. This page draws no graph and builds no chart; it does one collection, one table and one verdict.

## Answer key and marking notes

This page is not an assessment instrument: it gives no score, carries no grade, produces no percentage ranking, is not entered in a report card and is not a conduct or honesty record, and it does not compare pupils' spending either. "Complete" means only that the record sheet can be read.

**What a completed sheet should show:**

- The three conditions written down, matching the board.
- All four cells filled in for every entry: date, place, price and unit, portion basis.
- Two entries for P4-P5; three entries for P5-P6, with one average and the sentence "an average cannot stand for every person".
- The highest and the lowest entry, with the date, the place and the price — not a name.
- Each of the four items marked "same" or "not the same"; the source item marked "given" or "not given".
- One verdict at one level, with one sentence of reasoning that points at one of the four items.

**Fictional example (to show the format, not data)**

| Item | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|
| Date | Day X, month X, 20XX |
| Place (area set by the teacher) | First point on the designated route (code A) |
| Price and unit | XX dollars |
| Portion basis | One box |
| The tool's account (fictional) | "A meal around here averages about YY dollars", with no date, no district boundary and no meal stated |
| Date | Not the same (no period given) |
| District | Not the same (no boundary given) |
| Meal | Not the same (eat in or take away not stated) |
| Wording | Not the same (the tool says "average"; with a handful of records we cannot state the range of an average) |
| Source | Not given |
| Verdict | Unusable: all four items are not the same and no source is given; before use, the date, district, meal and wording all have to be added. |

> Every price, date and code above is a **fictional example** written for this exercise. None of it represents a real price, a real date, a real place or a published figure.

**A second fictional example, showing "usable only with a condition added":** the tool writes "in month X of 20XX, inside the designated area, lunch eaten in without a drink, about XX to YY dollars". Date, district and meal are all "same", the source item is "given", and the wording item says "about" while our table's highest and lowest are close together. Verdict: "usable only with a condition added — the wording must state that this is the approximate range of these entries, not the average for the whole district."

**Common answers and responses:**

- **Only the price is written, with no portion basis.** Response: without a portion basis two prices cannot be compared; go back to the price list and copy the portion.
- **Prices from different meals are averaged together.** Response: separate them by the three conditions first, then merge; different conditions are never merged.
- **The highest entry carries a classmate's name.** Response: only the date, the place, the price and the portion — no names of people.
- **No source is given, and the pupil still writes "usable".** Response: "not given" never takes "usable"; if all four items match, write "usable only with a condition added" and say the source must be added.
- **The verdict says "roughly usable".** Response: there are only three levels; choose one and give the reason.
