> Two to five prompts, one per line, four columns. Fill the four columns and a teacher can see where the pupil began to think, without looking at any screen.

## What this solves

A pupil asks a tool one question and an answer appears. The teacher holds the answer and cannot see how it was reached: which sentence was asked first, which word was changed on the way, and why. Two pieces of work then differ only in how tidy they look.

Asking pupils to hand in a chat record is neither practical nor private, and the moment a screenshot is required the emphasis moves from "how did I think" to "did I keep the evidence". This page takes the other route: **the prompts themselves become the work that is handed in**. One journal holds two to five prompts, one per line, each in four columns.

The four columns are the trace of one act of thinking — the question I first asked, what I changed, why I changed it, and how the answer differed. Three levels of questioning (facts, reasons, scope) and one three-step revision then give pupils a second chance to turn a vague prompt into one that asks something answerable. The journal carries no name, only class and group, and it collects no tool account or login detail.

## Learning objectives

Pupils will be able to:

1. Record **two to five** prompts in the journal, one per line, with all four columns filled.
2. Name the one place they changed (a word, a condition or a scope) and say in one sentence why they changed it.
3. Write **one sentence** on how the answer before and after the change differed.
4. Use the three levels of questioning (facts, reasons, scope) to revise one prompt once, and say which level they changed.
5. Understand that the journal carries no name, does not mark a prompt as good or bad, and is not a judgement about honesty.

The content covers two strands of AI literacy: human-machine communication and collaboration, and reflection on the application of AI. In values it rests on **critical discernment** and **timely and appropriate use**: critical discernment is being able to ask why a statement is so, and timely and appropriate use is judging which prompt deserves a second ask and which answer can simply be used.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson in five parts: 5 minutes of introduction, 10 minutes on the four columns, 15 minutes on the three levels, 15 minutes on one revision, and 15 minutes for the teacher's checks and the close. **The order of the five parts does not change**; if time runs short, cut depth, never the number of columns, levels or steps.

| Time | Part | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 5 min | Introduction | Hand out the journal and explain the four columns; state that no chat record is collected, no name is written, and a prompt is not marked good or bad. |
| 10 min | 1 — Four-column journal | Pupils put the two to five prompts they used today into the four columns, one per line. |
| 15 min | 2 — Three levels | Model the three levels; pupils ask one of their prompts once at each level. |
| 15 min | 3 — One revision | Choose one prompt, change one thing only, then set the two answers side by side. |
| 15 min | 4 and 5 — Checks and close | The teacher circulates and reads the four columns only; the class reads three closing sentences and hears the extension. |

### 1 — The four-column journal (four columns, fixed)

One sheet, four columns, one line per prompt. **Two to five prompts per journal, no more and no fewer**: one prompt shows no change, and from six onwards pupils start to copy.

| Column | What the pupil writes | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — The question I first asked | The sentence I first typed, transcribed word for word, not rewritten | One sentence (transcribed) |
| 2 — What I changed | The one place I changed: a word, a condition, or a scope | One place only |
| 3 — Why I changed it | Why I changed that place | One sentence |
| 4 — How the answer differed | How the answer before and after the change differed | **One sentence only** |

Four rules for the four columns:

- **One prompt, four columns, no fifth column.** Anything extra is said aloud, not written on the sheet.
- **Column 1 is transcribed word for word.** It may be rough, but it may not be tidied afterwards into a "good" prompt — the column wants the sentence as it was.
- **Column 2 names one place.** Change three things at once and column 4 can no longer say which one made the difference.
- **Column 4 is one sentence.** If it will not fit in one, the prompt needs revising again — the answer does not need to be longer.

### 2 — Three levels of questioning (three levels, fixed)

The three levels are **ways of asking**, not grades, and not three steps to be completed in turn. A pupil may stop at the second level; but **the scope question may not be asked before the facts question** — asking "where does this apply" before asking for a source only produces a vaguer answer.

| Level | How to ask it (read aloud) | What the level asks for |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Facts | What is the source of this statement? What is the reference, the year, the figure? | Whether there is any basis |
| 2 — Reasons | Why is it so? Under what conditions does it hold? | The reason and its conditions |
| 3 — Scope | Where does this answer apply? Where does it not apply? | The boundary |

Three rules of use:

- **The order is fixed: facts, then reasons, then scope.** Out of order, pupils are really asking the same level three times.
- **Once at each level is enough.** Not every prompt needs all three; the journal records one prompt only.
- **Read the wording as written; do not turn it into "what do you think".** "What do you think" asks for a feeling; the three levels ask for a basis.

### 3 — One revision exercise (three steps, fixed)

Once only, **three steps and no fourth** (no "ask again", no "compare again").

1. **Choose one prompt.** Take one line from the journal — usually the one whose answer was vaguest or whose column 4 was hardest to write.
2. **Change one thing only.** Add a condition, narrow a scope, or change the wording — **choose one of the three**, and name the place in column 2.
3. **Set them side by side.** Put the answer before and after the change next to each other and write one sentence in column 4: how the answer differed.

Stop after the third step. If column 4 has become a paragraph, the third step has not held to one sentence.

### 4 — The teacher's checks

1. **Read the journal, not the tool's screen.** Do not collect, ask for, or photograph a chat record, and do not ask for a tool account or login detail.
2. **Do not mark the prompt as good or bad.** Look only at whether the four columns are filled, whether column 2 names the place changed, and whether column 3 gives a reason.
3. **Do not compare pupils with pupils.** The journal carries no name, only class and group; discussion stays at group level and no journal is read aloud by name.
4. **Make no judgement about honesty.** The journal records the process of asking, not whether a person used a tool, and it does not enter a conduct or examination record.

### 5 — Close and extension

The class reads three sentences aloud (as written, without commentary):

1. "This is the question I first asked."
2. "I changed this one place, because ___."
3. "What was different about the answer was ___."

Two extensions, neither adding a column or a step:

- **Use it once more in another subject.** The back of the same sheet still has four columns, three levels and three steps. The subject changes; the four columns do not.
- **Turn the three levels on a classmate's answer.** Take one fictional answer, ask only the facts question and the reasons question, and write three lines under the journal — again with no name.

## Answer key and marking notes

The journal is **not marked, graded or ranked**. Below are answers a teacher can use while demonstrating and circulating. Every example is fictional.

### A filled journal (fictional)

| Column | What the pupil wrote |
|---|---|
| 1 — The question I first asked | "At how many degrees does water boil?" |
| 2 — What I changed | Changed "at how many degrees" to "at standard atmospheric pressure, at how many degrees" |
| 3 — Why I changed it | Because the boiling point changes with pressure, and the first sentence did not say which situation it meant. |
| 4 — How the answer differed | Before, it said "100 degrees"; after, it said "100 degrees at standard atmospheric pressure, lower when the pressure is lower". |

Column 4 is one sentence, and the two halves of the semicolon are two sides of one fact. **That is the right length, not a model answer**: what the teacher reads for is not how well it is written but whether it says where the difference lies.

### The three levels, demonstrated (fictional)

| Level | The original question | Revised to |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Facts | "What is photosynthesis?" | "What is the source of the statement about photosynthesis? Which page of the textbook?" |
| 2 — Reasons | "Why do plants need sunlight?" | "Why can photosynthesis not happen without sunlight? Under what conditions does it hold?" |
| 3 — Scope | "Is photosynthesis important?" | "Which plants does this statement apply to, and which does it not?" |

Each line changes one thing only. The third row moves from "is it important" to "which plants", so what it asks for is no longer an evaluation but a boundary.

### One revision, demonstrated (fictional, three steps)

1. **Choose one prompt**: "What are the benefits of recycling?"
2. **Change one thing only** — narrow the scope: "Where does the paper our class throws away each day go after it is collected?"
3. **Set them side by side**: before, the answer was a general list such as "less pollution, saved resources"; after, it pointed first to the school's collection arrangement and then to how the collector handles it. Column 4 reads: "Before, a general benefit; after, a process that can be checked."

### Common wrong answers and responses

- **Column 4 written as a paragraph.** Response: "One sentence is enough; if it will not fit, the prompt needs revising again." (The number of columns and the length do not change.)
- **Column 2 saying "I asked again".** Response: "Asking again is not changing one place. Which word or condition did you change?"
- **Column 3 saying "because the tool said so".** Response: "That is the tool's reason, not yours. Why did you want to change it?"
- **Only the scope question asked.** Response: "Asking about scope before asking for a source gets the same sentence back. Let us go to level one."
- **Asking pupils to paste the chat.** Do not. Replacing that practice, and collecting less pupil data, is the point of the journal.

### Checklist (the teacher runs it once before the lesson)

- □ The journal has four columns only: the question I first asked, what I changed, why I changed it, how the answer differed.
- □ The journal states two to five prompts per entry, one per line.
- □ The journal states that column 4 takes one sentence only.
- □ All three levels are present, in the order facts, reasons, scope.
- □ The revision exercise has three steps and no fourth.
- □ The journal has no name field, only class and group.
- □ Nowhere does the material ask for a chat record to be handed in, pasted or photographed.
- □ No line marks a prompt as good or bad, and no line raises honesty.
- □ No line compares one pupil with another.
- □ No tool, platform, brand, model or supplier is named anywhere; the wording is always "the tool the school has specified".
- □ Every example is fictional, and the page says so.
- □ Nothing is written as an Education Bureau requirement; the four columns, three levels, three steps and the 60-minute plan are this resource's suggestion.

> The core: **four columns record one change, three levels ask about one thing, and the revision happens once.** Fill the four columns and no teacher needs the screen; write a paragraph and nothing has been changed.
