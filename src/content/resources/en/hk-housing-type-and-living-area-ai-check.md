**This page does one thing: it sends a tool's sentence about housing area back to a statistic published by a government department, and then rewrites that sentence into one that can be quoted.**

## What this solves

"How big is the place a Hong Kong household lives in?" Put that question to the school-approved tool and it answers: "The average Hong Kong household lives in about XX square metres." The sentence is short, the figure is clear, so it goes into the project. What the sentence hides is that four things are missing at once: which housing type, which year, which way of measuring floor area, and whether the figure is per household or per person.

The first gap is the type. Public rental housing, subsidised sale flats, private residential and others are four different categories, and the living area of each is not the same figure. Ask about "the Hong Kong household" as one whole and the answer belongs to none of them.

The second gap is the definition. Saleable area, internal floor area and gross floor area are three measures, and the same flat is a different number under each of them. A tool usually writes only "area" and never says which measure it used. Different definitions cannot be compared.

The third gap is the year. Housing statistics are published every year, and a figure with no year may belong to another one; figures from different years cannot be compared directly.

The fourth gap is the scope. The same area can be per household or per person, for the whole territory or for one district. Scope is the item most often let through, because the numbers look close enough.

**This page builds no scoring model and ranks nobody.** It builds no model, sets no factors or weights, calculates no total score, draws no graph, converts nothing into a grade, and does not judge which housing type is better. It does five things only, in fixed numbers: one, one checkable sentence with four things in it; two, four ways of recording a figure; three, one question to the tool; four, four things to verify plus one source item; five, one rewritten sentence.

The page contains no real value at all, and every example is labelled as fictional. No housing estate, developer, brand, app, website or model is named anywhere; a tool is always "the school-approved tool", while a government department may be named in full. The four housing types are written with their official names, never with a name invented for this page.

When a tool states a floor area confidently and no source can be found for it, that is an AI hallucination; the only way to remove it is to fact-check the figure against the statistics the government department publishes, item by item, and to verify each one. The same housing type in the same year gives a different figure under a different measure, and that systematic leaning is where data bias shows itself. Questioning is the habit that catches it: ask which type, which year and which measure before quoting. The page also reminds pupils not to drift into over-reliance — copying the four ways of recording, verifying the four items and writing the rewrite are all done by hand.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson pupils can:

1. Write one checkable sentence with four things in it: a housing type (one of four), a district, a year, and a way of measuring floor area (one of three).
2. Copy four things out of a statistic published by a government department: the department's name in full, the year, the table number or page name, and the unit together with the definition.
3. Put that same sentence to the tool once, without giving it the year, the department or the measure.
4. Verify type, definition, year and scope item by item, and record the source item as either "given" or "not given".
5. Rewrite the sentence into one sentence in which the type, the measure and the year all appear.

This page covers the learning strand **reflection on the application of AI**, at the junior secondary key stage (S1-S3). Three values are practised: **critical discernment** asks which housing type, which year and which measure a floor area belongs to before it is quoted; **human-AI collaboration** keeps the tool to being asked once, while copying, verifying and rewriting stay with the pupils; **timely and appropriate use** is knowing which verification item is worth stopping for.

Floor area is a measurable quantity and its unit must be written as the document writes it; a sentence can only be verified if another person can find the same statistic from the same four items. The learning and teaching focus is the order of the verification, so the feedback a teacher gives during the demonstration and the close talks only about the four items and the rewrite, never about which housing type is better.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson. Four things in the sentence, four ways of recording a figure, four items to verify, one source item and one rewritten sentence: fixed numbers, nothing added and nothing dropped.

### One. One checkable sentence (four things, fixed)

Write one sentence with all four things in it. This is a **fictional example**, to show the format only:

> In 20XX, in one district, the average saleable area per household in public rental housing was XX square metres.

| Item | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Housing type | One of four: public rental housing / subsidised sale flats / private residential / others. Use the official name, never a name invented for the page |
| District | The whole territory, or one district named by the teacher |
| Year | The year the figure belongs to |
| Measure of floor area | One of three: saleable area / internal floor area / gross floor area |

Three things to hold on to: the four housing types carry their official names, and a short form is not a substitute; the measure is one of the three, never just "area"; if the four are not written in full, the sentence cannot be verified.

### Two. Four ways of recording the figure (four, fixed)

Take the sentence to the statistics a government department publishes, find the same figure, and write down all four:

| Item | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| Department name in full | The full name of the government department that published the statistic |
| Year | The year or period the figure belongs to |
| Table number or page name | The table number or page name the figure sits in |
| Unit and definition | The unit as the document writes it (square metres, for example), and the measure written out once as the official definition |

Three things to hold on to: with one of the four missing the set counts as unfinished; the unit and definition line must say whether the figure is per household or per person; the point of the four is that another person can find the same figure from the same four items.

### Three. Asking the tool (one sentence, three things withheld)

Put the sentence from part one to the school-approved tool once, but **without the year, without the department and without the measure**; copy the tool's answer exactly as it stands and do not rewrite it.

Two things to hold on to: ask once, and add no prompt that steers the tool towards a particular figure; copy the answer whether it matches our statistic or not, and verify only after copying.

### Four. Four things to verify, plus one source item

| Item | The question to ask |
|---|---|
| Type | Which of the four housing types does the tool's account belong to? Is it the same type as our sentence? |
| Definition | Is the tool talking about saleable area, internal floor area or gross floor area? Is it the same measure as our sentence? |
| Year | Does the tool's account state a year? Is it the same year as our statistic? |
| Scope | Is the tool's figure per household or per person? For the whole territory or for one district? |

**Stage arrangement (fixed):**

- **S1**: verify the type item only; the teacher reads the other three (definition, year, scope) aloud, and pupils mark "same" or "not the same" beside the sentence.
- **S2-S3**: verify the type and definition items, judge the year and scope items themselves, and carry that judgement into the rewrite.

**The source item** is recorded as "given" or "not given" only — whether the tool says where its figure came from. No website or app name appears on this page, so the source item goes no further than that.

Three things to hold on to: mark each of the four "same" or "not the same", and add one sentence on where a difference lies; the scope item is the one most often let through, because the numbers look close enough; where the source is "not given", the rewritten sentence must say that the claim has not been verified.

### Five. One rewritten sentence (one, fixed)

Rewrite the sentence into one that contains **the type, the measure and the year** together. A fictional example:

> In 20XX, the official statistics do not publish a single figure for the saleable area per household in public rental housing in one district; the tool's "about XX square metres" states neither a year nor a measure, has not been verified, and is not used.

Three things to hold on to: a rewrite missing the type, the measure or the year leaves the lesson unfinished; where the tool's claim cannot be verified, the rewrite says so and no plausible-looking number is filled in; the rewrite is one sentence, not a paragraph.

### Six. The 60 minutes, the offline version and the close

Explanation and demonstration 10 min → writing the sentence, finding the statistic and recording the four 18 min → asking the tool and copying its answer 8 min → the four items and the source 14 min → rewrite and reading them out 6 min → close and homework 4 min.

**Offline version:** the teacher prints a statistics extract and a sample tool reply beforehand, both prepared by the teacher (the sample reply may be fictional); pupils then do the four ways of recording, the four items and the rewrite just the same. Neither the page nor the attachment carries any real value.

Three closing sentences:

1. Write the four housing types and the three measures clearly, and the figure can be verified.
2. Year and scope are the two items most easily let through, because the numbers look close enough.
3. A rewritten sentence needs the type, the measure and the year before it can be quoted.

Homework: take a different housing type, such as subsidised sale flats, and run the sheet once more, writing only the four ways of recording and one rewritten sentence.

**How this page divides the work with one related page:**

- **Against "S1-S3 Where Hong Kong lives: comparing housing choices with a scoring model"**: on that page pupils set their own factors, give scores from 1 to 5, set weights and calculate a total for three fictional housing options. That is **mathematical modelling**, and its point is when a model can be trusted. This page **builds no model, sets no factors or weights, calculates no total and ranks nobody**; it does one thing: verify a figure that already exists in an official statistic, then rewrite it into a sentence that can be quoted. Both pages use housing, but one makes a tool and the other verifies a number; either order works, and the content does not overlap.

## Answer key and marking notes

This page is not an assessment instrument: it gives no score, carries no grade, produces no percentage ranking, is not entered in a report card and is not a conduct or honesty record; it ranks nobody and does not judge which housing type is better. "Complete" means only that the record sheet can be read.

**What a completed sheet should show:**

- One sentence with all four things: type (one of four), district, year, measure (one of three).
- All four recorded: department name in full, year, table number or page name, unit and definition.
- The tool's answer copied as it stands, not adjusted to match the statistic.
- Each of the four items marked "same" or "not the same", with one sentence on any difference; S1 does the type item only, with the other three read aloud by the teacher.
- The source item marked "given" or "not given" only.
- One rewritten sentence in which the type, the measure and the year all appear.

**Fictional example (to show the format, not data)**

| Item | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|
| Sentence | In 20XX, in one district, the average gross floor area per household in private residential was XX square metres. |
| Department name in full | Sample department (fictional) |
| Year | 20XX |
| Table number or page name | Sample statistical table 3 |
| Unit and definition | Square metres; gross floor area, per household |
| The tool's account (fictional) | "Households in the district average about YY square metres", with no year, no measure and no scope |
| Type | Same (the tool states no type, so it must be added) |
| Definition | Not the same (the tool states no measure) |
| Year | Not the same (the tool states no year) |
| Scope | Not the same (the tool states neither per household nor per person) |
| Source | Not given |
| Rewrite | In 20XX, for the gross floor area per household in private residential in one district, the official statistic is written differently from what the tool says; the tool's "about YY square metres" states no year, no measure and no scope, has not been verified, and is not used. |

> Every value, year, table number and department above is a **fictional example** written for this exercise. None of it represents a real statistic, a real year or a published figure.

**Common answers and responses:**

- **The sentence reads only "the average Hong Kong household lives in about XX square metres".** Response: the type, the year and the measure are all missing, so the sentence cannot be verified; write the four in full and look the figure up again.
- **Only the department and the figure are recorded.** Response: with no year, no table number and no unit and definition, the set counts as unfinished.
- **A gross floor area figure is compared directly with a saleable area figure.** Response: the two measures are not the same quantity; settle on one measure before comparing.
- **All four items are marked "same" without being read.** Response: mark each one, especially scope, and state whether it is per household or per person and whether it covers the whole territory or one district.
- **The rewrite fills in a plausible-looking number.** Response: where the claim cannot be verified, write that it has not been verified and fill in no number; the rewrite still needs the type, the measure and the year.
