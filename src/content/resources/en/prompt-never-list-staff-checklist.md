> An incident rarely starts with the tool. It starts with the paragraph that was pasted in. This page does one thing: five questions to answer before pressing send. Six categories, three rewriting methods, three exceptions — all on one sheet.

## What this solves

When a colleague pastes a paragraph into a tool, they are usually not taking a risk. They are short of time: producing a worksheet, rewording an explanation, tidying a record into a report. Nothing warns them. The tool is not broken and the colleague was not careless — nobody asked, at that moment, **who is in this paragraph?**

The school already has a document saying what may and may not go in. What is missing is **the five seconds before it goes out**: something that can sit beside the photocopier, be explained once in a meeting, and be in everyone's hand while the meeting runs.

That is what this page supplies: **six categories of data that never go in, three rewriting methods done in order, three exceptions that need written approval, and a one-page five-column checklist.** It replaces no school document and rewrites no existing requirement. It turns "five questions before sending" into one sheet.

**Why "never", not "handle carefully".** "Carefully" is an attitude. Everyone agrees with it in the meeting and there is no next step back at the desk. "Never" is a line that can be answered yes or no on the spot: yes means stop, and only no goes on to rewriting. The whole page is built around that line.

**Three statements to read first:**

1. **The names "six categories of data", "three rewriting methods" and "the one-page checklist" are this resource's suggestion, not something the Education Bureau has named.** They rest on two things the documents do ask for — **protection of personal data** and **professional judgement** — and the documents set no number of categories, no number of methods and no column headings.
2. **This page is not legal advice, and does not replace the school's personal information collection statement or its information security guidelines.** Which data may be handled, who approves it and how long it is kept are settled by the school's own documents and the colleague responsible for them. This page covers only the step before input.
3. **The Primary Information and Innovation Technology Curriculum Framework is a primary trial version.** This page covers P1–S6; the secondary stage uses the same set of terms but has no equivalent official framework, so the page does not present that framework as a secondary requirement and does not quote its items.

**Four things this page does not do:**

- **It cites no section number of any ordinance.** Where a rule comes from the school's documents, the page says so and leaves the wording to the colleague responsible.
- **It names no platform, brand, supplier or shop.** The phrase throughout is "the tool the school has designated".
- **It uses no real data in an example.** Every example on the page and in the attachments is invented: no real name, student number, telephone number, address, account, password or internal system link.
- **It demonstrates no tool.** No screen, button or setting appears; the page is about the five questions before input.

## Purpose

**Scope.** This page applies to every member of staff: teachers, teaching assistants, administrative colleagues, library and IT colleagues, and anyone who handles school or pupil data with a tool as part of their work, including trainees and supply staff. It is material for one staff meeting, and a card for the wall beside the photocopier.

After one meeting, a school can:

1. Read the six categories through once, and name the one its own daily work touches most.
2. Take one invented example through de-identification, abstraction and fictionalisation in that order, and say what separates the three steps.
3. Name the three exceptions that need written approval, and the four things the approval record must carry.
4. Use the one-page five-column checklist to decide, on the spot, whether a sentence may go out.

The page is written to the three learning strands of AI literacy — **the basic operation of AI**, **human-machine communication and collaboration**, and **reflection on the application of AI** — and takes nurturing the person, literacy first, application-oriented and technology for good as its values. It sits between **critical thinking** and **responsibility**: critical thinking is being able to ask who is in this paragraph, and responsibility is asking it when nobody is watching.

## How to use it

The attachments are two Word documents: **the one-page five-column checklist** (one per colleague, to copy and laminate) and **the meeting notes on the six categories and three rewriting methods** (one for whoever chairs, covering 45–60 minutes).

### Six categories that never go in (six, fixed)

The six are a working classification, **not legal definitions**. Read them one at a time, and for each ask one question: "is there anything of this kind in the sentence?"

| Category | What it is | What it covers (as printed) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · Pupil identity | Anything by which a particular pupil could be recognised | Name, student number, class and seat, photograph or face, voice |
| 2 · Pupil performance and support | Records of how a pupil is doing and what supports them | Marks, test and assessment records, special educational needs, medical and counselling records |
| 3 · Parents' and colleagues' personal data | The personal data of adults other than the pupil | Telephone, email, address, identity documents, salary and staff appraisal |
| 4 · Unpublished school documents | Material not yet released or not yet used | Unused papers and answer keys, meeting minutes, complaints and investigation notes, unannounced arrangements |
| 5 · Credentials and access | Anything that lets someone into a system | Accounts, passwords, one-time passwords, internal system links and codes |
| 6 · Third-party data under restriction | Data handed over by someone else, or bound by an agreement | Case material transferred by another organisation, content under a confidentiality agreement, other people's unpublished work |

Two sentences must be said together with the six, or the list gets copied as if it were exhaustive:

- **If one item falls into two categories, treat it as the stricter one.** A record carrying both a student number and counselling content is handled under category two, not category one.
- **If it is not clear which category it is, treat it as data that may not go in.** Hesitation already answers the question.

### Three rewriting methods (three, fixed, in order)

The three are **done in order, not chosen from**: de-identify first, then abstract, then fictionalise. The rewrite is finished only when all three are done.

| Order | Method | What is done | What is true afterwards |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | De-identify | Remove the name, student number, class and seat, and dates | The paragraph no longer points at one pupil (the school's existing document calls this step anonymisation; it is the same step) |
| 2 | Abstract | Remove subject marks, dates of events, numbers of people, places and other details that can be matched, leaving only the situation that has to be judged | The situation still makes sense, but it cannot be matched to one event |
| 3 | Fictionalise | Rewrite it as an invented example, and do not keep three or more matchable details at the same time | The paragraph can be used as an example, and demonstrated, safely |

Three notes:

- **Why it cannot be one of three.** De-identify only, and "Secondary 3, April, four people" still identifies the event. Fictionalise only, and the original details are still in the background. Each step removes one layer, and the order cannot be reversed.
- **"Three or more matchable details" is a ceiling, not a target.** Three details is already close to identifiable; fewer is better.
- **Run the six categories again after rewriting.** Rewriting is so that the sentence can go out, not so that the writer feels better.

### Three exceptions, and written approval (three, fixed)

Only three situations may go in, and **all three need written approval**:

| Exception | The situation | The approval record must carry |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A tool the school has designated, with its purpose already defined | The approver's post, the date, the purpose, the scope |
| 2 | A service with a signed processing agreement | The approver's post, the date, the purpose, the scope |
| 3 | A deployment used only inside the school's own systems, with nothing transmitted outside | The approver's post, the date, the purpose, the scope |

Three rules to state plainly in the meeting:

- **Parental consent is not an exception.** Consent is to take part in an activity or to display a piece of work; it is not consent to send that pupil's data to a third-party tool. **Never write "parental consent is enough to put it in."**
- **Verbal agreement, and "ok" in a message, do not count.** Exceptions require written approval; without a record there is no exception.
- **Approval is per occasion and per scope.** One approval covers one purpose and one scope; change either and it is approved again.

### The one-page five-column checklist (five columns, fixed)

One page, five columns, filled left to right, and **no column is skipped**. If column five cannot be answered, the sentence does not go out.

| Column | Ask yourself | What is written |
|---|---|---|
| 1 · What am I asking for | What the sentence is meant to produce | One line, for example "help me reword this explanation" |
| 2 · Whose data is in it | Who appears in the paragraph | The category (pupil / parent / colleague / another organisation); no names |
| 3 · Which category | Check against the six | Category one to six; if two, the stricter one; if unsure, "may not go in" |
| 4 · What it is after rewriting | Are the three steps done | One line each for de-identify, abstract, fictionalise; if unfinished, "not finished" |
| 5 · Who approved it | Is there an approval record | The approver's post, date, purpose, scope; if none, "none" |

Four rules, printed at the foot of the card:

- **The card records; it does not mark.** No score, level or ranking, and no personnel or conduct record.
- **Whoever chairs the meeting collects the cards**, and keeps them under the school's own retention arrangements. Not left on a wall, not left beside the photocopier overnight.
- **"May not go in" in column three means it does not go out.** That column is a line, not an opinion.
- **Exceptions are in writing.** With no approval record, column five reads "none" and the sentence does not go.

### The 45–60 minute meeting

It fits inside one staff meeting. The 45-minute version drops the second demonstration round and changes nothing else.

| Time | What happens | What the chair does |
|---|---|---|
| 5 min | **Opening**: this session is about the step before input, not about how to use a tool | State that the page replaces no school document |
| 10 min | **The six categories**: read each once, asking only "is there anything of this kind?" | Read out "two categories means the stricter one" and "not sure means it does not go in" |
| 15 min | **The three methods**: take one entirely invented example through de-identification, abstraction and fictionalisation | Pause after each step and ask which layer it removed |
| 10 min | **The three exceptions and written approval**: read the three, and fill the four fields of an approval record once | Say that parental consent is not an exception, and verbal agreement does not count |
| 10 min | **The five-column checklist**: fill one in as a demonstration, then hand the cards out | Demonstrate one case where column three reads "may not go in" |
| 5–10 min | **Close**: everyone reads the three closing sentences; the collection arrangement is announced | Collect the cards; note any post that needs follow-up, with no names |

**Two rules that do not change:** six categories means six, three methods means three, three exceptions means three, and the checklist has five columns. **Nothing is added or removed, and no sub-items are created.** The order of the three methods does not change.

### The teacher's and the office's four checks

1. **The chair chooses the example.** Every example is invented; no real case from the school is "lightly changed". An obviously fake example is better than a disguised real one.
2. **No tool is demonstrated.** No screen is opened, nobody logs in, no button is pressed; only the content of a prompt is read.
3. **"May not go in" is a line, not a reproach.** A colleague who answers yes is the moment the page is working, not a colleague who did something wrong.
4. **Exceptions go to the responsible colleague.** If someone believes an exception applies, it goes to the colleague responsible for personal data, under the school's documents; it is not approved in the meeting.

### Three closing sentences (read together)

1. "Who is in this sentence?"
2. "Which category is it?"
3. "Has it been rewritten — and who approved it in writing?"

Interfaces only; nothing here redoes their work:

- **The staff data-handling guide (what may and may not go into an AI tool)** covers the same subject for a different use: it is the school's standing guide, with seven negative items, the requirements it has to meet, what may go in and how to anonymise it, and four in-school procedures for the school to fill in. This page is a checklist for the step before input. **It does not rewrite that page's requirements or procedures, and the two pages use the same names for the same things** (for example "data that can identify a pupil", and anonymisation, which is this page's de-identification).
- **The personal data scenarios pack** works on situation judgement at pupil level; this page works on the boundary for staff input, and does not redo the scenarios.
- **The AI risk register and incident response** covers logging and reporting an incident; this page covers the step before one, and writes no register.
- **The AI budget and licensing worksheet** covers buying and licensing tools; this page's three exceptions say only that something is already approved, and cover no procurement.
