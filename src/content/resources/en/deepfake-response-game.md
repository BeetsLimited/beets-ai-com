## What this solves

On social platforms and in group chats, teenagers can receive a voice note or short clip that claims to be from their school — the office chasing a payment, a form teacher asking for personal details. Deepfake technology makes this kind of message **harder and harder to spot just by whether it sounds or looks right**.

This resource turns the response into a **screen-free sorting game**: pupils first receive a fictional school message, then sort five decision cards into the correct order of response, and finally hear a step-by-step debrief from the teacher. No device is needed at any point. It suits junior-secondary (S1–S3) Citizenship, Economics and Society teachers, and also works in a form-teacher period or a parent session.

> The scenario, and every person, school, phone and account number in it, is fictional.

## Learning objectives

By the end, pupils can:

1. Say that the first thing to do with a suspicious message is to **pause**, not to act or forward it straight away.
2. Say that "check" means using a **second source outside the message** (such as the school's official number you already have), not the link or number inside the message.
3. Put the steps into the correct order: **pause → check → report**.
4. Point out that "share it on" spreads the fake to more people and should not be done.
5. Say in a sentence or two why a fake voice note or clip can be convincing.

## Teaching steps

1. **Hook (5 min)** — Ask: "Have you or your family ever received a suspicious message that claimed to be from your school?" Collect the experiences without judging them. Write "pause, check, report" on the board.
2. **Play the scenario (5 min)** — Project or read aloud the fictional message from the files (a roughly 20-second voice note or clip that claims to be from the school office). Give no hints yet; ask each pupil to note what they would do.
3. **Group sorting (12 min)** — Give each group a set of five decision cards (pause / check a second source / report / ignore / share it on). Task: put the things you *should* do in the correct order, set the things you *should not* do aside, and say why.
4. **Whole-class checking (8 min)** — Check against the answer key in the files. Focus the discussion on why "ignore" alone is not enough and why "share it on" is the most harmful.
5. **Debrief (7 min)** — Use the "why a fake can be convincing" section to explain briefly (without technical detail) why deepfake messages persuade.
6. **Wrap-up (3 min)** — Read the chant together: "Stop, ask, tell a grown-up."

## Answer key and marking notes

**The correct order**

| Order | Card | Marking notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pause | Do not pay, do not hand over details, do not forward. A pupil who can say "stop and do nothing first" meets the mark. |
| 2 | Check a second source | Ring the school office on the official number or use the official website you already have — **not** the number in the message. |
| 3 | Report | Tell a parent, a teacher or another trusted adult, and report the message. |

**What not to do**

| Card | Why it is wrong |
|---|---|
| Ignore | You would not be tricked yourself, but nobody would know the school is being impersonated; the school and other parents need to know so they can warn everyone else. |
| Share it on | Most harmful. Forwarding helps spread the fake to more people, so more people can be tricked. |

**Open question: why can a fake be convincing?** A good answer includes at least two of:

- A voice and a face can be imitated, so it sounds or looks like a real person.
- Urgency ("by today", "your place will be cancelled") rushes people so they have no time to think.
- It uses a familiar identity (the school, a form teacher, the office) to lower a person's guard.
