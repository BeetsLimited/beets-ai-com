## What this solves

Where Hong Kong people live, and how much space they have, is something pupils see every day and almost never check. Ask an AI one question and it produces a confident answer — a floor area, a share, a trend — usually with no source at all.

This lesson hands pupils one such answer and asks them to **check it line by line**. First work out what each sentence is actually claiming (an area? a share? a trend?). Then find the matching official figure. Then sort each sentence into one of three boxes: **supported by the official figure**, **contradicted by it**, or **impossible to check** (no source, incompatible units, or an estimate stated as a fact). Finally the class redraws a corrected comparison chart from the official figures and reports where the AI went wrong.

It is one lesson inside the junior-secondary data-and-charts unit, and shows how professional gatekeeping works in a maths classroom: when an AI states a figure confidently and without a source, that is an AI hallucination, and the only way to remove it is to fact-check it against the official published statistics. The lesson also fits the reflection on the application of AI strand of a school's digital education plan, keeps critical discernment in view, and reminds pupils not to drift into over-reliance on the tool — measuring, converting units and reading a table stay in their own hands.

## Learning objectives

Pupils will be able to:

1. Break an AI's account into checkable sentences and name the kind of quantity each one claims (an area, a share, or a trend).
2. Find the matching **official** figure for each sentence (Census and Statistics Department, Housing Bureau / Housing Department, Rating and Valuation Department) and record the source and its year.
3. Mark each sentence as supported by the official figure, contradicted by it, or impossible to check, and give a reason.
4. Redraw a comparison chart from the official figures and describe in one sentence how it differs from what the AI said.

Written for the junior-secondary stage, it covers the reflection on the application of AI strand of AI literacy, with human judgement first and the tool second.

## Teaching steps

1. **Hook (5 minutes)** — Project "the AI's account" from the worksheet and ask: does this read as trustworthy? If you had to prove it right or wrong, what would you go and find?
2. **Split and label (8 minutes)** — As a class, split the account into 6-8 sentences and label what each one claims. Model one: turn "the average living space in Hong Kong is about X square metres" into "a claim about an area that does not say which kind of housing it means".
3. **Check (15 minutes, in groups)** — Each group takes 2-3 sentences, finds the matching figure in the official sources below, and fills in the worksheet's checking table: sentence → official figure → source and year → judgement.
4. **Redraw the chart (8 minutes)** — Plot a line or bar chart from the official figures, label the source on the chart, and set the AI's number beside the official one.
5. **Report and close (4 minutes)** — Each group reports in one sentence: "The claim we checked was ... the official figure shows ... so the AI's statement is ...".

## Answer key and marking notes

**What this worksheet checks is not "did the AI get it right", but "can this sentence be checked at all".**

A typical AI answer shows the five problems below. Pupils meet the standard if they identify them and can explain each one:

| Kind of problem | What pupils should point out |
|---|---|
| No source | Nothing in the passage says where any figure comes from. A figure with no source is already the first warning sign. |
| An estimate stated as a fact | It talks about "the next few years" in the present tense, confusing what has happened with a forecast. |
| Inconsistent quantities and units | It switches between square feet and square metres inside one paragraph, and never says which kind of housing it means (public rental, subsidised sale or private), so the numbers cannot be compared. |
| An average presented as "typical" | One "average" is used to stand for everybody, with no indication whether it is a mean or a median, and no statement of who is being averaged. This is a first example of data bias. |
| Public and private housing conflated | A question about one kind of housing is answered with a whole-market figure, so the conclusion sounds right while answering a different question. |

**Marking is process-based** — every check needs a source:

| Check item | Meets the standard | Needs work |
|---|---|---|
| Splitting the account | Every sentence says what kind of quantity it claims | Only "the AI is wrong" |
| Source | Every sentence has an official source name and year | "I found it online", or another AI's answer |
| Judgement | All three judgements are used, each with a reason | Everything marked "cannot tell", or everything marked "wrong" |
| Chart | Redrawn from the official figures, with the source on the chart | The AI's numbers plotted straight across |
| Conclusion | One sentence that says where the difference lies | Restating the question |

> The key judgement: **"impossible to check" is a legitimate answer, and the one pupils skip.** They tend to choose between right and wrong; this lesson is about noticing that a missing source, incompatible units or an unstated denominator already make a sentence uncheckable — which is exactly where an AI hallucination hides.

> If pupils want to copy the AI's numbers down, remind them: the "AI's account" in this worksheet was written for the exercise and is not a source. The official tables are what gets checked.
