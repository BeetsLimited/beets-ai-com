> The same task, the same learning objective, and a route that is allowed to differ. What changes is the way through, not the standard — and no pupil should have to prove anything for that to happen.

## What this solves

Once AI tools arrive in classrooms, two things tend to go wrong at the same time: "differentiation" quietly becomes "a lower standard", and everything a teacher notices about a pupil ends up written down in another document.

- **The task gets replaced instead of the route.** A group ends up doing a different, easier piece of work for ever — and nobody ever assesses that easier piece against the original learning objective.
- **The pupil is asked to explain themselves first.** The arrangement only happens once a pupil describes what they cannot do, or a parent hands over evidence. A pupil who needs support then pays for a tool with a disclosure.
- **Support information is left in too many places.** Class notes, group chats and the tool's input box all carry the same pupil's details, and six months later nobody can say which copy still exists.

This page does three things only: **five principles, four kinds of adjustment, and a one-page card.** It does not set the curriculum, and it does not handle an individual pupil's assessment or records.

**The boundary first.** The page uses one phrase throughout — pupils with additional support needs — and names no category, no diagnosis and no strength or weakness; that list does not belong to this page, and it certainly does not belong on a sheet a teacher picks up in a hurry. The five principles, the four adjustments and the card's fields are a suggestion from this resource, **not a requirement set by the Education Bureau**, and a school may adapt them. The card is unnamed, carries no comment on ability or progress, and is not an assessment report or an individual plan; it is not a pupil record of any kind.

## Purpose

Once it is set up, a teacher will be able to:

1. Hold the same learning objective with five principles: the same task, no disclosure required, support rather than substitution, an exit option, and minimal data.
2. Adjust within one part of a lesson using four kinds of adjustment — input, output, pace and assessment — three options in each.
3. Leave a one-page five-column card that records what was adjusted and who knows, without recording a judgement about the pupil.
4. Explain the arrangement to a parent in three sentences and hand over a one-page parent version, with no evidence required from the family.
5. Keep the teacher's own gatekeeping clear: which step the teacher decides, which step the teacher records, which step the teacher closes.

The page sits inside one strand of AI literacy — **reflection on the application of AI**: whether a tool is being used well is not a question about how fast it is, but about whether the pupil is still doing the same task and still doing the thinking.

## How to use it

The file is one Word document in five parts: the five principles, the four adjustments, the one-page five-column card (photocopiable on its own), three sentences and a page for parents, and the teacher's gatekeeping and close-out. Set it aside as one 60-minute session for teachers.

### Step 1: five principles, and only five

The order is fixed. The first sets the direction; the other four are what makes it possible.

| Order | Principle | In one line | Where it usually goes wrong |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The same task | What changes is the route through the same task, not the task itself | The task is swapped for an easier one, and the learning objective is left behind |
| 2 | No disclosure required | A pupil is never asked what support they need, and a parent is never asked for evidence | An arrangement that only happens after someone explains themselves |
| 3 | Support, not substitution | The tool helps the pupil finish; it does not think in their place | A generated answer handed in as the pupil's own |
| 4 | An exit option | A pupil may choose not to use the tool, and may return to the original method at any point | Not using the tool is treated as non-cooperation |
| 5 | Minimal data | Only the least data the task needs goes in | A pupil's circumstances typed into a prompt, or into a group chat |

The fourth one has to be written down and said to pupils: **the exit option is the default, not a concession.** If a pupil does not use the tool, the teacher asks nothing and records nothing.

### Step 2: four kinds of adjustment, and only four

The order is fixed, with three options in each. **One part of a lesson takes no more than two kinds** — with three, the adjustment has become a different piece of work.

| Kind | What it adjusts | Three options |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Input | How the pupil receives the task | Read the task aloud; break a long task into parts; give a spoken demonstration first |
| 2 Output | How the pupil hands the answer in | Say it instead of writing it; answer with a diagram or a table; hand it in in parts |
| 3 Pace | How much time, in how many sittings | Split it across two lessons; build in a break; do half, then decide the next step |
| 4 Assessment | How you see whether the pupil got there | Look at the process, not only the product; allow a spoken answer; mark the same task presented another way |

All twelve options point at the same learning objective, which is why **the four kinds are not stacked**: two is already enough for one part of a lesson, and a third usually means the task itself has been replaced. Where a tool genuinely helps, it is mostly in kinds one and two; kinds three and four are the teacher's decision, not a feature of any tool.

### Step 3: the one-page five-column card

One page, photocopiable on its own, five fixed columns. What is recorded is the part of the lesson, not the pupil.

| Column | What goes in it | What does not |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Class and part of the lesson | Which class, which lesson, which task | No pupil's name, seat number or any identifying description |
| 2 What the task asked for | What the task asked every pupil to do | Not "the easier version" |
| 3 Support already in place | What was already there in that part of the lesson: seating, grouping, time, materials | No category and no diagnosis |
| 4 The adjustment for this part | Which two of the four kinds, and which two options | No comment on the pupil's ability or performance |
| 5 Who knows, and where it is kept | Which posts know about the arrangement; where this page lives and when it is destroyed | Not "relevant parties informed" |

Column five is the reason the card exists: it answers "who knows". If no post can be named there, exactly one teacher knows, and the next colleague to take the class starts from nothing.

**The card is unnamed, carries no comment on ability or progress, and is not an assessment report, an individual plan or a counselling record.** One card per lesson, handed to a named post at the end and kept under the school's own retention arrangements; not on a wall, and not left by the copier overnight.

### Step 4: three sentences, and a page for parents

When explaining the arrangement, use three sentences. Anything beyond them is answered by the parent page.

1. "Your child is doing the same task and the same learning objective; only the way through it can differ."
2. "You do not need to provide any evidence or explain your child's circumstances — the arrangement is decided from what the school sees in class."
3. "Your child may choose not to use the tool, and may go back to the original method at any point, without having to explain why."

The one-page parent version turns the three sentences into a short note and adds two lines: one saying this is a school arrangement and not a requirement set by the Education Bureau, and one giving the class teacher as the contact for a change. Print it single-sided, send it with the parent letter, and hold no separate session.

### Step 5: the teacher's gatekeeping, and close-out

- **Gate 1: has the task been replaced?** Is the same learning objective still standing? If you cannot answer, it is not an adjustment but a different piece of work.
- **Gate 2: is anyone being asked to disclose?** Does this step need a pupil or a parent to explain something first? If it does, go back to deciding from what you see in class.
- **Gate 3: has anything gone into a prompt?** Every input box gets only the least data the task needs.
- **Gate 4: is there a post in column five?** If you cannot name one, then nobody knows.
- **Close-out: at the end of the lesson, one question.** "Is this pupil still doing the same task today?" That question is the whole of the close-out.

**The choice of adjustments, the way the card is filled and the wording of the three sentences are a suggestion.** A school may change the wording to suit itself, but the order of the five principles, the order of the four kinds, and the rule that one part of a lesson takes no more than two kinds are best left alone — they are why this fits on one page.

### How the 60 minutes is spent

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 10 minutes | The five principles: read each one, and confirm that the fourth — the exit option — is the default, not an exception |
| 10 minutes | The four kinds: read all four and the twelve options, and confirm the two-kind limit |
| 15 minutes | The card: fill one page together for a fictional part of a lesson, spending the time on column five |
| 15 minutes | The three sentences and the parent page: read them, then agree how the page goes out and who is named as the contact |
| 10 minutes | Gatekeeping and close-out: read the four gates and the single question, and announce where cards are collected |

All of it inside 60 minutes, with no workshop. Every example is fictional and has nothing to do with any real school, pupil or family.

## Review checklist

Before the session ends, check each item:

- All five principles are present, in order, and "the exit option is the default" is written in.
- All four kinds of adjustment are present, in order, with three options each — twelve in total.
- The two-kind limit for one part of a lesson is written in.
- The card has five columns and no sixth has been added.
- No category, diagnosis or strength or weakness is named anywhere; the page uses pupils with additional support needs and nothing else.
- Nothing asks a pupil to disclose what support they need, and nothing asks a parent for evidence.
- The card is unnamed, carries no comment on ability or progress, and is not an assessment report or an individual plan.
- Every example is fictional, and no support information has been turned into an example or typed into a prompt.
- The page states that this is not a requirement set by the Education Bureau, and that the principles, the adjustments and the card are a school-level suggestion.
- All three sentences and the one-page parent version are present, and the parent page names the arrangement as a school one and gives a contact.
- No tool make, model, supplier or service organisation is named anywhere.
- Where the cards are collected, and by which post, is written down.

## Official documents

Every resource in this library is written to the AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools and falls within the policy scope of the Blueprint for Digital Education Development in Primary and Secondary Schools.

This resource maps to:

- **Blueprint for Digital Education Development in Primary and Secondary Schools** (three-fold leaflet, p.2): overall objective 3, enhancing infrastructure and resource provision, asks for a digital education environment that is safe and efficient. This page puts "safe" into a single classroom adjustment, and what reaches the pupil is an unnamed card.
- **Supplement I: AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools**: listed here by name only. This page is about safeguarding arrangements, not about curriculum planning, so it fills in no learning point, no unit name and no tier — those belong to the school's own curriculum documents.
- **Supplement II: Guide to Using AI in Teaching in Primary and Secondary Schools** (pp.2-2 to 2-5): asks that teaching need come first, that the teacher be the professional gatekeeper, and that differences in learning be looked after. The four adjustments are that last requirement, landing in four places inside one part of a lesson.
- **EDB Circular No. 156/2026** (Appendix 1, section 4): asks schools to look after learning diversity and educational equity — using AI to push personalised content that meets differences in learning, while making sure every pupil, including those with additional support needs and those from different socio-economic backgrounds, can access and use digital learning resources on equal terms. That paragraph is the direct basis for the five principles: equal access starts from a route that may differ while the standard does not. The exact wording is quoted verbatim in the Chinese edition.
- **EDB Circular No. 156/2026** (Appendix 2, item 3, learning and teaching section): asks for differentiation — using AI to provide personalised learning resources, and giving pupils with additional support needs appropriate support, a read-aloud option being the example given — so that education stays equitable and inclusive, and materials stay accessible. The read-aloud and spoken-answer options in kinds one and two are where that example lands here.
- **EDB Circular No. 156/2026** (Appendix 3, unit 6): the table fields in that unit cover accessible design, differentiation in teaching, and universal design for learning. This page does not rewrite the unit; it takes only the direction all three share — one objective, several routes — and turns it into the four kinds of adjustment.
