> What parents most want to know is not which tool the school uses. It is two things: how my child will be treated, and whether I can say no.

## What this solves

Once a school decides to use AI in class, the questions reach the form teacher within the same week. Is my child using AI for homework? Will it keep records about my child? Can I refuse? And if I refuse, what happens to my child then?

Three common responses all leave the same damage behind:

- **A letter with no questions answered.** However well it is written, parents ring up one by one, and each colleague who answers says something slightly different.
- **Answering with "the Education Bureau requires it".** Calling a school-based practice an official requirement makes it impossible to change later, and it does not survive a parent asking to see the clause.
- **Consent with no arrangement for saying no.** The letter explains consent but not what happens afterwards, so parents ask each other in group chats and the school explains it again, with a new version each time.

This resource puts four things together: **a letter to parents, nine frequently asked questions each with an official basis, consent and non-participation arrangements, and home guidance written separately for junior and senior primary**, plus a one-page A4 consent form that can be photocopied.

**One thing must be said first.** The official documents this resource draws on — the Blueprint for Digital Education Development in Primary and Secondary Schools, the AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools, and the Guide to Using AI in Teaching in Primary and Secondary Schools — contain **no direct clause on parental consent**. The consent arrangements below are **this resource's suggestion for schools to adopt**; they are not an Education Bureau requirement. A school may adopt, change or decline them, and should say in its letter that the arrangement is the school's own.

## Purpose

With this pack, a form teacher and the school can:

1. Use a two-page letter to state how the school **does** use AI in class, and how it **does not**.
2. Answer the questions parents actually ask from nine prepared replies, each pointing at a document the parent can look up rather than at what the school happens to think.
3. Write consent and non-participation as an arrangement that can be carried out: what a non-participating pupil does, who arranges it, and how it is recorded.
4. Give home guidance separately for junior and senior primary, because the same instruction has the opposite effect in P1 and in P6.
5. Collect parents' decisions on one A4 page and file them by class.

## How to use it

### Step 1: Write the letter (about 30 minutes)

The attachment has the letter laid out in four paragraphs; the school fills in its name, the date and the contact.

1. **Why we use it** — not because it is the trend, but because the school's curriculum and development plan are meant to build pupils' AI literacy. One sentence is enough; no clause-quoting.
2. **How we use it** — in which subjects, in what kind of activity, and who is accountable. **Write only what has been decided.** Anything undecided (the number of lessons, the tool's name) should stay out of the letter, because whatever is written has to be delivered.
3. **How we do not use it** — AI will not replace the teacher's assessment decision; identifiable pupil data will not be entered into an unapproved tool; pupils will not be asked to present a tool's output as their own writing.
4. **How you can ask** — the form teacher, the subject head and the school's contact details, and a note that the consent form comes with the letter.

Write to parents, not to the Bureau. Avoid "literacy", "empowerment" and "ecosystem"; use "in class", "homework", "records".

**Three things not to write:**

- **No named commercial tool, and no demonstration link.** Tools change and the letter does not get reprinted; naming one means explaining the change to parents later.
- **No promise about lesson time that has not been decided.** "Two lessons a week" in a letter becomes a parent's expectation.
- **No promised outcome.** "Raises attainment" and "guarantees progress" cannot be verified, and they invite comparison between children.

### Step 2: Choose the nine questions (about 20 minutes)

The attachment has nine. A school may add or drop, but **every one needs a basis**; a question with no basis is better deleted than answered with "the school considers".

**Table 1: nine questions parents ask, and the basis for each answer**

> In this table the three documents are shortened to the Blueprint, the Framework and the Guide; their full titles are in "Official documents" below.

| A parent asks | A reply that holds up | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Why does the school use AI in class at all? | It is part of the curriculum and the school development plan: digital and AI education has to be built into the development plan and delivered across subjects | Blueprint, p.2 |
| Will my child use AI to do homework? | Depends on the subject and the unit. Tasks are designed so that pupils show their own thinking; the tool is an aid | Guide, pp.2-2 to 2-5 |
| Will the school use AI to mark my child? | The assessment decision is the teacher's; anything a tool produces is checked by the teacher before it is used as feedback | Guide, pp.2-2 to 2-5 |
| Will my child's personal data be entered into an AI tool? | The school does not enter identifiable pupil data into an unapproved tool; staff follow the school's data-handling requirements | Guide, pp.2-4 to 2-9 |
| Can I choose for my child not to take part? | Yes. The school arranges a non-AI activity with the same learning objective; grades and conduct are unaffected | School arrangement (not an official clause) |
| Will my child's work be displayed? | Display needs separate consent; declining does not affect learning or grades | School arrangement (not an official clause) |
| What can I do at home? | Junior and senior primary differ; see the next section | Framework, pp.1-6 to 1-10 |
| What if I find my child using an unsuitable tool? | Tell the form teacher. The school first finds out what was used and why, and handles it as an educational matter rather than a disciplinary one | School arrangement (not an official clause) |
| Which tool does the school use? | The tool is selected through the school's own procedure and named in the letter to parents; this page names no product | School arrangement (not an official clause) |

Keep the words "school arrangement". They tell a colleague which sentences they may reword, and which ones require telling parents again if changed.

### Step 3: Deal with non-participation (about 15 minutes)

The consent form has a "do not agree" option. Without an arrangement behind it, that option just hands the problem to the form teacher.

- **Non-participation is not absence.** The pupil stays in the room and does the same learning objective without AI. The subject teacher prepares it; parents are not asked to supply tools or make it up at home.
- **The alternative has to be genuinely equivalent.** If the AI version is "use the tool to organise material from three sources", the non-AI version is "do the same with three photocopied sources" — not "copy out your notes again".
- **Record it on the class arrangement sheet, not on the report card or the conduct column.** Taking part or not is an arrangement, not a behaviour.
- **Do not ask the pupil to explain why in class.** No pupil should have to justify a parent's decision to classmates.

### Step 4: Write home guidance separately for junior and senior primary (about 20 minutes)

The same instruction has opposite effects at the two ends of primary school: juniors need a parent beside them, seniors need a parent beside them who does not take over.

**Junior primary (P1-P3)**

- **A parent sits alongside, on the same screen.** One device each does not work at this age.
- **The parent types; the child says what to ask.** Typing is the adult's job; saying the question clearly is the child's practice.
- **Guess first, then ask.** Before each question, the child says what they think the answer is; afterwards the two are compared.
- **No AI-written sentences.** The child writes first, then compares their own sentence with the tool's and says which sounds more like them.
- **When an answer looks wrong, look it up together.** This is the main lesson at this age: the tool is sometimes wrong, and checking is normal.

**Senior primary (P4-P6)**

- **The child operates it; the parent watches without answering for them.** The parent's job is to watch the process, not the product.
- **Ask the child to show the process.** What they asked, which words they changed, why they trust the answer. If they cannot show the process, the work has not been done.
- **Three things not to do**: do not enter your own or a classmate's personal data; do not present the tool's words as your own; do not use it for homework that requires first-hand observation — measuring, interviewing, experimenting.
- **Check one answer together.** Find a second source and see where the two differ; that session is worth more than ten more questions.

### Step 5: Send out and collect the consent form (about 10 minutes)

The form is one A4 page with **four columns**: consent to take part in learning, consent to display work, date signed, school contact. Photocopy it, or send it electronically with the letter; the form teacher files the returned forms by class, and reviews them once before the year ends.

Three points:

- **"Do not agree" is a complete answer and needs no reason.** Requiring a reason turns a right into an application.
- **The two consents are separate.** A parent may agree to the learning and decline the display; merging them into one "I agree to take part" leaves only all or nothing.
- **The school contact column names a person and a number.** A parent needs to know who to ring, not just "the school".

## Review checklist

| Check | Done properly | Needs work |
|---|---|---|
| The letter | All four paragraphs, including "how we do not use it" | Benefits and vision only |
| Named tools | No commercial product or demonstration link anywhere in the pack | A brand, an app icon or a promotional link |
| Promises | No promised lesson counts, tools or outcomes | "Two lessons a week" or "raises attainment" |
| Questions | Nine, each with a document to look up or marked as a school arrangement | All answered with "the school considers" |
| Non-participation | An alternative task, a way to record it, and a named person | "Parents may opt out" and nothing more |
| Consent form | Two separate consents, with date and school contact | One "I agree to take part" covering everything |
| Wording | Junior and senior primary written separately | One paragraph for the whole school |
| Boundary | Page and attachment both say the consent arrangements are not an Education Bureau requirement | A school arrangement presented as an official rule |

> The test: **after reading it, a parent knows how their child will be treated, and what happens if they say no.** Miss either and the letter is only a notice.
