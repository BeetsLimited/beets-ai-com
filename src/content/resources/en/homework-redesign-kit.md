## What this solves

Now that AI is common, an assignment that only asks students to "find the answer" can be completed by copying a classmate, searching online, or handing it straight to an AI. Teachers mark only the finished product, and cannot see what the student understood or where they got stuck.

This kit does not ask you to ban AI. It shows a more practical move: **rewrite the assignment** so that, while keeping the same learning objective and the same time required, you add four elements — quote evidence, use your own words, give a personal reason, and explain briefly in class. Copying or AI-generated work stops being useful, and the student's thinking becomes visible.

The kit contains two complete before-and-after assignment examples (a Chinese Language reading response and a General Studies data task), plus a homework-redesign checklist you can apply to any existing assignment.

## Learning objectives

By the end, teachers can:

1. Name three common features of "copy-friendly" assignments and spot them in their own tasks.
2. Use the four moves (quote evidence, use your own words, give a personal reason, explain orally) to rewrite an existing assignment into a "thinking-visible" version.
3. Check after rewriting: has the learning objective been kept? Has the time required changed?
4. Use the homework-redesign checklist to review their own rewrite.

## Teaching steps

A self-guided flow of about 40 minutes (individual, or as a subject-panel planning session):

1. **Name the problem (5 min)** — Read the "before" version of Example 1 and ask: could a student copy this or hand it to an AI? Which question is easiest to have done for them?
2. **Compare the rewrite (10 min)** — Read the "after" version and the "what changed" notes, and circle each change that makes thinking visible.
3. **Second example (10 min)** — Read Example 2 (the General Studies data task) the same way.
4. **Apply it to your own assignment (15 min)** — Take one of your existing assignments, work through the homework-redesign checklist, and write a new version.

## Answer key and marking notes

The two before-and-after pairs below are the "answer key" of this kit — they show what a good rewrite looks like, and what a good student answer to the rewritten task contains.

### Example 1 — Chinese Language reading response (P5)

**Before (the version teachers commonly set)**

> Read the passage chosen by the teacher, then answer:
> 1. What is the main idea of the passage?
> 2. What rhetorical device does the writer use? Give one example.
> 3. Do you agree with the writer's view?

**After (same learning objective, same time required)**

> 1. State the main idea in one sentence, then circle the one sentence in the passage that best supports what you wrote.
> 2. Find one rhetorical device, copy the original sentence, and explain in your own words: what does this device make the reader feel?
> 3. Do you agree with the writer's view? Give one reason of your own — a reason the passage does not already state directly.
> 4. (Next lesson) In 30 seconds, explain your reason for Question 3 to a partner, who may ask one follow-up question.

**What changed, and why it makes thinking visible**

| Change | Why |
|---|---|
| Q1 asks for the supporting sentence | The student must go back to the text to find evidence; an AI can state the main idea, but "which sentence you circled" shows whether the student actually read |
| Q2 asks for an explanation "in your own words" | The answer cannot be copied from a model answer; it must be paraphrased, and the quality of the paraphrase shows understanding |
| Q3 asks for a personal reason not already in the text | It forces a genuine response; an AI gives generic answers, while a specific personal reason is hard to ghost-write |
| Q4 adds an oral explanation and a follow-up question | A copied answer falls apart under a follow-up question; the student must genuinely understand |

**What a good answer contains (what to look for when marking)**: (1) the quoted sentence is genuinely relevant to the main idea; (2) the "own words" explanation differs from the original but is correct in meaning; (3) the personal reason is specific and tied to the text; (4) the oral explanation survives a follow-up question without stalling.

### Example 2 — General Studies data task (P6)

**Before (the version teachers commonly set)**

> Research Hong Kong's visitor arrivals in recent years, draw a bar chart, and write down your observations.

**After (same learning objective, same time required)**

> 1. From the two sources the teacher provides, copy three years' visitor-arrival figures from each, and write the source and year next to each figure.
> 2. Compare the two sources for the same year: is anything different? Write down the difference you found.
> 3. Draw the bar chart, then write one sentence on "one thing this chart tells me", quoting a specific part of your own chart.
> 4. Write one question you want to ask: what does this chart NOT tell you?

**What changed, and why it makes thinking visible**

| Change | Why |
|---|---|
| Record the source and year for each figure | Every figure must have a provenance; copying or AI cannot explain "where you got it" |
| Compare two sources and find a difference | This is a checking behaviour that shows whether the student really verified |
| The observation must quote the student's own chart | The conclusion must be tied to the student's own work, not a generic restatement |
| Ask what the chart does not show | This forces higher-order thinking; an AI can describe a trend but struggles with the limits of the data |

**What a good answer contains (what to look for when marking)**: (1) every figure has a traceable source and year; (2) the student can name a specific difference between the two sources; (3) the observation quotes a specific year or value from the chart; (4) the follow-up question is about something the chart genuinely does not cover (not a question the student already knows the answer to).

> Note: the figures in Example 2 come from the sources the teacher supplies; this kit does not assume any real statistics, so nothing goes out of date or misleads.

## Homework-redesign checklist

```text
□ Learning objective — before rewriting, write down "what this assignment is for"; does the rewrite still reach it?
□ Quote evidence — does it ask the student to point to or quote a sentence, a figure or a source, rather than only give a conclusion?
□ Use own words — does it ask for a paraphrase or explanation, rather than a copy?
□ Personal reason — does it ask for a personal response that cannot be copied straight from the material?
□ Oral explanation — does it ask the student to explain a small part aloud next lesson (so copying is hard to hide)?
□ Checking — does it ask the student to compare sources, find a contradiction, or mark "I am not sure"?
□ Time — is the time required after rewriting about the same as before?
□ AI rules — does it state clearly whether students may use AI, and how they must declare it if they do?
```
