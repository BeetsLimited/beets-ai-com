**The records on this page mean official records the Observatory has already published and that anyone can look up again; what the sheet holds is the pupil's own copying and checking. The two are not the same document.**

## What this solves

Start with the familiar scene: a pupil working on a climate project asks a tool how much rain Hong Kong gets in a year. The answer reads fluently, the figures are tidy, and sometimes an explanation comes with it. The pupil copies the figures into the project. The teacher sees three figures and cannot see which year they belong to, which table they came from, or in what unit they were given. Nothing looks wrong with the number itself; what is wrong is that it cannot be looked up again.

The second gap is year and unit. The same record can be given in millimetres or in centimetres; it can be a whole year or a single month; and figures from two different years placed in one comparison become a claim nobody has checked. This is where tool answers most often fail: the value is right and the year is wrong, or the value and the unit have been taken from two different tables.

The third gap is the verdict. Asked which account can be used, the usual answer is "they are about the same". Without a verdict the checking leaves no result, and the record sheet is left holding a row of numbers set side by side.

This page does one thing only: a three-column record sheet, four kinds of difference, and one verdict sentence. It does not teach tool operation, does not compare one tool with another, does not mark answers right or wrong, does not rank, and does not deal with the writing of the project. It never asks for tool output, screenshots or account details to be handed in, uploaded or photocopied. The page contains no real figure at all, and every example is labelled as fictional.

When a tool states a figure confidently and it cannot be looked up anywhere, that is an AI hallucination; the most direct way to verify it is to ask one more question — which year, which table, which unit. The page also reminds pupils not to drift into over-reliance: copying the figure, reading the unit and writing the verdict are all done by hand.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, pupils can:

1. Find three figures in the Hong Kong Observatory's published records, and write down for each one the year, the page name or table number, and the unit (the three columns of the record sheet).
2. Put the same question to the school-approved tool, set the tool's figures beside their own, and copy both sides as they stand without rewriting either.
3. Circle one of four kinds of difference for each figure: a different number, a different year, a different unit, or no source at all.
4. Write one verdict sentence saying which account can be used, with a reason that names the year, the table number or the unit.
5. Explain why "the tool answered fluently" is not the same as "it has been verified", and write on the last line which column they will check first next time.

The page addresses the strand **reflection on the application of AI** at the upper primary key stage (P4-P6): what is being checked is not the tool but one figure it produced, and whether the pupil went back and found it. Three values are practised. **Critical thinking** asks "can I look this figure up" before the figure is used. **Being led by the evidence** means writing "not found" when it cannot be found, rather than supplying a number that looks reasonable. **Timely and appropriate use** means knowing which figure is worth stopping to cross-check and which can wait for the next lesson.

## How to use it

One 60-minute lesson. Three figures, three columns, four kinds of difference and one verdict sentence; the counts are fixed and nothing is added or removed.

### 1. Writing one question as three things (place, time, measure)

Before the record sheet comes one question, and one question asks about one measure only:

> Format: at ____ (place or station) in Hong Kong, in ____ (year and month), the value of ____ (the measure) is ____.

Fictional example: "At a station in Hong Kong, in July 20XX, the mean temperature is ____." The place, the year and the measure all have to be written to a standard that can be looked up before the question counts as finished.

Two sentences to remember: one question asks about one measure, so temperature and rainfall do not go into the same sentence; and if the place and the time are vague, the official record cannot be found, so the item counts as unfinished rather than being left blank.

### 2. The three-column record sheet (fixed at three)

| Column | What goes in it | What to write when it cannot be found |
|---|---|---|
| Figure | The value copied from the official record, exactly as published: not rewritten, not rounded, not converted | Write "not found" — never leave it blank |
| Year | The year or period the figure belongs to | If the record carries no year, write "the record gives no year" |
| Page name or table number, and unit | The page name or table number the figure sits in, and its unit as officially written | "Official records" alone does not count; with no name or table number, this column is unfinished |

Two sentences to remember: the three columns belong to one figure, one sheet per figure, so three figures take three sheets; and the unit is copied as published — pupils do not convert, because conversion is a different lesson.

### 3. Putting the same question to the tool

There is one question, asked the same way for all three figures. For example: "What is the value of [a named weather record] for Hong Kong?" Three figures, three questions, one thing per question.

Only three things are copied from the tool's answer — the **figure, the year and the unit** — written in the space below the record sheet. The whole passage is not copied, and a source the tool claims for itself is not copied down as the pupil's own source. The point is not to catch the tool out but to fact-check one figure against a record that can be opened again.

Three sentences to remember: ask the question once, because questioning is for making the question clearer, not for steering the tool to the answer one wanted; copy the tool's figure as it stands, even when it clearly differs from one's own; and never take a screenshot or upload the tool's output.

### 4. The four kinds of difference (fixed at four)

| Kind | When to circle it |
|---|---|
| Different number | The tool's value differs from the value in the official record |
| Different year | The two values are close, but belong to different years or periods |
| Different unit | The values are the same or close, but the units differ (millimetres and centimetres, a day and a year) |
| No source | The tool gives no year, table number or source, so it cannot be matched to the official record |

Two sentences to remember: circle one kind only per figure, and if two apply, circle the one that most affects the judgement and add one sentence beside it saying why. There is no fifth kind; when all items match, write "all four checked" beside the figure and say so in the verdict.

### 5. One verdict sentence and one boundary

The verdict is a single sentence naming which account can be used:

- "The official record can be used, because ..."
- "The tool's account can be used, because ..." (only when the figure, the year and the unit all match and the tool's source can be found)
- "Neither can be used; we need to check again, because ..."

The sentence must name the year, the table number or the unit. "Roughly right", "about the same" and "probably fine" are not verdicts.

**One boundary:** the tool can help us think of what to ask; the figures follow the official record. The three figures may carry three different verdicts, and there is no rule that the official record always wins — where the tool's account agrees with the record, writing "the official record" must also say that the two agree.

### 6. The 60-minute shape, the offline version and the close

Explanation and demonstration 10 min, then look up the records and copy three figures 15 min, ask the tool and set the answers side by side 10 min, sort the four kinds and write the verdict 15 min, read out the spread of the four kinds and close 10 min.

**Offline version:** the teacher prints three figures from the Observatory's published records beforehand (with the year, page name or table number, and unit), and pupils copy them; the tool step is demonstrated once by the teacher and the class circles the four kinds together. Neither the page nor the attachments carries a real figure in advance.

Three closing sentences:

1. A record needs its year, table number and unit before it can be compared with anything.
2. A fluent answer is not a verified answer.
3. One verdict sentence is what the checking produced.

Homework: bring one official figure found at home next lesson, fill all three columns, and tell a classmate once why it can be used.

## Answer key and marking notes

This page is not an assessment instrument: it gives no score, carries no grade, produces no percentage ranking, is not entered in a report card, and is not a conduct or honesty record. "Finished" is judged only by whether the record sheet can be written at all.

**What a finished sheet looks like:**

- Three figures, each with all three columns filled, including the year and the unit.
- The tool's figures copied as they stand, not edited to agree with the official record.
- One kind circled for each figure, and the kind is one of the four.
- One verdict sentence that says which account can be used and names the year, the table number or the unit.
- "Not found" written wherever something could not be found, with no blank left behind.

**A fictional example (to show the format, not to be used as data)**

| Item | Example (fictional) |
|---|---|
| Figure | 1,234 |
| Year | 20XX |
| Page name or table number, and unit | Example Table 3; millimetres |
| The tool's account (fictional) | 2,500 millimetres a year, no year and no table number given |
| Kind circled | Different number |
| Verdict sentence (example wording) | The official record can be used, because it gives the year, the table number and the unit; the tool's figure has no source. |

> Every figure, year and table number above is a **fictional example** written for this exercise. None of it represents a real record, a real year or a real published statistic.

**Common answers and responses:**

- **Only the figure column is filled.** Response: without a year and a unit the figure cannot be compared with anything; go back to the record and find the year and table number for that same figure.
- **The tool's figure has been edited to match the official one.** Response: both sides are copied as they stand; an edited figure hides the difference, and hides where the tool went wrong.
- **Two kinds circled for one figure.** Response: circle the one that most affects the judgement and write the other as a sentence beside it.
- **The verdict says "both are about the same".** Response: a verdict names which account can be used; "about the same" is not a verdict.
- **The tool gave no source, and the pupil still wrote that it can be used.** Response: with no source that sentence cannot be written; circle "no source" and say in the verdict that the tool's account cannot be used, or needs checking again.

The three figures may be swapped for other records at the teacher's choice; the format does not change, and the numbers in any worked example must stay fictional.
