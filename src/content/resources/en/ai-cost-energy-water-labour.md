> A tool can give you an answer, and there is a cost behind that answer. This page gives no figures at all: it takes three clues and splits each one in two — "what we know" and "what we do not know yet" — and then has pupils ask the next question. Three clues, three columns, five questions, nothing added or dropped.

## What this solves

Pupils use tools every day and rarely ask one thing: what did that use up?

The question is easy to lose in either of two directions. One is memorising a figure — one query costs so much water, so much electricity. It sounds forceful, but what pupils remember is a figure, not an understanding; and a figure moves, gets compared, gets carried across places, and stops being true — and once it has stopped being true, nobody can tell. The other direction is a moral conclusion: tools waste, tools are wrong. Pupils nod, then carry on, because they are holding no question they can ask.

This page takes a third route: **write "what we know" and "what we do not know yet" as two separate columns.** What is known is explained as a process; what is not known is left open, with "who could we ask, and what kind of source could we check" beside it. Pupils leave the room holding several questions rather than one figure.

**Why the page carries no figures at all.** Because the thing being taught is not a conclusion but a method: telling apart what I know from what I do not, and knowing where to look next. Any single quantity turns the lesson into a memory test, and a memory test has one right answer — at which point pupils stop asking. So the page gives no energy used, no water used, no emissions, no cost, and no conversion or comparison of any kind. **That is deliberate, not an omission.**

**Three statements to read first:**

1. **The three clues, the three-column table and the verification checklist are this resource's suggestion.** The official documents do not use these names and do not set how many clues, columns or checklist items there should be. The grounds are the two requirements the documents do set out — reflecting on the limitations and impact of AI, and technology for good. **None of this may be presented as an Education Bureau requirement.**
2. **This page quotes two official grounds only, verbatim.** The AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools exists here only as a scanned copy with no text layer, so this page **names the document and quotes nothing from it**, and fills in none of its learning focuses, unit names or level descriptors. Where a school wants that mapping, the page provides blank fields for the school to complete from the original.
3. **The Primary Information and Innovation Technology Curriculum Framework (trial version) is a primary pilot.** This page covers S1-S6; at secondary level the same set of terms is used, but there is no equivalent official framework, so the page neither presents it as a secondary requirement nor quotes items from it.

**This page does five things it does not do:**

- **It carries no quantity of any kind.** Energy used, water used, emissions, training cost, query-count conversions and comparisons between places or years do not appear; nor does "one query equals this much water", "training a model equals this many households' electricity", or "this uses several times the power of a search engine".
- **It names no company, brand, model, service, country, city or institution.** It says "the tool".
- **It accuses nobody.** The labour clue says only that "someone labels data, moderates content and handles reports"; it uses no accusatory description and points at no place or group of people.
- **It evaluates no tool.** It does not argue for or against using one, and does not compare one with another.
- **It asks for no pupil's own usage record.** The class tally records one total for the whole class, unnamed, with nothing per pupil.

## Learning objectives

**Who it is for.** S1-S6, across subjects. The same material differs between younger and older classes in the depth of the follow-up question, not in the number of clues. A teacher may set the single run through the three-column table as a follow-up task.

After one lesson, pupils can:

1. Name the three clues and, for each, say what we know and what we do not know yet.
2. Use the three-column table to record one thing they have seen, with a person they could ask or a kind of source they could check.
3. Take part in one tally that records a class total only, and say why it is unnamed and why nothing is converted.
4. Read out the five questions and say which is hardest to answer, and why.

The page rests on the three learning strands — **how AI basically works**, **human-machine communication and collaboration**, and **reflection on the application of AI** — and takes **people-centred** education and **technology for good** as its values. It is also a rehearsal in **timely and appropriate use**: you can only call a use timely once you can ask what it took and whether that was worth it. It sits between **a sense of responsibility** and **critical thinking**: critical thinking is being able to say how much you know and how much you do not; a sense of responsibility is going after the next question once you know you do not know, instead of treating a figure you were told as the answer.

## Teaching steps

The attachments are two Word documents: the **three-clues worksheet** (one per pupil) and the **three-column table with the one-page verification checklist** (one per group, printable on its own).

### Step one: the three clues (three, fixed, in this order)

Take them one at a time and stop after each one, so pupils can say the "we do not know yet" side themselves. **The order is fixed**: energy first, then water, then labour — from what can be seen (power, water) to what cannot (people).

| Clue | What we know | What we do not know yet |
|---|---|---|
| One — energy | The tool runs in a data centre somewhere; training and every use draw electricity, so there is somewhere drawing power behind every answer | Where that place is, what kind of power it uses, who pays the bill, and when in the day it is busiest |
| Two — water | The servers in a data centre have to be cooled, cooling uses water, and the water is clean water that has been treated; water has a source and it has an outlet | Where the water comes from, where it goes, how it is allocated in a dry season, and who monitors it |
| Three — unseen labour | There are people behind the answer: someone labels data, someone moderates content, someone handles reports, and that work is usually invisible | Who does that work, how it is paid, whether there is a route to raise a concern, and who follows up when something goes wrong |

Three rules for the three clues:

- **"We do not know yet" is not a blank — it is a question waiting to be asked.** Every cell must be written as a question; if you cannot write the question, the thinking is not finished.
- **Say how what is known actually works.** "It uses power" is not a description, it is a process: something has to run somewhere, be cooled, and be prepared by people first.
- **Do not turn "we do not know" into an accusation.** The labour clue especially: say that people are doing work, and do not say that anyone is being taken advantage of; point at no place, trade or group.

### Step two: the three-column table (three columns, fixed)

One per group, filled left to right. **The third column is the one that matters**: without it, the first two are only a reaction.

| Column | What it asks | What goes in it |
|---|---|---|
| One — what I saw | The one thing I actually saw | One fact: a use, a picture, an answer — the situation, not a feeling |
| Two — what I still need to know | The clue this leads me to follow | One question, with the clue it belongs to (energy, water or labour) |
| Three — who to ask, what kind of source to check | Where to look next | A person (a post or role, never a name), or a kind of source (official statistics, an organisation's annual report, a textbook, direct observation) |

The foot of the table carries one row: **source and year** — anything named in column three is written there with its source and year; anything without a source does not count as checked. The row is left blank for pupils, and the page pre-fills no source.

Two rules:

- **Column three may not say "look it up online".** Name the kind of source or the role; "online" is not a source.
- **One row per thing.** This is a record of one follow-up question, not a list; the narrower it is, the further it goes.

### Step three: one small class tally (counts and minutes only)

The whole class does this together, recording two things only: **how many times the class used a tool over a set period before the lesson, and how many minutes in total.** One total for the class.

Four rules, stated as it is done:

- **Counts and minutes only, never a person.** No name, no class, no seat number, and nobody is asked how long they used it.
- **Nothing is converted.** Counts do not become electricity, minutes do not become water, and neither becomes a cost. A conversion needs a factor, and this page supplies no factor.
- **One class total.** The total goes on the board, as one number, and is wiped off afterwards.
- **The tally is there to raise a question, not to rank anyone.** No comparison between classes, and none between pupils.

The point of the step is not to arrive at a quantity but to let pupils see that **between "how many times we used it", which we can answer, and "how much power that took", which we cannot, sit several questions.**

### Step four: the five questions (five, fixed, in this order)

The teacher reads the five out; pupils need not answer all of them. **Whatever cannot be answered goes on the "we do not know yet" side.**

| Order | Question | What it follows |
|---|---|---|
| One | Who pays the electricity bill? | Energy: where the bill lands |
| Two | Where does the water come from, and where does it go? | Water: source and outlet |
| Three | Who is labelling the data? | Labour: who does the invisible work |
| Four | Without AI, how much time and how many people would it take? | The two sides of a trade-off: what is saved and what is spent |
| Five | Who decided to use it? | The decision: who took this step |

The fifth is the one that closes the set: the first four set out the cost, the fifth names the decision. A cost does not simply occur; someone decided to use the tool.

### The shape of the lesson

| Time | What happens | What pupils do |
|---|---|---|
| Ten minutes | Opening, and one run of the class tally | Count the class total together, write it on the board, wipe it off |
| Fifteen minutes | The three clues, one at a time | Say the "we do not know yet" side of each, written as a question |
| Fifteen minutes | The three-column table, one row per group | Fill the three columns and add source and year at the foot ("not found" where nothing was found) |
| Ten minutes | The five questions | Read them out, name the hardest, and say why |
| Ten minutes | Closing | Read the three sentences together and take the checklist away |

Younger classes may merge the table and the questions into one oral exercise; everything else stays. **The number and order of the clues do not change.**

### Teacher checks (four)

1. **Do not ask pupils to guess a figure.** When a pupil asks "but how much is it", the answer is "this page will not tell you — but here is where you could check".
2. **Evaluate no tool and no company.** Do not say which tool is better, worse or greener; the page covers how the thing works and where the cost shows up.
3. **The labour clue states that work exists, and accuses nobody.** No accusatory description, no pointing at a place, a trade or a group; when a pupil makes an accusation, take it back to column three: "where could you check that?"
4. **The tally is unnamed and unranked.** One class total, wiped off afterwards; nothing per pupil, no class comparison.

### Three closing sentences (read together)

1. "What I saw, I can say."
2. "What I do not know yet, I can say."
3. "Who I can ask, and what kind of source I can check, I can say."

## Answer key and marking notes

There is **no correct answer** here and **nothing is scored**. What follows is for the teacher to compare against, never to mark right or wrong.

**Notes on the three clues**

- Energy: "training and use both draw electricity" and "the power is drawn in a data centre" are enough; following it up with "who pays the bill" is a bonus, not a requirement.
- Water: "cooling uses clean water" and "water has a source and an outlet" are enough; noticing that source and outlet are two different questions is better still.
- Labour: "someone labels data, moderates content and handles reports" is enough. **An answer that accuses a place, a trade or a group of people is not correct** and goes back to column three.

**Notes on the three-column table**

- Column one is adequate if it gives the situation (a use, a picture, an answer); a reaction such as "AI is wasteful" means the cell is not filled.
- Column two must be a question, with its clue named. "I want to know more" does not pass.
- Column three must name a role or a kind of source. **"Look it up online" and "ask the AI" both fail**: the first is not a source, the second is the thing being asked.
- A blank "source and year" row is normal; where nothing was found, write "not found" rather than a year someone mentioned.

**About quantities**

A pupil who produces a specific figure neither gains nor loses marks: the page deliberately supplies no quantity, and a teacher should not add one in the room. **Where a pupil brings a figure, the response is three questions: where is it from? What year? Under what conditions was it produced?** Those three questions are the verification.

**What counts as finished**: all three clues covered, at least one row per group in the table, the tally recorded as a class total only, and the five questions read with the hardest one named.
