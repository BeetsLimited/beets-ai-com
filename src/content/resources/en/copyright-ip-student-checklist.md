> In a classroom, a copyright question rarely arrives as the word infringement. It arrives as an ordinary question: **may I use this image?** Four stages, five judgements and a one-page declaration turn that question into a lesson that leaves a record.

## What this solves

The visual arts workflow has gained a stretch in recent years: pupils make an image with a generation tool, and on the way they search for references, write a prompt, generate, revise, and show the result. Every stage is a decision, and the person deciding is usually a teenager with a few minutes.

Four situations recur:

- **"It was only a reference."** A pupil finds an image, drops it into a prompt as a reference, and the result looks very like it. The pupil believes they only referred to it, and never considers that the image has an owner.
- **"Educational use is always allowed."** This travels around schools as if it were a rule that can be relied on. It is not: it treats "used in a school" and "free to use in any way" as the same thing.
- **"Better not touch anything."** The opposite reaction: pupils decide that anything belonging to someone else is off limits, so they stop looking at references and stop making collage, and fear narrows the work.
- **"I wrote the source, so it is fine."** A pupil writes "image from somewhere" under the work and considers the matter closed. But **crediting a source and obtaining permission are two different things**: a credit says where the image came from, permission says whether use was allowed. A credit can sit under an image that was never licensed.

This page fixes the workflow into four stages, the judgement into five questions, and then records the answers on a one-page, five-column declaration. It does not ask pupils to memorise legislation. It gives them a sheet they can finish before they hand the work in.

**Four statements to read first, and to print on every pupil sheet:**

1. **This page is not legal advice.** It names the Copyright Ordinance and nothing more: **no section numbers, no section text, no penalties**, because this page holds no verified text to quote. Individual cases go to the teacher, to be handled under the school's own guidance — not decided by the pupil, and not decided by this page.
2. **The four stages, five judgements and five-column declaration are this resource's suggestion, not an Education Bureau requirement.** What the official documents ask of this ground is that copyright be respected, that permission be obtained before anyone else's image is uploaded to the internet, and that intellectual property be respected. No document prescribes four stages, a number of judgements, or the columns of a declaration.
3. **This page shares the ground with the likeness and image rights resource.** That one deals with **the consent of the person in the picture**: whose face it is, whether anyone was asked, how far it may be shown. This page deals with **the source and licensing of the material**: where this image, this text, this pattern came from, and whether use was permitted. Both are needed, and they are not the same question; a work can pass one and fail the other.
4. **This page does not compare tools and does not recommend any.** A tool name appears only as an example of what goes in column two: a blank the pupil fills with the name and version date of whatever they used. Change the tool and the judgement is unchanged.

**Two sentences that must not go into the material:** not "educational use is always allowed" — that is not a rule anyone can rely on; and not "it is always infringement" — that stops pupils from making legitimate references and quotations. Both sentences cancel the judgement, and judgement is what this page teaches.

**Every case on this page is invented**: no real school, person, work title or brand.

## Purpose

After this lesson, pupils can:

1. State the **four stages** in order, and say which stage they made a decision at.
2. Answer the **five judgements** one by one with **yes, no or not sure**, and when answering "not sure", write down which part they are unsure about.
3. Complete a **one-page, five-column use declaration**, including their signature.
4. Tell **crediting a source** apart from **obtaining permission**: one records origin, the other records permission.
5. Know when their own case should go to the teacher, to be handled under the school's guidance, rather than being decided by themselves.

The page maps to the AI literacy strand **reflection on the use of artificial intelligence**. Of the four attitudes, it sits closest to **responsibility** and **respect for others**: responsibility is remembering, after the work has gone out, that something in it belongs to someone else; respect for others is not treating their work as free material.

## How to use it

There are two Word documents: **four stages and five judgements** (with the one-page teacher edition at the end) and the **one-page five-column use declaration**. One 60-minute lesson runs in four parts; a two-lesson split is at the end of this section.

### Step one: four stages, in a fixed order

There are **four stages, and the order does not change**: find references, write the prompt, generate and revise, show the work. The order is part of the content — each stage leaves the record the next one needs, and a class that thinks about copyright only at the end is usually too late.

| Stage | What the pupil is doing | The record this stage should leave |
|---|---|---|
| One — **find references** | Collecting reference images, text, patterns or material | Where each item came from: a name or a link, and whether it says it may be used |
| Two — **write the prompt** | Writing a prompt; possibly pasting in a reference image or a passage | The prompt as written; whether anyone else's work or photograph went in |
| Three — **generate and revise** | Generating, choosing, then revising | Which tool and version date; what the pupil changed |
| Four — **show the work** | Classroom display, handing in, posting online | How far it is shown (classroom, school, internet); whether the declaration is complete |

**One point to make:** stages one and four are the ones pupils treat as unrelated to copyright — one is only looking, the other is only showing. But the source is settled at stage one and the risk appears at stage four; the middle two stages only carry out what was already decided.

### Step two: five judgements, answered yes, no or not sure

The five judgements are **in a fixed order**, answered one by one, and **there are only three possible answers: yes, no, not sure**. The page **is not marked, not graded, not levelled**; "not sure" is a valid answer, and often the most honest one.

| Judgement | What it asks |
|---|---|
| One — **whose work is in it** | Which part of this work did I not make myself? Whose is it? |
| Two — **is there permission** | Did I get permission to use it? What kind of permission? |
| Three — **how much did I change** | What did I change? Is the original still traceable in the result? |
| Four — **is it credited** | Did I write down the source? Where? |
| Five — **may it be shown** | In its present state, how far may this be shown? |

Three points to make clear:

- **When the answer is "not sure", write down what you are unsure about.** "Not sure" is an answer, not a blank. "Not sure whether there is permission" is far more useful than "permission obtained" when nobody was asked.
- **The five judgements are not a score.** Three yes answers are not worse than five, and two "not sure" answers do not mean a fail; the judgements exist to find the one item that needs dealing with.
- **Judgement four and judgement two are separate.** This is the most important line on the page: a credit does not mean permission, and permission does not remove the need for a credit. Two columns, two answers.

### Step three: the one-page, five-column declaration

One page, **five columns plus the pupil's signature**; one per pupil, filled in by the pupil.

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| One — **work title and date** | What the work is called, and when it was finished |
| Two — **tool name and version date** | The tool used and its version date; write "none" if no tool was used |
| Three — **references or source material** | Each source by name or link; write "none" if there were no references |
| Four — **permission status** | One of four: **permitted / free to use / not permitted / not sure** |
| Five — **what I changed and where the credit sits** | What was changed; where the credit appears on the work |

Under the last column are the **pupil's signature** and the date.

**The four options in column four are this page's division, not a legal classification:**

- **Permitted** — specific permission was obtained, for example the author or rights holder agreed to this use in this context.
- **Free to use** — the material itself says it may be used this way (public domain, or material whose conditions are clearly stated), so no separate permission is needed.
- **Not permitted** — no permission was obtained, and the material is not free to use either.
- **Not sure** — the pupil cannot say which of the other three applies.

**Column five and column three are not the same thing.** Column three says where it came from; column five says what was changed and where the credit is written. Having both on one page is deliberate: it lets a pupil see that writing a source does not answer column four.

### Step four: the teacher's close (5 minutes)

Three sentences, written on the board one at a time:

1. **This is for whoever looks at the work** — they will not know where you looked for references.
2. **A credit records origin; permission records consent** — two things, two columns, two judgements.
3. **Anything you answered "not sure" goes to the teacher, under the school's guidance** — do not settle it yourself in the classroom.

### Splitting into two 60-minute lessons

| | Lesson one | Lesson two |
|---|---|---|
| Content | Stages one to three (references, prompt, generate and revise); judgements one to three | Stage four (showing the work); judgements four and five; the use declaration; the close |
| Timing | 10 minutes framing · 40 minutes stages and judgements · 10 minutes summary | 5 minutes framing · 15 minutes stage four · 25 minutes declaration · 15 minutes close |
| What pupils take away | A judgement page filled to judgement three | A completed five-column declaration, signed |

For a single 60-minute lesson: 10 minutes framing, 15 minutes on the four stages, 20 minutes on the five judgements, 10 minutes on the declaration, 5 minutes to close.

## Review checklist

This page is a tool, not a test, so the checklist asks whether it was worked through and recorded — not whether the answers were right.

- **Were the four stages worked through in order?** Filling in the declaration first, then the stages, usually means the earlier record was written afterwards; that is allowed, but it should say so.
- **Were all five judgements answered?** Each needs a yes, a no or a "not sure"; a blank is unfinished.
- **Does each "not sure" say what it is unsure about?** Three words on their own are not an answer.
- **Does column four have exactly one option?** Both "free to use" and "not permitted" ticked means the difference has not landed, and judgement two should be redone.
- **Are columns three and five filled separately?** Merging the source and the position of the credit into one sentence is the commonest error on this page, and the one most worth correcting.
- **Is the declaration signed?** The signature is not a legal instrument; it marks that the pupil stands behind the record on the page.
- **Did the "not sure" items go to the teacher?** Handing them over is correct; a pupil deciding on their own that something "should be fine" or "definitely is not allowed" should be corrected at the close.

The **one-page teacher edition** (the last page of the first document, removed before printing for pupils) sets out the 60-minute and two-lesson sequences, how to ask each judgement, the difference between the four permission options, the three commonest filling errors, and one reminder: this page is not legal advice, and individual cases go to the teacher under the school's guidance.

- **Blueprint for Digital Education Development in Primary and Secondary Schools** (three-fold leaflet, p.2): schools are asked to plan digital and AI education in their development plan and to account for the arrangements to teachers, pupils and parents; reducing "where did the material in this work come from" to one page a pupil can complete is part of that account.
- **Supplement I: AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools** (p.1-4): sets out the three learning strands; this resource addresses "reflection on the use of artificial intelligence".
- **Supplement II: Guide to Using AI in Teaching in Primary and Secondary Schools** (p.2-4): puts education first and technology second, and asks teachers to exercise professional judgement; here the judgement stays inside the making process rather than being repaired by the teacher before publication.
- **Visual Arts Curriculum Guide (Primary 1 to Secondary 6) (2024)**: the subject covers creating and appreciating; this resource is mainly about making, with appreciation alongside, and the four stages are one way of recording a making process.
