> A decision about an image of a person is usually made in under a minute: whose face is it, was anyone asked, is the source usable, where will it be shown, and is it labelled. Work the five judgements in their fixed order and they settle into three outcomes — **can do, ask first, cannot do** — which gives a pupil a line they can say out loud instead of a feeling.

## What this solves

The scene repeats in most Visual Arts rooms: a pupil generates an image of a person within a few minutes and then asks whether it can go online. The teacher has to answer, on the spot, a question with no simple answer.

- **Judging by feel.** One pupil believes a fictional figure is automatically fine; another believes any photograph is automatically out. Two pupils in one class reach opposite conclusions and neither can give a reason.
- **Assuming a label settles it.** The pupil writes "AI-generated" on the image and treats the matter as closed. But **labelling and consent are two different things**: a label says where the picture came from, and consent is about the face inside it.
- **Merging two questions into one.** "Was anyone asked" and "may this be shown publicly" become the same question, so an agreed display inside the school and an unagreed post on a social platform get the same answer.
- **No record.** At the end of term, when portfolios are handed in, nobody can remember whether the figure in a particular image was invented or was a classmate's photograph. The teacher is left relying on memory.
- **A misleading name.** Some say Hong Kong has an image rights law; others say that without one anything goes. Neither is accurate, and a lesson has no time for a law lecture.

This page fixes the five judgements, keeps their order, resolves them into three outcomes, and uses a one-page six-column declaration to keep the decision on record. Two lessons of 60 minutes: identifying and appreciating, then making and declaring.

**Three statements to read first, and to print on every pupil sheet:**

1. **The name, the five judgements, the three outcomes and the declaration are this resource's suggestion, not an Education Bureau requirement.** What the Bureau asks of this material is three things: respect copyright, obtain permission before uploading anyone else's image to the internet, and respect intellectual property. No document prescribes five judgements, three outcomes, or the columns of a declaration.
2. **There is no statute in Hong Kong called "portrait rights".** The protection this page refers to comes from the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486), the Copyright Ordinance, defamation and the common law. This page names them only: it quotes no sections, states no penalties, and **gives no legal advice**. An individual case follows school policy and professional advice.
3. **This page deals only with the judgement made while working in the classroom.** Publishing approval, informing parents and complaints procedures follow the school's existing arrangements; nothing here replaces any of them.

**Two sentences that must not go into the teaching material:** that Hong Kong has a portrait rights statute — there is no such statute, so writing it would be false; and that labelling an image as AI-generated makes it acceptable — **labelling and consent are two different things**, and a labelled image may still need permission and may still be one you cannot make.

**This page also names nobody:** no artist, no case, no platform, no tool. Every example is marked fictional, and no real school or person appears.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the two lessons, pupils can:

1. Work through the **five judgements** in their fixed order and say what each one asks.
2. Place any image of a person into one of the **three outcomes** — can do, ask first, cannot do — and give the reason.
3. Distinguish **consent** from **labelling**: one is about the person's face, the other about where the picture came from.
4. Complete a **one-page six-column** image-use declaration, stating who is in the image, consent, source, audience and how AI was used.
5. Make the judgement while they are working, rather than repairing it just before publishing.

The page addresses one of the three learning strands of AI literacy: **reflection on the application of AI**. It sits between **critical thinking** and **respect for intellectual property**: critical thinking means not mistaking "this sounds acceptable" for "permission was obtained", and respect for intellectual property means not treating another person's face or work as free material. Of the four attitudes, the two that matter most here are **integrity** and **empathy**: integrity is filling in the declaration honestly when nobody is checking, and empathy is asking once what it would be like if the face were your own.

## Teaching steps

The attachments are two Word documents: **the five judgements with the image-use declaration** (the judgement cards, the three outcomes and the six-column declaration) and **the two-lesson plan** (with its three columns for learning area, learning elements and notes on AI).

### Step 1: Five judgements, in a fixed order

The five judgements are **in a fixed order and must not be rearranged**. The order is itself the teaching point: who the face belongs to comes first, and whether the image is labelled comes last — because labelling is the easiest step and the one most often mistaken for the finish.

| Judgement | What it asks | Options (fixed) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Whose face is it** | A fictional person / my own / a classmate / a public figure / not sure |
| 2 | **Is there consent** | Not applicable / obtained (and withdrawable) / still to obtain; for a minor, handled by the parent |
| 3 | **Is the source usable** | My own photograph / a licensed image library / provided by the school / an image from the internet / someone else's work |
| 4 | **Purpose and audience** | Classroom display / display within the school / the school website / a social platform |
| 5 | **Is it labelled** | AI-generated / AI-assisted / no AI |

Four points for pupils to hold on to:

- **"Not sure" is an answer to judgement 1, and choosing it is not a failure.** It means stop and ask. Choosing wrongly is the failure.
- **"Obtained" has to be withdrawable.** Consent is not a one-off licence; a pupil should be able to say "they can tell me to stop showing it at any time" before consent counts as obtained.
- **Judgement 3 asks about the source, not the quality.** A low-resolution image from the internet is still an image from the internet.
- **Judgement 5 is the last and the lightest of the five.** It cannot stand in for the other four; a labelled image still has to answer judgements 2 to 4.

### Step 2: Three outcomes, fixed at three

Once the five judgements are answered, they resolve into **three outcomes, and no more**. An outcome is not a mark; it is an action.

| Outcome | When it applies | What happens in the lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Can do** | A fictional person who cannot be **identified** as any real person | Finish the work and complete the declaration |
| **Ask first** | My own or a classmate's photograph, display within the school, consent obtained and the image labelled | Stop, obtain consent, write down the date, then continue |
| **Cannot do** | A public figure's face, an unconsenting classmate's face, putting a real person into a fictional situation (sexual, violent or political), and removing or adding anyone's body in any circumstances | Change the approach; do not submit it |

Four points to be explicit about:

- **"Cannot do" is a creative instruction, not a punishment.** The work can still be finished with an invented figure; what is ruled out is that method, not the idea.
- **A public figure's face is "cannot do" even for a classroom display.** Consent does not enter into it: a public figure's face is not available merely because nobody objected.
- **"Removing or adding anyone's body" is the one rule with no exceptions.** It goes on the card, on the declaration and in the brief for the work.
- **The three outcomes and the audience are separate questions.** A "can do" piece still has to answer judgement 4 before it goes on a social platform, and an "ask first" piece still needs asking even for a display inside the school.

### Step 3: Lesson 1 — identifying and appreciating (60 minutes)

| Time | Stage | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 10 min | Explanation | The names and order of the five judgements and the names of the three outcomes. No examples yet |
| 20 min | Activity 1: the five questions demonstrated | The teacher takes one image of a fictional person and asks all five questions live, writing the answers up. Say plainly: "this one is can do, because it cannot be identified as any real person" |
| 10 min | Deepening | The teacher offers a counter-case: the same invented figure, but with a classmate's face swapped in — which judgement fires first? Land on "labelling and consent are two different things" and "judgement 5 cannot stand in for judgement 2" |
| 15 min | Activity 2: three cards in groups | Each group takes three cards (the worked examples are in the answer key), writes its own judgement first, then spends three minutes comparing. Each group gives one reason per card |
| 5 min | Close | Display the three outcomes side by side and ask which card is most often mistaken for "can do". Give no answer; leave the question for lesson 2 |

### Step 4: Lesson 2 — making and declaring (60 minutes)

| Time | Stage | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 5 min | Explanation | Set the task: finish one image of an invented person and complete a declaration for it. Restate the four "cannot do" cases |
| 20 min | Activity 1: making an invented person | Pupils generate an invented figure from a written description, with no photograph of a real person supplied as reference. A pupil who wants to use their own or a classmate's photograph goes through "ask first" |
| 10 min | Deepening: peer appreciation | In pairs, pupils ask each other the five judgements. They discuss the judgement, not the drawing |
| 15 min | Activity 2: the image-use declaration | Each pupil completes a one-page six-column declaration; the teacher checks each group that the consent column and the audience column agree |
| 10 min | Close and homework | Collect the declarations, read out the three most specific, and set the homework: complete a declaration for the most recent piece of work |

### Step 5: The one-page image-use declaration, fixed at six columns

The declaration is one page and **six columns, no more and no fewer**. It is not an administrative form; it is the pupil's account of their own work.

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Title of the work and class** |
| 2 | **Who is in the image**: fictional / myself / someone else / not applicable |
| 3 | **Consent**: not applicable / obtained and the date / still to obtain |
| 4 | **Source**: my own photograph / a licensed image library / provided by the school / other |
| 5 | **Audience**: classroom / within the school / outside the school |
| 6 | **AI labelling**: AI-generated / AI-assisted / no AI, with the pupil's signature |

Three notes on using it:

- **Columns 3 and 5 have to agree.** If column 5 says "outside the school", column 3 cannot say "not applicable" or "still to obtain". This is where a declaration most often contradicts itself, and it is the check worth giving the pupils to run on their own work.
- **The class in column 1 is not there to identify anyone.** It lets the teacher match a work to a class before a display; the declaration has no student number and no parent signature line.
- **The pupil signs it.** A parent handles consent for a minor, but the declaration is filled in and signed by the pupil, because the judgement being practised is the pupil's own.

## Answer key and marking notes

### Worked examples: five fictional cards

All five are fictional, for classroom demonstration only, and correspond to no real person, school or event.

| Card | Situation (fictional) | Outcome | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | A character generated from a written description, with no photograph of a real person as reference | **Can do** | It cannot be identified as any real person |
| B | Made from the pupil's own photograph, parent's consent obtained, image labelled, shown inside the school only | **Ask first** | Own photograph, display inside the school, consent obtained and labelled — all three present before it can proceed |
| C | A public figure's face placed into an invented campaign poster | **Cannot do** | A public figure's face, even for a classroom display |
| D | A classmate's face placed into an invented violent scene | **Cannot do** | An unconsenting classmate's face, and a real person placed in a fictional situation (violence) |
| E | A classmate's body attached to a different photograph | **Cannot do** | Removing or adding anyone's body, in any circumstances |

### Marking points

- **Order.** Were the five judgements answered 1 to 5? Starting from judgement 5 (writing "AI-generated" first) counts as unfinished.
- **Specificity.** Judgement 3 needs a named source, not just "online"; judgement 4 needs an audience, not just "sharing".
- **Consistency.** Do the outcome and the five answers match? "Cannot do" with a reason that mentions only the missing label is unfinished — the label is not what made it impossible.
- **The declaration.** Are all six columns filled in, and do columns 3 and 5 agree?
- **Language.** In the appreciation stage, can the pupil use the words consent, identifiable and source to explain the decision, rather than only "you can't"?

**One common wrong answer, and how to answer it:** a pupil says "I've written AI-generated on it, so I can post it". The reply is about order: labelling is judgement 5 and audience is judgement 4, so answering the fifth leaves the fourth — and the second — still to answer.
