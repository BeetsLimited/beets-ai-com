> Schools already hear what pupils think about AI: a hand goes up in class, someone says two sentences at break, a parent passes it on at parents' evening. What pupils do not hear is what the school changed because of it — and that sentence is the reason they will bother telling you next time.

## What this solves

When a school surveys its pupils, the problem is rarely the survey. It is what happens afterwards. Three situations come up most often:

- **Too much collected, too little said back.** Thirty questions are asked, the counts are done, a report goes into a file, and no pupil ever hears a result. The response rate in the next round halves.
- **The count turns into a league table.** To have "data", the results are broken down by class and by year, so a survey of opinion becomes a comparison between classes, and form teachers start minding their own class's numbers.
- **Something did change, and nobody knows.** The school really did change something — added a time limit, changed a tool, adjusted a homework expectation — but the change is recorded in a committee minute. Pupils conclude that saying anything makes no difference.

This page does three things: **eight anonymous questions, one page of counts, one page of "what we changed"** — and puts them into a cycle of four points.

**Three things to say plainly first:**

1. **The questions and the format are a suggestion from this resource, not an Education Bureau requirement.** The Bureau sets expectations for teaching with AI and for professional gatekeeping, but prescribes no pupil survey; a school may change, add or drop questions to suit itself.
2. **The results are anonymous, are not recorded by class, are not used in teacher appraisal and are not compared between classes.** That sentence must be printed on the **front page of the survey**, not only in the teacher's notes: a pupil has to see it before writing.
3. **This page does not replace the school's annual review.** An annual review brings a year's evidence together (policy, training, classroom practice, incidents, provision); this page is one piece of pupil-voice evidence within it and does not carry the review's conclusions.

## Purpose

By the end of this resource a school will be able to:

1. Use **eight questions in three groups** to get, in 15 minutes, what pupils actually do with AI, what goes wrong for them, and what they want the school to change first.
2. Use a **one-page count** to turn the responses into numbers that can be read: frequencies only, and a group of fewer than 30 reports headcounts rather than percentages.
3. Use a **one-page "what we changed"** (three columns: what pupils said / what we changed / when it takes effect), with no more than three changes per cycle, and "not changing" written with one reason where something cannot be done.
4. Use **four points** to join ask, tell, change and ask again into a cycle, rather than a survey that happens once.
5. Keep the survey itself from creating a new risk: no names, no class, no identifiable personal data, and no account of any kind required of a pupil.

The arrangement is built on the guiding principles of **nurturing the whole person, literacy first, application-oriented use and AI for good**, and it addresses the hardest of the three learning strands of AI literacy to teach and to assess — **reflection on the application of AI**: a pupil has to judge whether this use is appropriate, who is affected, and what they would do differently next time, and part of that only the pupil can say. The school has to ask to hear it. **Critical discernment** (a pupil being able to say where the problem actually is), **timely and appropriate use** (when and for how long, which is the thing pupils most want said clearly), **human-AI collaboration** (pupils want to know which part they should still decide themselves) and **technology for good** (pupils notice what a tool does to their classmates) are what the eight questions are really asking about.

## How to use it

The file is a Word document in five parts: the survey (one page, eight questions), the counts page (one page), the "what we changed" page (one page, three columns), the four points of the cycle (one page), and a page of notes for the form teacher. The form teacher administers it in a form-teacher period; the curriculum lead or a vice-principal does the counting and the announcing.

### Step 1: eight questions, three groups, one page

The eight are fixed, in three groups: how pupils use AI (three questions), what goes wrong (three), and what the school should change first (two). All are single- or multiple-choice except the last, which is open.

**Group 1 — how pupils use AI**

1. This school year, have you used an AI tool for classwork or in a lesson? (Never / a few times / a few times a month / most weeks)
2. What do you mostly use it for? (Tick any: finding information / understanding a text or a concept / writing or editing words / making images or slides / practising a language / checking my own answers / other: ___)
3. Where do you usually use it? (In the classroom / in the computer room or library / at home / other: ___)

**Group 2 — what goes wrong**

4. Which of these have happened to you? (Tick any: the tool gave an answer that was wrong / I did not know whether the teacher allowed it / I did not know how to ask well / the tool or the account would not work / I worried that what I typed could be seen / none of these)
5. When you think an answer may be wrong, what do you usually do? (Ask the same tool again / check a second source / ask a teacher or a classmate / use it anyway / other: ___)
6. Has a teacher said what "using it properly" means? (Yes, clearly / yes, but I cannot remember / no / not sure)

**Group 3 — what the school should change first**

7. If the school could change only one thing, what should it be? (Tools that are easier to use / clearer rules on when it may and may not be used / teaching us how to check an answer / more lesson time to practise / other: ___)
8. In one sentence, the one thing you most want the school to know: ______________________

Three things worth saying about using them:

- **Eight is eight.** Adding questions turns a 15-minute survey into half a lesson, and a survey that takes half a lesson is one the form teacher will not want to run again.
- **Every question says "AI tool", never a named commercial product.** Name a brand and the answers become a review of that brand; say "AI tool" and pupils answer about their own use.
- **The open question needs a real blank.** One line gets three words; two lines is what gives the school a sentence it can act on.

### Step 2: 15 minutes, and anonymity is a design decision, not a promise

- **Time**: 15 minutes of a form-teacher period. Read the front-page sentence once, then let pupils fill it in themselves — no discussion, and do not read over shoulders.
- **The front-page sentence must be printed**: "This survey does not record your name or your class; it is not used in teacher appraisal and no class is compared with another." That sentence is part of the design, not politeness: it decides whether pupils write what is true.
- **Collection**: mix the whole class's sheets together and have one colleague hand them all in at once — not stacked by class, not ordered by seat number. The mixing is itself the promise to pupils.
- **No class, but do record the year group and the date**: the counts page needs to know which year group in which month, so that a trend is visible without pointing at any one class.
- **Nothing anywhere asks a pupil to open an account or sign in to a tool.** The survey is on paper or on a school form; if it has to be online, use an anonymous link with no school sign-in, and say beforehand that nothing identifiable is collected.

### Step 3: one page of counts, frequencies only

The counts page is one page, one line per question, one number per option. Three rules:

- **Frequencies only, no commentary.** No sentence like "most pupils feel that" or "the situation is concerning" appears on the counts page; that sentence belongs on the "what we changed" page, and it has to be written from the numbers.
- **Fewer than 30 responses: headcounts only, no percentages.** In a group of 20, 20% is four pupils; written as a percentage, four pupils look like a trend.
- **The open question: at most ten sentences, quoted verbatim.** No corrected spelling, no tidying, no merging; if there are more than ten, pick ten at random and write "XX sentences in all; ten quoted" on the page.

Three more points:

- **No column for class.** The counts page has two columns, option and frequency; there is no class column. To see differences, look at year group and year.
- **Mask anything identifiable when quoting.** If a pupil writes a classmate's name or class in the open question, quote it as "a classmate"; verbatim means the sentence, not somebody else's name.
- **Date the page and name the post that completed it.** An undated set of counts cannot be compared with anything next year.

### Step 4: one page of "what we changed" — three columns, at most three items

The feedback page is one page, three columns: **what pupils said / what we changed / when it takes effect**.

| Column | What to write | What not to write |
|---|---|---|
| What pupils said | One sentence summarising what pupils said on one question, with that question's frequency | No sentence like "pupils generally feel" without a number behind it |
| What we changed | One concrete change: which arrangement, which rule, which practice | No "we will strengthen" or "we will pay attention" — a promise with no action |
| When it takes effect | A date, or "this term" or "from next school year" | No "as soon as possible", no "later" |

Three boundaries:

- **At most three changes a cycle.** Write five and deliver one, and next year pupils will not believe the page; three is what a school can actually do.
- **Where it cannot be done, write "not changing" with one reason.** For example "not changing: tools are bought centrally from the school budget and none will be added this year; the reason is in the letter to parents". "Not changing" is more honest than a blank line, and safer than pretending.
- **The changes have to be announced to pupils, not just filed.** On the classroom board, once in a form-teacher period, or in the parent newsletter — any of the three, but somewhere a pupil will see it.

### Step 5: one cycle, four points

| Point | When | What happens | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Survey | Start of the school year (September to October) | 15 minutes in a form-teacher period; the eight anonymous questions | The completed sheets |
| 2 Count and tell | Within two weeks of the survey | 30 minutes of counting; the one-page count is completed and shown to pupils | The counts page, displayed |
| 3 Announce the changes | Middle of the year (January to February) | The three-column page is completed, at most three changes, and shown to pupils | The "what we changed" page |
| 4 Ask again and compare | End of the year (May to June) | The same eight questions again, compared with point 1 | Two sets of counts, side by side |

Three things worth saying about the cycle:

- **Four points are a floor, not a ceiling.** A school may add a quick mid-term check, but point 4 cannot be dropped: without asking again there is no way to know whether a change did anything.
- **Point 2 has to be early.** Counting that slips to next term finds the pupils' memory and interest gone cold; two weeks is chosen for when the lesson is still fresh.
- **Point 4 uses the same eight questions.** Change the questions and nothing can be compared; a new question goes at the end, with "new this year" noted on the counts page.

### Step 6: the boundary with the school's other documents

Each does its own part; none replaces another:

- **The student AI-use code with classroom posters**: what pupils may and may not do; this page only asks what they actually do, and changes no wording of the code.
- **The parent communication pack**: explains the school's AI use and its consent arrangements to parents; the counts and the changes here can be one section of it.
- **The school AI-use policy template**: the policy layer of what is and is not allowed; what comes out of this survey may support a revision, but this page does not revise policy.
- **The scheme-of-work AI literacy mapping table**: which unit carries which learning element; this page only asks where pupils use AI and what goes wrong.
- **The whole-school AI risk register and incident process**: a specific incident a pupil mentions in the survey (a classmate's photograph uploaded, for example) is not handled on this page — it goes through the school's existing procedures and the requirements of the law, and into the same register.

### Step 7: primary and secondary

The eight questions are the same in both sectors; the difference is **how they are administered**. In upper primary (P4 to P6) the form teacher should read each question aloud and confirm it, and the open question can be a sentence or a drawing; in secondary (S1 to S6) pupils can fill it in themselves, and at point 3 a class representative can help choose the three changes. At primary level the questions can be tied to the corresponding topics in the AI component of the Primary Information and Innovation Technology curriculum framework (a **trial version**), which gives the "how pupils use AI" group a concrete setting. **At secondary level there is no equivalent official framework**, so only the AI Literacy Learning Framework, the Guide to Using AI in Teaching and the Education Bureau circulars apply, and none of it may be written as an Education Bureau requirement.

## Review checklist

Once the survey has run and the changes have been announced, check each line:

- □ The survey is one page, with all eight questions in the three groups above and no ninth added.
- □ The front page prints "no name, no class, not used in teacher appraisal, no class compared with another".
- □ No named commercial AI tool appears anywhere; every question says "AI tool".
- □ The collection method is written down as mixed, not stacked by class or ordered by seat.
- □ The counts page has two columns only, option and frequency, with no class column and no commentary.
- □ A group of fewer than 30 responses reports headcounts, not percentages.
- □ The open question quotes at most ten sentences verbatim, with the total number of sentences stated.
- □ Quotations mask a classmate's name or class and anything else identifiable.
- □ The counts page carries a date and the post of the person completing it.
- □ The feedback page has all three columns: what pupils said / what we changed / when it takes effect.
- □ The feedback page has at most three changes; anything not done says "not changing" with one reason.
- □ "When it takes effect" carries a date or a defined period — no "as soon as possible", no "later".
- □ The changes were announced somewhere pupils see, not only filed.
- □ All four points of the cycle are present, and point 4 repeats the same eight questions.
- □ The page says it does not replace the school's annual review.
- □ The page says the questions and format are this resource's suggestion, not an Education Bureau requirement.
- □ The page says the AI component of the primary framework is a trial version and that there is no equivalent official framework at secondary level.
- □ Where a pupil mentions a specific incident, the page says it is handled under the school's existing procedures and the requirements of the law and recorded in the whole-school AI risk register, and opens no separate procedure.

## Official documents

Every resource in this library is written to the AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools and falls within the policy scope of the Blueprint for Digital Education Development in Primary and Secondary Schools.

This resource maps to:

- **Blueprint for Digital Education Development in Primary and Secondary Schools** (three-fold leaflet, p.2): its four principles are technology for good, nurturing the whole person, equity and access, and forward-looking innovation; "no class recorded, no class compared" is one way of writing equity and access inside a school — a pupil's view should not be ranked by which class they are in.
- **Supplement I: AI Literacy Learning Framework for Primary and Secondary Schools** (p.1-4): the third learning strand is reflection on the application of AI; the eight questions, especially groups 2 and 3, ask pupils to put that reflection into their own words.
- **Supplement II: Guide to Using AI in Teaching in Primary and Secondary Schools** (pp.2-2 to 2-5): starting from a teaching need, and the teacher's professional gatekeeping; what comes out of this survey is what a teacher adjusts an arrangement with, not a judgement on a teacher.
- **EDB Circular No. 156/2026** (Appendix 1, section 4): attending to pupils' physical and mental well-being and cultivating habits of appropriate use of digital tools; group 2 (what goes wrong) and group 3 (what the school should change first) are written to it.
- **EDB Circular No. 107/2026** (paragraph 4): bringing digital education into the school development plan and/or the annual school plan, and reviewing it through a self-evaluation cycle of planning, implementation and evaluation; the four points of this cycle are one piece of pupil-voice evidence for it.
